*The Arabian Nights*, or *The Thousand and One Nights* (Arabic *Alf Layla wa-Layla*), is a collection of Middle Eastern folk tales compiled in Arabic over many centuries by many hands, with no single author. Its stories are set inside a frame tale in which Scheherazade tells them, night after night, to a king who means to put her to death. Tales such as those of Sindbad, Aladdin and Ali Baba have made it one of the best-known story collections in the world.

## History

The earliest mentions of the *Nights* describe it as an Arabic translation of a Persian book, *Hezār Afsān* ("A Thousand Tales"). In the tenth century al-Mas'udi, in *The Meadows of Gold*, referred to the *Hezār Afsān* and said that its Arabic version was generally known as *Alf Layla*, "A Thousand Nights," and that it included Shirazad (Scheherazade) and Dinazad. In the same century Ibn al-Nadim, in the *Fihrist*, a catalogue of books compiled in Baghdad, described the Persian book's frame story: a king kills a succession of wives after their wedding night, until one saves herself by telling him a story every evening and leaving it unfinished until the next. He judged it "truly a coarse book, without warmth in the telling." No copy of the *Hezār Afsān* survives; it in turn drew partly on Indian sources, and some animal stories in the *Nights* reflect Sanskrit fables.

The Arabic collection grew in layers. A fragment of a few lines headed "The Book of the Tale of a Thousand Nights," dating from the ninth century, was found by the scholar Nabia Abbott and is the earliest surviving piece of the work. A twelfth-century document from Cairo, recording a Jewish bookseller's loan of a copy, is the first known use of the full title, *The Thousand and One Nights*. Over time cycles of Arab stories were added, among them tales of the caliph Harun al-Rashid and his Baghdad, and later stories from late medieval Cairo. Two main manuscript traditions survive, a Syrian and an Egyptian. The oldest substantial manuscript, of the Syrian branch, is now in the Bibliothèque nationale de France in Paris and holds 281 nights. The Egyptian manuscripts are later and much fuller, and copyists went on adding tales into the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

Europe met the *Nights* through the French orientalist Antoine Galland. In 1701 he published a French translation of a separate manuscript of the voyages of Sindbad, and from 1704 he issued *Les Mille et une nuit, contes arabes traduits en français*, which grew to twelve volumes, the last two published after his death. Galland worked mainly from the Syrian manuscript, but he also took in material from elsewhere. In 1709 he met Hanna Diyab, a Maronite Christian from Aleppo then in Paris, who told him fourteen stories, and Galland used seven of them. These so-called "orphan tales," among them "Aladdin" and "Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves," have no known Arabic text earlier than Galland's book. He also left out the poetry and cut erotic passages to suit French taste.

Galland's version was a great success and was soon translated across Europe. An anonymous English rendering of it, known as the "Grub Street" version, appeared from 1706 under the title *Arabian Nights' Entertainments*, the first known use of the common English title. Arabic texts were printed in the nineteenth century, at Calcutta, Breslau and Bulaq, and English translators turned to the fuller Egyptian tradition. Edward William Lane's translation left out what he thought immoral and added notes on Arab customs. John Payne's unexpurgated translation came out from 1882, and Richard Francis Burton's *The Book of the Thousand Nights and a Night*, in ten volumes from 1885 with six further volumes of *Supplemental Nights*, was printed privately for subscribers.

In 1898 Andrew Lang, the editor of the coloured Fairy Books, published *The Arabian Nights Entertainments*, a selection for young readers with pictures by H. J. Ford. Its stories were translated from Galland's French, shortened in places and without the verses.

## Tales

The frame story concerns King Shahryar, who discovers his wife's unfaithfulness, has her killed, and resolves to marry a new bride every day and execute her the next morning. Scheherazade, the vizier's daughter, offers herself as the next bride. On the wedding night she begins a tale but does not finish it, and the king, wishing to hear the end, postpones her execution; the next night she finishes it and begins another. This goes on for a thousand and one nights. The versions differ on how it ends, but they agree that the king pardons her.

Inside the frame, stories open within stories. A character in one of Scheherazade's tales begins a tale of his own, and that may hold another, so that in "The Fisherman and the Jinni" or the Sindbad cycle the narration passes through several voices. The tales include love stories, tragedies, comedies, crime stories, burlesques, fables and erotic tales, and many feature jinn, ghouls, sorcerers and enchanted cities alongside real places and historical figures such as Harun al-Rashid, his vizier Ja'far and the poet Abu Nuwas. Most are in prose, with verse used for songs, riddles and moments of strong feeling.

The influence of the *Nights* on European literature followed Galland. Voltaire's *Zadig* and William Beckford's *Vathek* belong to the fashion for Oriental tales it began. Coleridge and De Quincey wrote of the nightmares the book gave them as children, Wordsworth and Tennyson recalled reading it, and Charles Dickens and Edgar Allan Poe drew on it. Genies, magic lamps and flying carpets passed from it into modern fantasy, and Aladdin, Sindbad and Ali Baba became figures of popular culture.

## In this library

- [The Seven Voyages of Sindbad the Sailor](sindbad) — A poor porter of Baghdad who complains of his lot is invited in by a rich merchant who shares his name, Sindbad, and who tells him how he won his fortune in seven voyages, among them landing on a whale and escaping a valley of diamonds with the help of a roc. The Sindbad cycle had an independent existence, is absent from the early Arabic manuscripts of the *Nights*, and entered Galland's third volume from his separate translation of a Sindbad manuscript.
- [Camaralzaman and Badoura](camaralzaman) — Prince Camaralzaman, who will not marry, and Princess Badoura of China, who will not marry either, are brought together for one night by a fairy and a genie disputing which of the two is more beautiful, and each then searches for the other. The story belongs to the Arabic *Nights*, where it is the tale of Kamar al-Zaman, and it was among the stories in Galland's French version.
- [The Enchanted Horse](enchanted-horse) — At the Persian New Year an Indian inventor brings the king a mechanical horse that flies wherever its rider wishes, and the prince who mounts it is carried off to Bengal, where he meets a princess. Hanna Diyab told this tale to Galland in May 1709 and it appeared in his eleventh volume; under the title "The Ebony Horse" it is also found in Burton's translation from the Arabic.
