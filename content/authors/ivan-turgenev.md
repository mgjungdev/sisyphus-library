Ivan Turgenev (1818–1883) was a Russian novelist, short story writer, poet, playwright and translator, and a leading interpreter of Russian literature to Western Europe. His *A Sportsman's Sketches* was a landmark of Russian realism, and his novel *Fathers and Sons*, with its nihilist hero Bazarov, is counted among the major works of nineteenth-century fiction.

## Life

Ivan Sergeyevich Turgenev was born in 1818 in Oryol, into a noble family. His father, Sergei Nikolaevich Turgenev, was a cavalry colonel who had fought in the war of 1812; his mother, Varvara Petrovna Lutovinova, had inherited a great fortune and many serfs. Turgenev and his brothers were brought up by their mother, an educated and domineering woman, on the family estate of Spasskoye-Lutovinovo. They were taught by foreign tutors, the household spoke French even in its prayers, and Turgenev became fluent in French, German and English. His first acquaintance with Russian poetry came from a serf who read him verses from Kheraskov's *Rossiad*. He often witnessed his mother's harshness to her serfs, and the experience left him a lifelong opponent of serfdom.

In 1827 the family moved to Moscow for the children's education. Turgenev studied for a year at the University of Moscow and then at the University of Saint Petersburg, and his father died while he was a student. He went on to the University of Berlin, where he studied philosophy, especially Hegel, and history, and he came home convinced that Russia should learn from the ideas of the European Enlightenment. He worked for a short time in the civil service, in the Ministry of the Interior. His early poems and sketches were praised by the critic Vissarion Belinsky, for whom he kept a lasting regard.

Turgenev first made his name with the stories of peasant life later collected as *A Sportsman's Sketches* (1852), drawn from his hunting trips around Spasskoye. In 1852 he wrote an obituary of Nikolai Gogol that the Saint Petersburg censor refused to pass; when it appeared in a Moscow newspaper, Turgenev was held responsible, imprisoned for a month and then confined to his country estate for nearly two years. There he wrote the story "Mumu," about a deaf and mute serf forced to drown his dog. His novels followed in quick succession: *Rudin* (1856), *A Nest of the Gentry* (1859), *On the Eve* (1860) and *Fathers and Sons* (1862). The hostile reception of *Fathers and Sons* led him to leave Russia, and he lived for the rest of his life mostly in Baden-Baden and Paris.

The great attachment of his life was to the opera singer Pauline Viardot, near whose family he lived for many years and for whom he wrote opera libretti. He never married; a liaison with a serf on the family estate gave him a daughter, Paulinette. His relations with Tolstoy and Dostoyevsky were often strained. In 1861 a quarrel with Tolstoy ended in a challenge to a duel, and the two did not speak for seventeen years, until Tolstoy wrote in 1878 asking for his forgiveness. Dostoyevsky, with whom he quarrelled at Baden-Baden after the publication of *Smoke* (1867), caricatured him as the vain novelist Karmazinov in *The Devils* (1872). His last long novel, *Virgin Soil*, appeared in 1877.

In his later years Turgenev was a well-known figure in European letters. He was a close friend of Gustave Flaubert, served beside Victor Hugo as a vice-chairman of the international literary congress in Paris in 1878, and in 1879 received the honorary degree of Doctor of Civil Law from the University of Oxford. He died of complications of cancer in 1883 at his house at Bougival, near Paris. On his deathbed he urged Tolstoy, "My friend, return to literature!" His body was taken back to Russia and buried in the Volkovo Cemetery in Saint Petersburg.

## Writing

Turgenev moved early from Romantic idealism to a realism of close observation, encouraged by Belinsky. *A Sportsman's Sketches* showed the lives of serfs with a sympathy that is credited with turning public opinion toward the emancipation of 1861, and Turgenev himself thought it his most important book. His novels portray the educated Russians of his generation, and especially the "superfluous man" of talent and good intentions who can find no use for them, a type named in his story "The Diary of a Superfluous Man." In his lecture "Hamlet and Don Quixote" he described humanity as divided between self-absorbed doubt and idealistic generosity, a contrast that runs through his fiction. In *Fathers and Sons* he drew the classic portrait of the nihilist and helped make the word "nihilism" widely current.

Unlike Tolstoy and Dostoyevsky, he kept religious and moral teaching out of his art, and his prose is valued for its clarity, its landscapes and its portraits of women. Henry James, who wrote five essays on him, held that "his merit of form is of the first order," and Joseph Conrad also preferred him to his great contemporaries. Vladimir Nabokov admired his "plastic musical flowing prose" but called him "not a great writer, though a pleasant one." His complete novels were translated into English by Constance Garnett.
