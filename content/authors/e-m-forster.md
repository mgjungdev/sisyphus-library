E. M. Forster (1879–1970) was an English novelist, short-story writer and essayist. His novels, among them *A Room with a View*, *Howards End* and *A Passage to India*, examine class, hypocrisy and the struggle for personal connection across social and national divides. His story "The Machine Stops" (1909) is often regarded as the beginning of technological dystopian fiction.

## Life

Edward Morgan Forster was born on 1 January 1879 at 6 Melcombe Place, Dorset Square, in London, the only child of Edward Morgan Llewellyn Forster, an architect, and Alice Clara "Lily" Whichelo. He was registered as Henry Morgan Forster but baptised, by accident, Edward Morgan. His father died of tuberculosis on 30 October 1880, before the boy's second birthday, and he was brought up by his mother with help from his father's sisters. From 1883 to 1893 mother and son lived at Rooks Nest, a house near Stevenage in Hertfordshire that he remembered fondly and later used as the model for Howards End. A legacy of £8,000 in trust from his great-aunt Marianne Thornton, daughter of the abolitionist Henry Thornton, gave him enough to live on and made a writing life possible. He was unhappy as a day boy at Tonbridge School in Kent.

From 1897 to 1901 he was at King's College, Cambridge, where he read classics and history, met Lytton Strachey and Leonard Woolf, and was elected to the Apostles, a secret discussion society; many of its members later formed the Bloomsbury Group, to which Forster belonged in the 1910s and 1920s. In 1903 he travelled in Greece and Italy, and in the summer of 1905 he worked as a tutor to the children of the novelist Elizabeth von Arnim at Nassenheide in Pomerania. His first novel, *Where Angels Fear to Tread*, appeared in 1905, followed by *The Longest Journey* (1907), *A Room with a View* (1908) and *Howards End* (1910). All six of his novels were completed at Weybridge in Surrey.

In 1912 and 1913 he spent six months in India with R. C. Trevelyan and Goldsworthy Lowes Dickinson, staying with Syed Ross Masood, a close friend he had met in England in 1906, and on his return he began *A Passage to India*. A visit in 1913 to the socialist writer Edward Carpenter and his partner George Merrill inspired *Maurice*, a novel of homosexual love that Forster finished in 1914 but kept unpublished. During the First World War he was a conscientious objector and served with the British Red Cross in Alexandria as a searcher for missing servicemen. There he became a friend of the Greek poet C. P. Cavafy and fell in love with a tram conductor, Mohammad el Adl. In the early 1920s he returned to India as private secretary to the Maharajah of Dewas, an experience later recorded in *The Hill of Devi*. *A Passage to India*, published in 1924, won the James Tait Black Memorial Prize. It was the last novel he published in his lifetime. His critical book *Aspects of the Novel* followed in 1927.

From 1925 until his mother's death in 1945 Forster lived with her at West Hackhurst, a house in Abinger Hammer, Surrey. In 1930 he began a forty-year relationship with Bob Buckingham, a married policeman, and became close to his wife May and godfather to their son. Forster was open about his homosexuality with friends but not in public. In the 1930s and 1940s he became a well-known broadcaster on BBC radio, and he campaigned for individual liberty and against censorship, serving as president of the National Council for Civil Liberties. His humanist beliefs were set out in the essay "What I Believe" (1938). In January 1946 King's College made him an honorary fellow, and he lived mostly in the college from then on. He co-wrote the libretto for Benjamin Britten's opera *Billy Budd* (1951). He declined a knighthood in 1949, but became a Companion of Honour in 1953 and received the Order of Merit on his ninetieth birthday in 1969. In 1960 he gave evidence for the defence in the obscenity trial over D. H. Lawrence's *Lady Chatterley's Lover*. After a series of strokes he was cared for by the Buckinghams at their home in Coventry, where he died on 7 June 1970, aged 91. *Maurice* was published in 1971.

## Writing

Forster's fiction centres on the English middle classes and on what keeps people apart: class, convention, nationality and sexual taboo. *Where Angels Fear to Tread* and *A Room with a View* send narrow-minded English tourists, Baedekers in hand, into Italy; *Howards End* sets the cultured Schlegels against the commercial Wilcoxes and the struggling clerk Leonard Bast, under the epigraph "Only connect"; *A Passage to India* carries the same concern into relations between British and Indians under the Raj. His humanism is at the heart of his work, and he relied on symbols such as the wych elm at Howards End; his friend Roger Fry criticised his attachment to mysticism.

Reviewers greeted his first novel as "astonishing" and "brilliantly original," and the *Manchester Guardian* called *Howards End* "a novel of high quality written with what appears to be a feminine brilliance of perception." Lionel Trilling's study of 1943 called him "the only living novelist who can be read again and again," and David Cecil wrote that "He tells a story as well as anyone who ever lived." Some critics have objected to the unlikely pairings of his lovers and to the lack of realistic sexual attraction between them. Early reviewers of *A Passage to India* questioned details drawn from his time in India and were uneasy at the friendship between Aziz and Fielding, while later postcolonial critics have read it as a European's Orientalist fiction. "The Machine Stops," which Forster wrote as a reply to H. G. Wells's *A Modern Utopia*, has been read as a forecast of instant messaging and the Internet. M. Keith Booker ranks it with *We* and *Brave New World* among "the great defining texts of the genre of dystopian fiction."

## In this library

- [A Room with a View](room-with-a-view) — Lucy Honeychurch, touring Florence with her fussy cousin Charlotte Bartlett, is kissed by the free-thinking George Emerson, and back in Surrey must choose between him and the snobbish aesthete Cecil Vyse. Published in 1908, it grew out of a "Lucy novel" that Forster was already writing in 1902.
- [The Machine Stops](machine-stops) — In a future where people live alone in underground cells, their every need supplied by a worldwide Machine that they come to worship, Kuno tries to persuade his mother Vashti that life is possible on the surface. First published in *The Oxford and Cambridge Review* in November 1909 and collected in *The Eternal Moment and Other Stories* (1928).
- [Howards End](howards-end) — The idealistic Schlegel sisters, Margaret and Helen, become entangled with the wealthy Wilcox family and the poor clerk Leonard Bast, and with the fate of the Wilcoxes' country house, Howards End. Conceived in 1908 and published in 1910.
- [A Passage to India](passage-to-india) — In the Indian city of Chandrapore, Dr. Aziz befriends the newly arrived Mrs. Moore and Adela Quested and takes them to the Marabar Caves, where Adela's belief that she has been attacked leads to his arrest and trial. Published in 1924, it won that year's James Tait Black Memorial Prize for fiction.
