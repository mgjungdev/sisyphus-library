Edith Wharton (1862–1937) was an American novelist, short-story writer and designer who drew on her inside knowledge of upper-class New York to portray the lives and morals of the Gilded Age. Her best-known works are *The House of Mirth* (1905), the novella *Ethan Frome* (1911) and *The Age of Innocence* (1920), which in 1921 made her the first woman to win the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction.

## Life

Edith Newbold Jones was born on January 24, 1862, at her parents' brownstone at 14 West 23rd Street in Manhattan, to George Frederic Jones and Lucretia Stevens Rhinelander; she had two elder brothers. Her father's family was wealthy and socially prominent, with its money in real estate. From 1866 to 1872 the Joneses travelled in France, Italy, Germany and Spain; she became fluent in French, German and Italian, and at nine she nearly died of typhoid fever at a spa in the Black Forest. Back in the United States the family spent winters in New York and summers in Newport, Rhode Island. She was taught by tutors and governesses and read widely in her father's library, though her mother forbade her to read novels until she was married.

She made up stories from early childhood. At fifteen she secretly wrote a novella, *Fast and Loose* (1877), and in 1878 her father had a collection of her poems, *Verses*, privately printed. Five of her poems appeared anonymously in the *Atlantic Monthly* in 1880. Her family did not think writing a proper occupation for a society woman, and through the 1880s she took part instead in the social rituals of New York's upper class, observing changes she later used in her fiction. Her father died at Cannes in 1882, and that year an engagement to Henry Leyden Stevens was broken off. On April 29, 1885, she married Edward Robbins Wharton, known as Teddy, who was twelve years older and came from an established Boston family. The couple lived at Newport and for years spent part of every year abroad, mostly in Italy.

She returned to print with the poem "The Last Giustiniani" in *Scribner's Magazine* in October 1889, and her first published story, "Mrs. Manstey's View," appeared in the same magazine in 1891. With the designer Ogden Codman she wrote *The Decoration of Houses* (1897), her first major published work. Her first long novel, *The Valley of Decision*, came out in 1902. That year she designed The Mount, her house at Lenox, Massachusetts, where she wrote *The House of Mirth* and gathered material for *Ethan Frome*, and where her close friend Henry James was among her guests. Teddy Wharton suffered for years from depression, and in 1908 his condition was judged incurable; in that year she began an affair with Morton Fullerton, a correspondent for *The Times* of London. The Mount was her main home until 1911. She then settled in Paris, at 53 Rue de Varenne, and in 1913 she divorced her husband after 28 years of marriage.

When the First World War began she stayed in Paris and worked for the French war effort. In August 1914 she opened a workroom for unemployed women, she helped set up the American Hostels for Refugees, and in 1915 she organized the Children of Flanders Rescue Committee. With her friend Walter Berry she made five journeys to the front lines in 1915, which she described in articles for *Scribner's Magazine*, collected as *Fighting France*. She edited *The Book of the Homeless* (1915) for the benefit of refugees, with contributions from Henry James, Joseph Conrad and others, and on April 18, 1916, she was made a Chevalier of the Legion of Honour.

After the war she bought Pavillon Colombe, an eighteenth-century house at Saint-Brice-sous-Forêt north of Paris, and spent the winters and springs at Hyères on the Riviera. She returned to the United States only once, in 1923, to receive an honorary doctorate from Yale University. She was nominated for the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1927, 1928 and 1930, and her autobiography, *A Backward Glance*, was published in 1934. On June 1, 1937, she suffered a heart attack, and she died of a stroke at Pavillon Colombe on August 11, 1937. She was buried in the Cimetière des Gonards at Versailles.

## Writing

Wharton did not publish a novel until she was forty, but she went on to write fifteen novels, seven novellas and eighty-five short stories, as well as poetry, books on design and travel, criticism and a memoir. Her best-known fiction is set in the upper-class New York in which she grew up, a society she became one of the sharpest critics of, often through dramatic irony. Her recurring subjects have been summed up as "social and individual fulfillment, repressed sexuality, and the manners of old families and the new elite," and houses carry much of the meaning in her work; the critic Maureen Howard wrote that they "are never mere settings." *Ethan Frome* turns from New York to the austere rural New England she came to know at Lenox. Versions of her mother recur in her fiction, which her biographer Hermione Lee called "one of the most lethal acts of revenge ever taken by a writing daughter."

*The House of Mirth* made her reputation. Charles Scribner told her it was showing "the most rapid sale of any book ever published by Scribner," and *The New York Times* called it "a novel of remarkable power." Paul Bourget, who translated it into French in 1908, called it the greatest American novel. The *Times* found *Ethan Frome* "a compelling and haunting story," though Lionel Trilling later wrote that its ending was "terrible to contemplate." For the 1921 Pulitzer Prize the fiction judges chose Sinclair Lewis's *Main Street*, but Columbia University's advisory board, led by Nicholas Murray Butler, overturned them and gave the award to *The Age of Innocence*. Critics have long admired her craftsmanship, her attention to structure and her subtle ironies.

## In this library

- [The House of Mirth](house-of-mirth) — Lily Bart, a beautiful, well-born but penniless young woman in New York society of the 1890s, fails to make a wealthy marriage and slides over two years to the margins of that world. It was serialized in *Scribner's Magazine* from January 1905 and published as a book on October 14, 1905.
- [Ethan Frome](ethan-frome) — A visitor spending a winter in the town of Starkfield, Massachusetts, learns the story of Ethan Frome, a poor farmer married to the sickly Zeena, who fell in love with her cousin Mattie. It was published in New York by Charles Scribner's Sons in 1911.
- [The Age of Innocence](age-of-innocence) — In the New York of the 1870s Newland Archer, engaged to the conventional May Welland, is drawn to her cousin Ellen Olenska, who has fled an unhappy marriage to a Polish count. It was serialized in four parts in *Pictorial Review* in 1920 and published as a book by D. Appleton & Company on October 25, 1920.
