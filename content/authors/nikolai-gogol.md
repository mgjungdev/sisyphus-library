Nikolai Gogol (1809–1852) was a Russian novelist, short-story writer and dramatist of Ukrainian origin. He is known for the comedy *The Government Inspector*, the novel *Dead Souls* and the St Petersburg stories "The Nose" and "The Overcoat," which mix comedy, the grotesque and pathos and marked the turn of Russian fiction from Romanticism to realism.

## Life

Gogol was born in 1809 in Sorochyntsi, a Cossack town in the Poltava Governorate of the Russian Empire, in what is now Ukraine. His father, Vasily Gogol-Yanovsky, was said to descend from Ukrainian Cossacks and owned the village of Vasylivka; his mother descended from Leonty Kosyarovsky, an officer of the Lubny Regiment. The family spoke Ukrainian, Russian and Polish. From 1820 to 1828 he attended the gymnasium at Nizhyn, where he edited a manuscript periodical and wrote plays.

Hoping for literary fame, he moved to St Petersburg. There he published a Romantic poem, *Hans Küchelgarten*, under the pseudonym "V. Alov"; it was ridiculed, and he destroyed the copies. By 1830 he had shortened his surname from Gogol-Yanovsky to Gogol. He took a clerical post in the civil service, which he soon left, and taught at the Patriotic Institute. In 1831 he published *Evenings on a Farm Near Dikanka*, tales of Ukrainian village and Cossack life presented under the pen name "Rudy Panko," and it was an immediate success.

In 1834 he became professor of medieval history at the University of St Petersburg, a post for which he had no qualifications, and he resigned in 1835. In that year he published two collections, *Mirgorod* and *Arabesques*, the latter a mixture of essays and stories. His comedy *The Government Inspector*, a satire on provincial officials, was staged in 1836 only through the intervention of Emperor Nicholas I. Although his satire was aimed at Russian officialdom, Gogol himself held Slavophile views and supported the autocracy.

From 1836 to 1848 Gogol lived mostly abroad, above all in Rome, which he came to adore. There he worked on *Dead Souls*, the story of an adventurer who travels through the provinces buying up the names of dead serfs; the first part appeared in Moscow in 1842 and established his reputation as one of the greatest prose writers in the language. In the same year the third volume of his collected works, which included "The Overcoat," was printed.

In his later years Gogol became increasingly absorbed in religion. In 1848 he made a pilgrimage to Jerusalem, and afterwards he lived in Russia, where he came under the influence of the starets, or spiritual elder, Matvey Konstantinovsky. He suffered from deep depression and practised severe asceticism. In February 1852 he burned a number of his manuscripts, which contained most of the second part of *Dead Souls*, and he died in Moscow nine days later, at the age of forty-two. He was buried at the Danilov Monastery, and in 1931 his remains were moved to the Novodevichy Cemetery.

## Writing

Gogol began as a Romantic storyteller and moved toward a realistic picture of Russian life, though his realism is shot through with fantasy and the grotesque. The 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* praised the early Ukrainian tales for their "light and colour, its freshness and originality," and saw his strength in the humorous depiction of provincial society and its types. Later critics stressed his strangeness: Viktor Shklovsky took him as an example of "defamiliarization," the presenting of common things in an unfamiliar way. In "The Overcoat" Gogol blends mockery of his hero with pity for him, shifting abruptly from comedy to tragedy. According to the memoirs of P. V. Annenkov, the story grew from an office anecdote Gogol heard in 1834 about a poor official who had long saved for a gun and then lost it; while the others laughed, Gogol listened in silence. In his last years, the 1911 *Britannica* judged, a mystical preoccupation weakened his writing.

"The Overcoat" drew few reviews and was not reprinted in his lifetime, but his stories of poor St Petersburg clerks shaped the "natural school" of the 1840s and its image of the downtrodden "little man." The French critic Eugène-Melchior de Vogüé wrote, "We all came out from under Gogol's Overcoat," a saying often attributed to Dostoevsky, and Vladimir Nabokov called "The Overcoat" "the greatest Russian short story ever written."

## In this library

- [The Overcoat](overcoat) — Akaky Akakievich Bashmachkin, a poor copying clerk in a St Petersburg government office, scrimps to buy a new overcoat, is robbed of it, and dies after a general rebuffs his plea for help, after which his ghost haunts the city stealing overcoats. Its Russian title is *Шинель*; it first appeared in the third volume of Gogol's collected works, printed in 1842 and placed on sale early in 1843.
