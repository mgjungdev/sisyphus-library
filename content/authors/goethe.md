Johann Wolfgang von Goethe (1749–1832) was a German poet, dramatist, novelist and scientist, the leading figure of German literature in his age. His short novel *The Sorrows of Young Werther* (1774) made him famous across Europe at twenty-four, and his verse drama *Faust* occupied him for most of his long working life in Weimar.

## Life

Goethe was born on 28 August 1749 in Frankfurt am Main, the son of Johann Caspar Goethe, a lawyer, and Catharina Elisabeth Textor. Of the couple's children only he and his sister Cornelia survived to adulthood. In October 1765 he left Frankfurt to study law at the University of Leipzig, where he attended the lessons of the poet Christian Fürchtegott Gellert and spent more time on poetry and love affairs than on law. Illness sent him home in 1768.

In April 1770 he went to Strasbourg to finish his legal studies. There he met Johann Gottfried Herder, who turned his attention to Shakespeare and to folk poetry, and he fell in love with Friederike Brion, a pastor's daughter at Sessenheim; the lyrics he wrote for her opened a new period in German poetry. He took his licentiate in law in 1771 and began practising in Frankfurt, though without much commitment. His historical drama *Götz von Berlichingen* (1773) was drawn from the biography of a German nobleman.

In the summer of 1772 Goethe worked at Wetzlar, the seat of the imperial court, and fell in love with Charlotte Buff, who was engaged to Johann Christian Kestner. That autumn a young acquaintance, Karl Wilhelm Jerusalem, shot himself. Goethe joined the two experiences in *Die Leiden des jungen Werthers*, published in 1774. The book was read across Europe and its hero imitated, and Goethe found himself, at twenty-four, the best-known German writer of his day.

In 1775 he accepted an invitation from Karl August, Duke of Saxe-Weimar-Eisenach, and arrived in Weimar on 7 November; he lived there for the rest of his life. He became a minister of the small state, taking charge of the war commission, mines and roads, and later of its finances, and he was ennobled, adding "von" to his name. From 1776 he had a close attachment, lasting about ten years, to Charlotte von Stein, a married woman of the court. Official work slowed his writing, and he turned increasingly to natural science, including anatomy and botany.

In September 1786 he left Karlsbad secretly for Italy, reaching Rome on 29 October and staying, with a journey to Sicily, until 1788. The encounter with classical art and architecture changed his idea of form. After his return he lived with Christiane Vulpius, who bore him a son, August, on 25 December 1789; they married in 1806, in the days after Napoleon's army occupied Weimar. He published *Egmont* (1788), *Torquato Tasso* (1790) and a first fragment of *Faust* (1790), and from 1791 directed the court theatre.

In June 1794 Friedrich Schiller invited him to contribute to his new journal *Die Horen*, and the friendship and working partnership that followed lasted until Schiller's death in 1805. In these years Goethe completed the novel *Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship* and the verse epic *Hermann and Dorothea* (1798). The first part of *Faust* appeared in 1808, followed by the novel *Elective Affinities* (1809) and his *Theory of Colours* (1810), which opposed Newton's account of light.

Christiane died in 1816. In later life Goethe wrote the autobiography *Poetry and Truth* (published 1811–1833), the account of his *Italian Journey* (1816–1817), and the poems of the *West-Eastern Divan* (1819). He finished the second part of *Faust* shortly before his death. He died in Weimar on 22 March 1832.

## Writing

Goethe wrote in nearly every form: lyric poetry, plays in prose and verse, novels, travel writing, autobiography and scientific essays. His early work, including *Götz von Berlichingen* and *Werther*, belongs to the years of the Sturm und Drang, and its poems and prose give a large place to nature and to strong feeling. After Italy and in partnership with Schiller he moved toward a classical ideal of balance and form, and the two are regarded as the leading figures of Weimar Classicism. In later life he distanced himself from *Werther*.

*Werther* is an epistolary novel: almost all of it consists of letters from Werther to his friend Wilhelm, so that the reader sees the world only through the feelings of a young man whose love for an engaged woman becomes more than he can bear. Goethe revised the book substantially for an edition of 1787.

Its reception was immediate and intense. No German author before Goethe had drawn the attention of all Europe in this way. Young men copied the clothes Werther wears in the novel, and fears that readers would also copy his suicide led to the book being banned in Leipzig in 1775 and in Denmark and Italy. The term "Werther effect" was later coined for imitative suicides. Napoleon carried the novel on his Egyptian campaign and said he had read it seven times. In English it was read as *The Sorrows of Werter*, and in Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* (1818) the creature finds it, together with *Paradise Lost* and Plutarch's *Lives*, and calls it "a never-ending source of speculation and astonishment."
