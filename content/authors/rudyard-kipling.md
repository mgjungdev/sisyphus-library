Rudyard Kipling (1865–1936) was an English short-story writer, novelist, poet and journalist, born in Bombay in British India, the country that supplied much of his material. He is known for his tales of Anglo-Indian life, the two *Jungle Books*, the novel *Kim* and poems such as "Gunga Din" and "If—". In 1907 he became the first English-language writer to receive the Nobel Prize in Literature.

## Life

Joseph Rudyard Kipling was born in Bombay on 30 December 1865, the son of John Lockwood Kipling, a sculptor and pottery designer who taught at the newly founded Sir J. J. School of Art, and Alice Kipling, born MacDonald. Two of his mother's sisters married the painters Edward Burne-Jones and Edward Poynter, and a third was the mother of his cousin Stanley Baldwin, later prime minister. As was the custom in British India, Kipling and his younger sister Alice, called Trix, were sent to England in 1871 and boarded with a couple at Lorne Lodge in Southsea until 1877. He remembered the place as "the House of Desolation"; his only relief was a month each Christmas with the Burne-Joneses in Fulham. In January 1878 he entered the United Services College at Westward Ho! in Devon, a school that prepared boys for the army and later provided the setting for *Stalky & Co.* (1899).

His parents could not afford Oxford, and his father, by then curator of the Lahore Museum, found him a post on the *Civil and Military Gazette* in Lahore. He sailed for India in September 1882 and worked for the *Gazette* and its larger sister paper, *The Pioneer* of Allahabad, until early 1889. *Departmental Ditties*, a book of light verse, came out in 1886, and his newspaper stories were gathered in *Plain Tales from the Hills*, his first prose collection. Six slim paper-covered volumes of tales followed in Wheeler's Indian Railway Library, among them *Soldiers Three* and *The Phantom 'Rickshaw*.

On 9 March 1889 Kipling left India for London, travelling by way of Rangoon, Singapore, Hong Kong and Japan to San Francisco, then across the United States, where he called on Mark Twain at Elmira, New York. He reached Liverpool in October 1889 and found himself famous. He published a novel, *The Light That Failed* (1891), suffered a breakdown, and wrote *The Naulahka* with the American agent Wolcott Balestier. After Balestier's sudden death from typhoid, Kipling married his sister, Caroline Starr Balestier, on 18 January 1892 at All Souls Church, Langham Place; Henry James gave away the bride. *Barrack-Room Ballads*, containing "Mandalay" and "Gunga Din," appeared the same year.

The couple settled near Brattleboro, Vermont, first in a rented cottage, where their daughter Josephine was born in December 1892, and then in a house they built and named Naulakha. There Kipling wrote the two *Jungle Books*, the novel *Captains Courageous* and the poems of *The Seven Seas*. A second daughter, Elsie, was born in 1896. A quarrel with his brother-in-law Beatty Balestier, which led to a court hearing, drove the family back to England in July 1896. Their son John was born in 1897, the year of "Recessional"; "The White Man's Burden" followed in 1899. From 1898 to 1908 the family wintered most years in South Africa, in a house on Cecil Rhodes's estate near Cape Town, and during the Boer War Kipling briefly worked on a newspaper for the British troops at Bloemfontein. In 1899, on a visit to the United States, Josephine died of pneumonia.

*Kim* appeared in 1901 and the *Just So Stories* in 1902, the year Kipling bought Bateman's, a seventeenth-century house at Burwash in Sussex that remained his home for the rest of his life. He received the Nobel Prize in 1907. *Puck of Pook's Hill* (1906) and *Rewards and Fairies* (1910), which contains "If—", followed. He declined a knighthood and the poet laureateship. At the start of the First World War he wrote propaganda at the government's request, and in September 1915 his son John, serving with the Irish Guards, was killed at the Battle of Loos at the age of eighteen. Afterwards Kipling worked for the Imperial War Graves Commission, choosing the inscription "Their Name Liveth For Evermore" and the phrase "Known unto God" for unidentified graves, and wrote a history of the Irish Guards (1923). He died at Middlesex Hospital in London on 18 January 1936 of a perforated duodenal ulcer, and his ashes were buried in Poets' Corner in Westminster Abbey, beside Charles Dickens and Thomas Hardy.

## Writing

Kipling's early stories made British India, its soldiers, officials, children and Indian inhabitants, the material of short, swift, densely detailed fiction. The 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* judged that "in method they owed something to Bret Harte; in matter and spirit they were absolutely original," while finding the style "to some extent disfigured by jerkiness and mannered tricks." J. M. Barrie called "The Man Who Would Be King" "the most audacious thing in fiction." Kipling's verse, much of it in soldiers' slang, won him a second and wider public with *Barrack-Room Ballads*, and the *Jungle Books* were counted by many critics as his most flawless work. In his late speculative stories, such as "With the Night Mail" (1905), he used a method of indirect exposition that later science fiction writers adopted.

He was among the most popular writers in Britain in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Henry James called him "the most complete man of genius, as distinct from fine intelligence, that I have ever known," and the Nobel citation praised his "power of observation, originality of imagination, virility of ideas and remarkable talent for narration." His open support for empire divided readers then and later. T. S. Eliot, who edited *A Choice of Kipling's Verse* (1941), called him "a writer impossible wholly to understand and quite impossible to belittle," while George Orwell, replying in 1942, called him "morally insensitive and aesthetically disgusting" as a "jingo imperialist" yet granted him a real grip on political reality. Randall Jarrell wrote that "very few have written more and better stories."

## In this library

- [The Phantom Rickshaw](phantom-rickshaw) — In Simla, the narrator, Jack, casts off his lover, Mrs. Wessington, and becomes engaged to Kitty Mannering, but after her death her rickshaw and its bearers return to him as phantoms carrying her ghost. First published in *Quartette*, the Christmas annual of the *Civil and Military Gazette*, for 1885, and revised for *The Phantom 'Rickshaw and Other Tales*.
- [The Man Who Would Be King](man-who-would-be-king) — Two vagabond adventurers, Daniel Dravot and Peachey Carnehan, set out from Lahore to make themselves kings of remote Kafiristan, and succeed until Dravot decides to take a wife. First published in *The Phantom 'Rickshaw and Other Tales* in 1888.
- [Rikki-Tikki-Tavi](rikki-tikki-tavi) — A young mongoose, nearly drowned in a seasonal storm, is taken in by an English family in India and defends them against the cobras Nag and Nagaina. First published in *St. Nicholas Magazine* in November 1893 and collected in *The Jungle Book* (1894).
- [Kaa's Hunting](kaas-hunting) — The man-cub Mowgli is carried off by the monkey-people to the ruined city of the Cold Lairs, and Baloo the bear and Bagheera the panther enlist the python Kaa to rescue him. First published in the magazine *Today* in 1894 and collected in *The Jungle Book* the same year.
- [Kim](kim) — Kim O'Hara, the orphaned son of an Irish soldier, who lives by begging and running errands on the streets of Lahore, becomes the disciple of a Tibetan lama and is drawn into the Great Game of espionage between Britain and Russia. Serialised in *McClure's Magazine* and *Cassell's Magazine* and published as a book by Macmillan in October 1901.
