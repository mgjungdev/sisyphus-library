Joseph Conrad (1857–1924) was a Polish-born British novelist and short story writer who did not speak English fluently until his twenties and is regarded as one of the greatest writers in the language. After nearly twenty years in the French and British merchant navies, he wrote novels and tales, many of them set at sea or in the colonial world, among them *Heart of Darkness*, *Lord Jim*, *Nostromo* and *The Secret Agent*.

## Life

Józef Teodor Konrad Korzeniowski was born on 3 December 1857 in Berdychiv, in Ukraine, then part of the Russian Empire, the only child of Apollo Korzeniowski, a writer, translator and political activist, and Ewa Bobrowska. The family belonged to the Polish landed nobility. In 1861 Apollo joined the resistance to Russian rule in Warsaw and was arrested, and in 1862 the family was exiled to Vologda, north of Moscow, and later to Chernihiv in Ukraine, where Ewa died of tuberculosis in 1865. Apollo taught his son at home, reading him the Polish Romantic poets, and the boy also read Shakespeare and Victor Hugo's *Toilers of the Sea*. Father and son moved to Austrian Poland, settling in Kraków in 1869, where Apollo died that May, leaving Conrad an orphan at eleven. He was placed in the care of his maternal uncle, Tadeusz Bobrowski.

In October 1874, at sixteen, Conrad went to Marseille to begin a career on French merchant ships, with a monthly allowance from his uncle. In debt and unable to obtain the papers he needed to keep serving on French ships, he attempted suicide in March 1878 by shooting himself in the chest. He recovered, and in April 1878 he joined the British merchant marine, probably having begun to learn English only shortly before. Over the next fifteen years he rose from crewman to mate and finally to captain; his only command was the barque *Otago*, which he sailed from Sydney to Mauritius in 1888 and 1889. He became a British subject in August 1886 and was released from Russian nationality in 1889.

In 1890 he was hired by a Belgian trading company and went to the Congo Free State, where he served on a river steamer and took command when its captain fell ill. He left Africa at the end of that year. He had begun his first novel, *Almayer's Folly*, in 1889, and on the clipper *Torrens* in 1893 he met John Galsworthy, who became a friend. In 1894 he gave up the sea. *Almayer's Folly*, set in Borneo, appeared in 1895 under the name Joseph Conrad, and *An Outcast of the Islands* followed in 1896. On 24 March 1896 he married Jessie George; they had two sons, Borys and John.

Almost all of Conrad's work was first published in magazines and newspapers before appearing as books. *The Nigger of the "Narcissus"* (1897) was followed by *Heart of Darkness*, serialized in *Blackwood's Magazine* in 1899, *Lord Jim* (1900), *Typhoon* (1903) and *Nostromo* (1904), and he wrote several books in collaboration with Ford Madox Ford. *The Secret Agent* (1907) and *Under Western Eyes* (1911) turned to political subjects. For years he lived on advances and loans, and suffered from gout, malaria and depression; a civil list pension of £100 a year, granted in 1910, eased his finances. Popular success came only with *Chance* in 1913. From 1910 to 1919 he lived at Capel House in Orlestone, Kent, where he wrote *Victory* (1915), *The Arrow of Gold* (1919) and *The Rescue* (1920).

In the summer of 1914 Conrad took his family to Poland, and the outbreak of the First World War left them in Zakopane until they could return to England in November. Apart from holidays in France and Italy, that journey, and a visit to the United States in 1923, he spent the rest of his life in England. He refused honorary degrees from several universities, including Cambridge and Yale, and in 1924 declined a knighthood offered by the prime minister, Ramsay MacDonald. Conrad died at his house, Oswalds, in Bishopsbourne, Kent, on 3 August 1924, probably of a heart attack, and was buried in Canterbury Cemetery under a misspelled form of his Polish name.

## Writing

Conrad wrote about crises of human individuality in a world he saw as indifferent and amoral, often among sailors, traders and colonial officials far from Europe. He drew on his own voyages and on public events: the Patna episode of *Lord Jim* recalls the abandonment of the pilgrim ship *Jeddah* by its officers in 1880, and *The Secret Agent* the death of the anarchist Martial Bourdin in 1894, apparently while trying to blow up the Greenwich Observatory. *Heart of Darkness* and earlier tales contain bitter reflections on colonialism. *Heart of Darkness* and *Lord Jim* are told largely by the seaman Marlow, and in *Lord Jim* events reach the reader from several viewpoints and often out of chronological order. In the preface to *The Nigger of the "Narcissus"* he wrote that his task was "by the power of the written word to make you hear, to make you feel ... before all, to make you see." He wrote in his third language, after Polish and French, and his English sometimes carries French and Polish turns of phrase.

His early Malay novels earned him a reputation as a teller of exotic tales, a reading that frustrated him for the rest of his career. After *Chance* he was discussed and praised more than any other English writer of the time, and his circle included Henry James, Stephen Crane, H. G. Wells and Galsworthy. H. L. Mencken was among the first American critics to champion him, and William Faulkner, F. Scott Fitzgerald and many later writers acknowledged his influence. In 1975 Chinua Achebe called *Heart of Darkness* "an offensive and deplorable book" that dehumanized Africans; other critics have answered that he fails to distinguish Marlow's views from Conrad's.

## In this library

- [Heart of Darkness](heart-of-darkness) — The sailor Charles Marlow tells of his journey up an African river as a steamer captain for a Belgian company to find Kurtz, an ivory trader at a station far in the interior. First published in three parts in *Blackwood's Magazine* in February, March and April 1899, and collected in *Youth: A Narrative, and Two Other Stories* (1902).
- [Lord Jim](lord-jim) — Jim, a young British mate, jumps from the pilgrim ship *Patna* when he believes it is sinking, is disgraced at the inquiry, and seeks redemption in the remote settlement of Patusan, as Marlow tells his story. Serialized in *Blackwood's Magazine* from October 1899 to November 1900 and published as a book in 1900.
- [The Secret Agent](secret-agent) — In London in 1886, Adolf Verloc, a shopkeeper in Soho who spies for a foreign embassy, is ordered to blow up the Greenwich Observatory, and the plot ends in the death of his wife's brother, Stevie. Serialized in the American magazine *Ridgway's* and published as a book on 12 September 1907, with a dedication to H. G. Wells.
