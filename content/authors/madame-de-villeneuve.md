Madame de Villeneuve (1695–1755), born Gabrielle-Suzanne Barbot, was a French novelist and writer of fairy tales. She is known above all as the author of "La Belle et la Bête," the earliest known version of the fairy tale "Beauty and the Beast," published in 1740.

## Life

Gabrielle-Suzanne Barbot was born in Paris into a powerful Protestant family of La Rochelle. Her father, Jean Barbot, seigneur de Romagné et des Mothais, was a councillor of the king at the court of La Rochelle, and her mother was Suzanne Allaire, also of a Protestant family of that city. Members of the Barbot family had served several times as mayors of La Rochelle; one ancestor, Amos Barbot, sat as a deputy in the Estates General of 1614, and a relative, Jean Barbot, was an early explorer of West Africa and the Caribbean who settled in England to escape the persecution of Protestants after Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685.

On 9 February 1706, in the church of Saint-Barthélemy at La Rochelle, she married Jean-Baptiste de Gaalon, seigneur de Villeneuve, an officer of infantry. Within months the marriage was in difficulty: on 8 November 1706 she obtained from the court of La Rochelle a separation of property, which restored her dowry, because her husband had lost much of their joint fortune at gambling and through bad management. A daughter, Marie Louise Suzanne, was born at La Rochelle on 13 February 1708. Her husband died at Pamplona on 14 June 1711, and she was left a widow without resources. Having lost her fortune, she had to find a way to support herself, and she turned to writing.

She eventually made her way back to Paris, where she came to know Prosper Jolyot de Crébillon, known as Crébillon père, the most celebrated writer of tragedies of the day, who had been named censor of one of her novels. The two agreed to share a house and a table, an arrangement that lasted until her death. They probably began living together in the early 1730s, although the earliest documented date is 1748. She helped Crébillon in his duties as royal literary censor, which gave her a close knowledge of the tastes of the Parisian reading public.

Her works include a novella, *Le Phénix conjugal* (1734); the collection of tales *La Jeune Américaine, et les contes marins* (1740), which contained "La Belle et la Bête"; and *Les Belles Solitaires* (1745). In the last years of her life she wrote several novels, among them *La Jardinière de Vincennes* (1753), which was considered her masterpiece and brought her greatest commercial success, and *Le Juge prévenu* (1754). Many of her books appeared anonymously or signed only "Mme de V***," which has made some attributions uncertain. She also published a reworking of a romance by Madeleine de Scudéry as *Anecdotes de la cour d'Alphonse onzième du nom, roi de Castille*.

Madame de Villeneuve died on 29 December 1755 in Paris, in the rue des Douze-Portes in the parish of Saint-Gervais, in the house where Crébillon had settled some years before. Some of her works appeared after her death, including the novel *Mémoires de Mesdemoiselles de Marsange* (1757).

## Writing

Madame de Villeneuve wrote under the influence of Madame d'Aulnoy, Charles Perrault and the précieuse writers of the seventeenth century, whose fairy tales were written for adult readers in the salons. *La Jeune Américaine, et les contes marins* is as long as a conventional novel and follows the manner of seventeenth-century fiction, with many subplots and stories set inside the main narrative, one of which is "La Belle et la Bête."

Her version of the tale is long and elaborate. Beauty is the youngest of a merchant's twelve children, and the Beast courts her each evening while she dreams of a handsome stranger. The tale goes on to tell the history behind the enchantment: the Beast is a prince cursed by a wicked fairy, and Beauty, raised as the merchant's daughter, is in fact the child of a king and a good fairy. The Beast is "bête" in both senses of the French word, a beast and a dullard.

The tale soon reached the stage in Pierre-Claude Nivelle de La Chaussée's play *Amour pour amour* (1742). After her death Jeanne-Marie Leprince de Beaumont abridged and rewrote it as a moral tale for young girls, giving no credit to Villeneuve. Beaumont's much shorter version became the best known, and she is still often wrongly named as the author of the story. In English, Andrew Lang retold Villeneuve's version in *The Blue Fairy Book* (1889), keeping her lavish descriptions of the Beast's palace.

## In this library

- [Beauty and the Beast](beauty-and-the-beast) — A ruined merchant picks a rose for his youngest daughter, Beauty, from the garden of an enchanted palace, and to save his life she goes to live with its master, the Beast, who asks her every night to marry him until her love breaks the spell. Villeneuve's tale was published anonymously in *La Jeune Américaine, et les contes marins* in 1740; the English text here is the abridged retelling in Andrew Lang's *The Blue Fairy Book* (1889).
