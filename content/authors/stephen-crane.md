Stephen Crane (1871–1900) was an American novelist, short-story writer, poet and journalist whose work belongs to the traditions of Realism, Naturalism and Impressionism. He won international fame with *The Red Badge of Courage*, a Civil War novel told from inside the mind of a frightened young soldier, and is also known for short stories such as "The Open Boat," "The Blue Hotel" and "The Bride Comes to Yellow Sky."

## Life

Stephen Crane was born on 1 November 1871 in Newark, New Jersey, the fourteenth and last child of Jonathan Townley Crane, a Methodist Episcopal minister, and Mary Helen Peck Crane, a clergyman's daughter active in the Woman's Christian Temperance Union. A sickly child, he taught himself to read before he was four. In 1876 the family moved to Port Jervis, New York, where his father became pastor of Drew Methodist Church; he died there in 1880, when Stephen was eight. The boy was raised largely by his sister Agnes and lived for a time with his brother Townley, a journalist, in Asbury Park, New Jersey. Agnes died of meningitis in 1884, and in 1886 his brother Luther was killed by a train while working as a railroad flagman.

Crane attended Pennington Seminary and then Claverack College, a quasi-military school, where he excelled at drill and baseball, and later called his time there "the happiest period of my life although I was not aware of it." From 1888 he spent his summers helping Townley at a news bureau on the New Jersey shore. In 1890 he entered Lafayette College to study mining engineering, but after one semester he moved to Syracuse University. There he attended few classes, played baseball and published a story in the *New York Tribune*, and in June 1891, calling college "a waste of time," he left to become a writer and reporter. That summer he met Hamlin Garland, who became his mentor. His mother died in December 1891.

Crane reported on the poor districts of New York, and especially on the Bowery, which became the setting of *Maggie: A Girl of the Streets*. When *The Century Magazine* rejected it, he had it printed privately with money inherited from his mother, under the pseudonym "Johnston Smith"; it did not sell. Reading the *Century*'s series on Civil War battles, he complained that the veterans never told "how they *felt* in those scraps," and he began a war novel. *The Red Badge of Courage* was serialised in abridged form in newspapers in December 1894 and published in full by D. Appleton & Company in October 1895. It became a best seller in the United States and Britain and made him famous at twenty-four. His first book of poems, *The Black Riders and Other Lines*, appeared the same year to largely hostile reviews.

In 1896 Crane's testimony on behalf of Dora Clark, a woman arrested for solicitation, led to a public scandal and an attack on his reputation in court. Late that year he went to Jacksonville, Florida, to sail to Cuba as a war correspondent, and there met Cora Taylor, the proprietor of a local hotel of assignation, who became his companion for the rest of his life. On 2 January 1897 his ship, the *Commodore*, sank, and he and three other men spent about thirty hours in a small dinghy before reaching shore at Daytona Beach; one of them drowned. He told the story in "The Open Boat." In 1897 he and Cora reported on the Greco-Turkish War for the *New York Journal*, she as the war's first woman correspondent. They then settled in England, first at Ravensbrook in Oxted, Surrey, where he met Joseph Conrad, who became a close friend.

Pressed for money, Crane wrote rapidly for English and American magazines, producing *The Monster*, "The Bride Comes to Yellow Sky" and "The Blue Hotel." In 1898 he covered the Spanish-American War in Cuba, going ashore with the Marines at Guantánamo Bay, before returning to England in January 1899. He and Cora moved to Brede Place, a fourteenth-century manor house in Sussex, where he worked hard despite worsening tuberculosis; Conrad, Henry James and H. G. Wells came to their Christmas party in 1899. After a series of hemorrhages he was taken to a sanatorium at Badenweiler in the Black Forest, Germany, where he died on 5 June 1900 at the age of 28. He was buried in Evergreen Cemetery in Hillside, New Jersey.

## Writing

Crane's fiction is usually described as Naturalist, Realist or Impressionist, or a mixture of the three. His prose is compressed and vivid, full of color imagery and irony, and he often withheld names from his characters, calling them "the youth" or "the correspondent." He used everyday speech and regional dialect, which early reviewers criticized. Fear, courage, isolation, spiritual crisis and the indifference of nature are his central themes, most of all in *The Red Badge of Courage*, which he called "a psychological portrayal of fear." He insisted that "a story should be logical in its action and faithful to character." His poems, which he preferred to call "lines," were written in free verse without rhyme, meter or titles.

*The Red Badge of Courage* drew what Wells called "an orgy of praise." Joseph Conrad wrote that it "detonated... with the impact and force of a twelve-inch shell charged with a very high explosive," and the novelist Harold Frederic called it "a book outside of all classification." The veteran and writer Ambrose Bierce was among its detractors. Crane's later novels were less successful, and after his death he was nearly forgotten for two decades before critics revived his reputation in the 1920s. Ernest Hemingway included the whole novel in his anthology *Men at War* (1942), calling it "one of the finest books of our literature," and Crane's work is thought to have influenced the Modernists and the Imagists.

## In this library

- [The Red Badge of Courage](red-badge-of-courage) — Henry Fleming, a young private in the Union army, runs from his first battle, wanders among the wounded and longs for a wound of his own, a "red badge of courage," before returning to fight as his regiment's flag-bearer. An abridged version was serialised in newspapers in December 1894, and D. Appleton & Company published the book in October 1895.
- [The Open Boat](open-boat) — A correspondent, an injured captain, a cook and an oiler, survivors of a sunken steamer, struggle through a night and a day in a small dinghy off the Florida coast, within sight of a shore they cannot safely reach. It appeared in *Scribner's Magazine* in June 1897 and gave its title to a collection published in New York and London in 1898.
- [The Bride Comes to Yellow Sky](bride-comes-to-yellow-sky) — Jack Potter, marshal of a small Texas town, brings his new bride home from San Antonio on the train, unaware that the drunken gunman Scratchy Wilson is out looking for a fight. It was written in England and first published in *McClure's Magazine* in February 1898.
