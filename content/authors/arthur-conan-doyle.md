Arthur Conan Doyle (1859–1930) was a British writer and physician, born in Edinburgh. He is best known for the four novels and fifty-six short stories about the detective Sherlock Holmes and his friend Dr. Watson, and for *The Lost World* (1912), the first of his stories of Professor Challenger.

## Life

Arthur Ignatius Conan Doyle was born on 22 May 1859 at 11 Picardy Place, Edinburgh, the son of Charles Altamont Doyle, an artist of Irish Catholic descent, and Mary Foley. His father's alcoholism broke up the household for a time, and he suffered years of psychiatric illness before his death in 1893. Supported by wealthy uncles, the boy was sent at nine to Jesuit schools in Lancashire, first Hodder Place and then Stonyhurst College, which he attended until 1875. He spent a further year at the Jesuit school Stella Matutina at Feldkirch in Austria. He was raised a Catholic but later abandoned the faith.

From 1876 to 1881 he studied medicine at the University of Edinburgh, and while a student he began to write. His first published story, "The Mystery of Sasassa Valley," appeared in *Chambers's Edinburgh Journal* in 1879. In 1880 he sailed as ship's doctor on the Greenland whaler *Hope*, and after graduating in 1881 he served as surgeon on a voyage to the West African coast. He took his doctorate of medicine in 1885. In 1882 he settled in Southsea, near Portsmouth, with less than ten pounds, and set up a medical practice that never prospered; while waiting for patients he returned to writing fiction. In 1885 he married Louisa Hawkins, the sister of one of his patients.

*A Study in Scarlet*, which introduced Sherlock Holmes and Dr. Watson, was sold to Ward, Lock and Co. for 25 pounds and appeared in *Beeton's Christmas Annual* for 1887. Doyle modelled Holmes partly on Joseph Bell, his former teacher at Edinburgh. A second Holmes novel, *The Sign of the Four*, was commissioned by *Lippincott's Monthly Magazine*. In 1891 he tried to train as an eye specialist in Vienna, gave it up, and opened a consulting room in London that drew no patients. The same year he began a series of Holmes stories in *The Strand Magazine*, starting with "A Scandal in Bohemia," and their popularity made him one of the best-paid authors of his time. Wishing to give more time to his historical novels, he had Holmes fall to his death with Professor Moriarty at the Reichenbach Falls in "The Final Problem" in December 1893. Public outcry brought Holmes back in *The Hound of the Baskervilles*, serialised from 1901, and Doyle went on writing Holmes stories until 1927.

During the Second Boer War he served at the Langman Field Hospital in South Africa in 1900, and he wrote *The Great Boer War* and a defence of Britain's conduct of the war. He was knighted in 1902. He stood for Parliament as a Liberal Unionist in Edinburgh in 1900 and in the Hawick Burghs in 1906 and was defeated both times. He took up the cases of George Edalji and Oscar Slater, two men he believed wrongly convicted, and the Edalji case contributed to the creation of the Court of Criminal Appeal in 1907. He campaigned against abuses in the Congo Free State and wrote *The Crime of the Congo* (1909).

Louisa, who had tuberculosis, died in 1906, and in 1907 he married Jean Leckie, whom he had loved since 1897. They settled at Crowborough in Sussex, where he lived for the rest of his life. He had two children with his first wife and three with his second. His interest in psychic phenomena dated from the 1880s, and from 1916, amid the deaths of the First World War, he presented himself publicly as a spiritualist. His son Kingsley died in 1918. Doyle lectured on spiritualism in Britain, Australia, New Zealand, Europe and the United States, and in *The Coming of the Fairies* (1922) he defended the Cottingley fairy photographs, later shown to be faked; his beliefs led to a public quarrel with Harry Houdini. He died of a heart attack at his home in Crowborough on 7 July 1930.

## Writing

Doyle wrote in many forms: detective stories, historical novels, adventure and science fiction, tales of the sea and the supernatural, plays, poetry, history and works on spiritualism. Holmes is the reasoning detective in the line of Poe's C. Auguste Dupin, whom Holmes mentions dismissively in *A Study in Scarlet*. Doyle credited Joseph Bell: "It is most certainly to you that I owe Sherlock Holmes." Robert Louis Stevenson, reading the stories, asked, "can this be my old friend Joe Bell?"

*A Study in Scarlet* attracted little public interest at first, though it was well reviewed in *The Scotsman* and the *Glasgow Herald*. The short stories in *The Strand*, beginning in 1891 and illustrated by Sidney Paget, made him immensely popular and found a host of imitators. Doyle himself was ambivalent about his detective. In 1891 he wrote to his mother, "He takes my mind from better things." He and many critics thought his seven historical novels, among them *The White Company* and *Sir Nigel*, his best work, and he also wrote popular stories of the Napoleonic soldier Brigadier Gerard. His story "J. Habakuk Jephson's Statement" (1884) popularised the mystery of the *Mary Celeste*, and its invented details came to dominate later accounts. With *The Lost World* he set out, as he told his editor, "to do for the boys' book what Sherlock Holmes did for the detective story." He also wrote for the theatre, adapting "The Adventure of the Speckled Band" as a play in 1910. Holmes has become the most often portrayed literary character in film and television.

## In this library

- [A Study in Scarlet](study-in-scarlet) — Dr. Watson, back from the Afghan war, takes rooms with Sherlock Holmes and follows him into a London murder case whose roots lie among the Mormon settlers of Salt Lake City. First published in *Beeton's Christmas Annual* for 1887 and as a book by Ward, Lock and Co. in 1888.
- [The Sign of the Four](sign-of-the-four) — Mary Morstan brings Holmes the mystery of her vanished father and the pearls sent to her each year, which lead to a stolen Indian treasure. Commissioned by *Lippincott's Monthly Magazine* at a dinner in London in 1889, it first appeared there as *The Sign of the Four; or The Problem of the Sholtos*.
- [A Scandal in Bohemia](scandal-in-bohemia) — The King of Bohemia engages Holmes to recover a compromising photograph from the opera singer Irene Adler, who outwits him. The first Holmes short story, published in *The Strand Magazine* in July 1891.
- [The Red-Headed League](red-headed-league) — The pawnbroker Jabez Wilson is paid to copy out the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* for a society of red-headed men until the society suddenly dissolves. It first appeared in *The Strand Magazine* in August 1891.
- [The Adventure of the Speckled Band](speckled-band) — Helen Stoner fears she will die as her twin sister did, in a locked bedroom at the house of their stepfather, Dr. Grimesby Roylott. First published in *The Strand Magazine* in February 1892; Doyle called it his best story.
- [The Adventure of the Blue Carbuncle](blue-carbuncle) — At Christmas, a stolen gem turns up in the crop of a goose, and Holmes traces the bird back to the thief. It was first published in *The Strand Magazine* in January 1892.
- [The Hound of the Baskervilles](hound-of-the-baskervilles) — Holmes and Watson investigate the legend of a spectral hound that is said to haunt the Baskerville family on Dartmoor. Serialised in *The Strand Magazine* from August 1901 to April 1902 and published as a book by George Newnes in 1902.
- [The Lost World](lost-world) — The journalist Edward Malone joins Professor Challenger's expedition to a remote plateau in the Amazon basin where dinosaurs and other prehistoric animals survive. Serialised in *The Strand Magazine* from April to November 1912 and published as a book by Hodder and Stoughton in October 1912.
