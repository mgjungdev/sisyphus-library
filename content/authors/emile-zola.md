Émile Zola (1840–1902) was a French novelist, critic and journalist, the leading figure of the literary school of naturalism. He is known for *Les Rougon-Macquart*, a cycle of twenty novels following one family through the Second Empire, among them *L'Assommoir*, *Nana* and *Germinal*, and for his open letter "J'accuse," written in defence of Captain Alfred Dreyfus.

## Life

Émile Édouard Charles Antoine Zola was born in Paris on 2 April 1840, the son of François Zola, an engineer of Italian and Greek descent who had been born in Venice, and Émilie Aubert, who was French. When he was three the family moved to Aix-en-Provence in the south-east, where his father worked on the dam that bears the family name. His father died in 1847, leaving his mother with little to live on, and in 1852 Zola became a boarder at the Collège Bourbon in Aix. In 1858 the family moved to Paris, where his childhood friend Paul Cézanne soon joined him. His mother had hoped he would study law, but he twice failed the baccalauréat examination.

After years of poverty he worked as a clerk in a shipping firm and then, from 1862, in the sales department of the publisher Hachette, while writing literary and art reviews for newspapers. In 1862 he was naturalised as a French citizen. His first book, the collection of tales *Contes à Ninon*, appeared in 1864. When his autobiographical novel *La Confession de Claude* (1865) attracted the attention of the police, Hachette dismissed him, and he turned to literature and journalism, including aggressive criticism of literature and art in the journal *L'Événement*. As a political journalist he made no secret of his dislike of the emperor Napoleon III. In 1865 he met Éléonore-Alexandrine Meley, a seamstress, and they married on 31 May 1870. His first major novel, *Thérèse Raquin*, was published in 1867.

By the age of twenty-eight Zola had planned the whole of *Les Rougon-Macquart*, a "natural and social history of a family under the Second Empire," and its first volume, *La Fortune des Rougon*, appeared in 1871. *L'Assommoir*, a novel of drink among the working people of Paris, made him wealthy and allowed him to negotiate better terms with his publisher, Georges Charpentier; *Nana* (1880) and *La Débâcle* (1892) sold even more. From 1880 he entertained Guy de Maupassant, Joris-Karl Huysmans and other writers at his country house at Médan, near Paris, and became the acknowledged leader of the naturalists. His friendship with Cézanne ended after *L'Œuvre* (1886), a novel about a painter. The publication of *La Terre* provoked "The Manifesto of the Five," in which five younger writers attacked him in *Le Figaro*. Though nominated several times, he was never elected to the Académie française.

In 1888 his wife hired a young seamstress, Jeanne Rozerot, with whom Zola had two children, Denise in 1889 and Jacques in 1891. The cycle ended with *Le Docteur Pascal* in 1893, and Zola went on to the trilogy *Lourdes*, *Rome* and *Paris*, and to novels on population (*Fécondité*) and work (*Travail*).

Captain Alfred Dreyfus, a Jewish army officer, had been convicted of treason in 1894 and sent to Devil's Island, and in January 1898 a court-martial acquitted Ferdinand Walsin Esterhazy, the officer whom new evidence had implicated. On 13 January 1898 Zola published "J'accuse...!", an open letter to President Félix Faure, on the front page of *L'Aurore*, accusing the army of a miscarriage of justice and antisemitism, and hoping to be prosecuted for libel so that the evidence would be made public. He was convicted in February 1898 and removed from the Legion of Honour. When a second trial opened in July, he fled to England on his lawyer's advice and lived there, in Surrey and then in Upper Norwood, until June 1899, when the original verdict against Dreyfus was annulled and he returned to France. He wrote of the affair, "The truth is on the march, and nothing shall stop it."

Zola died in his Paris home on 29 September 1902 of carbon monoxide poisoning from a chimney flue. He had just finished *Vérité*, a novel drawn from the Dreyfus affair. Dreyfus attended his funeral, and Anatole France gave the oration. In 1908 his remains were moved from the Montmartre Cemetery to the Panthéon.

## Writing

Zola called himself the leader of French naturalism. His fiction drew on the realism of Balzac and Flaubert and on contemporary ideas of heredity and environment from Claude Bernard, Hippolyte Taine and the physician Prosper Lucas. In the preface to *La Fortune des Rougon* he wrote that he would follow "the thread that leads mathematically from one man to another" through "the double question of temperaments and environments," tracing the respectable Rougons and the disreputable Macquarts over five generations, against violence, drink and prostitution in a France changed by industry and by Haussmann's rebuilding of Paris. He prepared each novel with careful documentation, and he wrote every day for about thirty years under the motto *Nulla dies sine linea*, "not a day without a line."

The 1911 *Britannica* observed that he "idealized the elements that are bestial," presenting human lust and greed "as in a vision, magnified, overwhelming, portentous," and that his "descriptions of crowds in movement have never been surpassed." His critics accused him of obscenity and exaggeration, but his methods influenced the writers of the American "new journalism," and Tom Wolfe named him, with Dickens and Steinbeck, among the novelists whose tradition he followed. *Germinal*, defended by Zola against charges of exaggeration from conservatives and of slandering the workers from socialists, came to be seen as his masterpiece, and at his funeral crowds of workers greeted the cortège with cries of "Germinal! Germinal!"

## In this library

- [Germinal](germinal) — Étienne Lantier, a young unemployed worker, comes to the coal-mining town of Montsou in northern France, finds work in the pit with the family of the miner Maheu, falls in love with Maheu's daughter Catherine, and leads the miners in a long and violent strike that ends in defeat and disaster underground. Written in 1884–1885 after Zola had visited mining towns in the north, it was serialised in *Gil Blas* from November 1884 to February 1885 and published as a book in March 1885; it is the thirteenth novel of *Les Rougon-Macquart*.
