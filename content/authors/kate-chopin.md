Kate Chopin (1850–1904) was an American short story writer and novelist from St. Louis, Missouri, whose fiction is set mostly among the Creoles and Cajuns of Louisiana. Her stories, many of them first printed in *Vogue*, deal frankly with marriage, race and women's inner lives. Her novel *The Awakening* (1899) was widely condemned on publication and is now regarded as an early classic of feminist fiction.

## Life

Katherine O'Flaherty was born in St. Louis, Missouri, on February 8, 1850. Her father, Thomas O'Flaherty, was a successful businessman who had come to the United States from County Galway, Ireland; her mother, Eliza Faris, belonged to the French community of St. Louis, and Kate grew up speaking both French and English. In 1855 her father was killed in a railroad accident. She was raised largely by women, her mother, grandmother and great-grandmother, Victoire Charleville, who taught her French, music and history. She attended the St. Louis Academy of the Sacred Heart, where the nun Mary O'Meara encouraged her to write regularly, and graduated in 1868. During the Civil War her great-grandmother died, and her half-brother, who had enlisted in the Confederate army, died of typhoid fever after being captured.

On June 8, 1870, in St. Louis, she married Oscar Chopin, a Louisianian of French descent. After a wedding journey through Germany, Switzerland and France, the couple settled in New Orleans, where Oscar set up as a cotton factor. Like other prosperous families in the city they spent summers on Grand Isle, a Creole resort in the Gulf of Mexico. Between 1871 and 1879 Kate gave birth to five sons and a daughter, Lélia. In 1879 Oscar's business failed, and the family moved to Cloutierville, a small French village in Natchitoches Parish in northwestern Louisiana, where he managed plantations and kept a general store. In the local Creole culture Chopin found much of the material for her later fiction.

Oscar Chopin died in 1882, leaving his wife with six children and $42,000 in debt. She ran the store and plantations herself for a time before selling the business and moving the family back to St. Louis, where her mother died in 1885. Her obstetrician and family friend, Dr. Frederick Kolbenheyer, suggested that she write, both for her own sake and as a source of income. Influenced by Guy de Maupassant, she began to compose fiction, and in 1889 one of her stories appeared in the *St. Louis Post-Dispatch*. Her first novel, *At Fault*, about a Catholic widow in love with a divorced man, was published privately in 1890.

Through the 1890s she wrote about a hundred stories and kept careful records of where she sent them and what she was paid. They appeared in national magazines, among them *Vogue*, the *Atlantic Monthly*, *The Century Magazine*, *Harper's Young People* and *The Youth's Companion*. Houghton Mifflin published *Bayou Folk*, a collection of twenty-three stories, in 1894; it brought her national attention, and she wrote that she had seen a hundred press notices of it. A second collection, *A Night in Acadie*, followed from Way and Williams of Chicago in 1897 and was also praised. She wrote "The Storm" in 1898 but did not send it to publishers, and it was not printed until 1969.

She finished *The Awakening* in 1898, and Herbert S. Stone published it in April 1899. A few reviewers admired its artistry, but most called it morbid, unhealthy or "poison," and Willa Cather dismissed it as trite and sordid. The publisher later cancelled her contract for a third collection of stories, *A Vocation and a Voice*, which did not appear as a book until 1991. Chopin continued to write, mainly short stories, though she never earned much from her writing, and in 1900 she was listed in the first edition of Marquis *Who's Who*. On August 20, 1904, after a hot day at the St. Louis World's Fair, she suffered a brain hemorrhage, and she died two days later, on August 22. She was buried in Calvary Cemetery in St. Louis.

## Writing

Chopin was first read as a local-color writer of Louisiana, admired for her use of Creole and Cajun dialect. Her model was Maupassant, in whose stories she found "life, not fiction," told by a man "who had escaped from tradition and authority." Her own subject was "human existence in its subtle, complex, true meaning, stripped of the veil with which ethical and conventional standards have draped it," as she put it in an essay of 1894. Her fiction turns again and again on women's struggles to form an identity of their own within marriage and Southern society, and on desire, which she treated with unusual openness. "Désirée's Baby" takes up race and ancestry in antebellum Louisiana, and critics have traced in *The Awakening* her disagreement with Darwin's account of sexual selection and the female role.

Within a decade of her death she was recognized as a leading writer of her time; in 1915 Fred Lewis Pattee wrote that "some of [Chopin's] work is equal to the best that has been produced in France or even in America." By the 1950s, though, her books were out of print. Edmund Wilson discussed her in *Patriotic Gore* (1962) and encouraged the Norwegian scholar Per Seyersted, whose *Complete Works* and biography of 1969 began her revival. Seyersted wrote that she "broke new ground in American literature" as "the first woman writer in her country to accept passion as a legitimate subject for serious, outspoken fiction." Since the 1970s feminist critics have made *The Awakening* one of the novels most often taught in American literature courses.

## In this library

- [Désirée's Baby](desirees-baby) — Désirée, a foundling raised by a wealthy Creole family, marries the planter Armand Aubigny, who turns against her when their child appears to have African ancestry. First published in *Vogue* on January 14, 1893, and collected in *Bayou Folk* (1894).
- [The Story of an Hour](story-of-an-hour) — Louise Mallard, who has heart trouble, is told that her husband has been killed in a railroad accident and, alone in her room, discovers a sense of freedom. Written in April 1894 and published in *Vogue* on December 6, 1894, as "The Dream of an Hour."
- [A Pair of Silk Stockings](pair-of-silk-stockings) — Mrs. Sommers, a poor mother who comes into fifteen dollars, means to spend it on her children but instead gives herself a day of small luxuries in the city. Written in 1896 and first published in *Vogue* in 1897.
- [The Awakening](the-awakening) — On holiday at Grand Isle and back in New Orleans, Edna Pontellier, wife of a Creole businessman, falls in love with Robert Lebrun and begins to withdraw from the duties of wife and mother. Published by Herbert S. Stone on April 22, 1899.
