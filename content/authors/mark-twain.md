Mark Twain (1835–1910), the pen name of Samuel Langhorne Clemens, was an American humorist, novelist, journalist and lecturer. He grew up in a small Missouri town on the Mississippi River, and the river and its towns gave him the material for *The Adventures of Tom Sawyer* and *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn*. His use of colloquial American speech and his satire of hypocrisy made him one of the most widely read writers of his country.

## Life

Samuel Langhorne Clemens was born on November 30, 1835, in Florida, Missouri, the sixth of seven children of John Marshall Clemens and Jane Lampton. The family moved to Hannibal, Missouri, a port town on the Mississippi River that became the model for the St. Petersburg of his novels. His father died in 1847, and the boy left school to become a printer's apprentice. From 1851 he worked as a typesetter and wrote humorous sketches for the *Hannibal Journal*, a newspaper owned by his elder brother Orion. At eighteen he left Hannibal and worked as a printer in New York, Philadelphia, St. Louis and Cincinnati.

He then went back to the river. The pilot Horace Bixby took him on as a cub pilot between New Orleans and St. Louis, and after more than two years he was licensed. In 1858 his younger brother Henry, for whom he had found work on the steamboat *Pennsylvania*, died after the boat's boiler exploded, and Clemens held himself responsible for the rest of his life. The Civil War curtailed river traffic in 1861. He served for two weeks in a Confederate militia unit before it disbanded, and then travelled by stagecoach to Nevada with Orion, who had been given an office in the territorial government.

He failed as a silver miner in Virginia City and joined the staff of the *Territorial Enterprise*, where on February 3, 1863, he first signed a piece "Mark Twain," the leadsman's call for a depth of two fathoms. In 1864 he moved to San Francisco. His tall tale of a jumping frog, published in New York in November 1865, brought him national attention. In 1866 the *Sacramento Union* sent him to the Sandwich Islands (Hawaii), and his letters from there became the basis of his first lectures. In 1867 San Francisco and New York newspapers paid for his passage on the *Quaker City* excursion to Europe and the Middle East; his letters became *The Innocents Abroad* (1869), which made him widely popular.

On the voyage he met Charles Langdon, whose sister Olivia he married in Elmira, New York, in February 1870; he then worked for a time as an editor of the *Buffalo Express*. Their daughters were Susy, Clara and Jean. *Roughing It*, an account of his years in the West, appeared in 1872. The family then settled in Hartford, Connecticut, where they built a house next door to Harriet Beecher Stowe. In these years he wrote *The Adventures of Tom Sawyer*, *The Prince and the Pauper*, *Life on the Mississippi* (1883), *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* and *A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur's Court* (1889).

He earned large sums and lost most of them. He spent about $300,000 on the Paige typesetting machine, and his publishing house, Charles L. Webster and Company, prospered with the memoirs of Ulysses S. Grant and then failed. In 1891 the family moved to Europe. With the help of the Standard Oil executive Henry Huttleston Rogers he filed for bankruptcy in April 1894, and in July 1895 he set out on a lecture tour around the world to repay his creditors in full, although he was not legally bound to do so; the tour produced *Following the Equator* (1897). He returned to the United States in 1900 with his debts paid.

His last years were darkened by loss. Susy died of meningitis in 1896, Olivia died in Florence in 1904, and Jean died on December 24, 1909. From 1901 he was a vice-president of the Anti-Imperialist League, and he attacked Belgian rule in the Congo in "King Leopold's Soliloquy" (1905). In 1906 he began publishing chapters of his autobiography in the *North American Review*, and in 1907 Oxford University gave him an honorary doctorate. Born shortly after an appearance of Halley's Comet, he had predicted that he would "go out with it." He died of a heart attack on April 21, 1910, at his house, Stormfield, in Redding, Connecticut, the day after the comet's closest approach to the Sun, and was buried at Woodlawn Cemetery in Elmira.

## Writing

Twain began as a newspaper humorist of the Sagebrush School and a platform lecturer, and his early books are comic travel narratives and sketches. He later recalled that at first his "sole idea was to make comic capital out of everything I saw and heard." Over time humor became a vehicle for social criticism. *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn*, narrated by Huck himself, is among the first major American novels written throughout in vernacular English, and it joins comedy and adventure to a satire on racism and the slaveholding South. Ernest Hemingway wrote that "all modern American literature comes from one book by Mark Twain called *Huckleberry Finn*."

The book offended many on its American publication in 1885. The library committee of Concord, Massachusetts, excluded it, one member calling it "the veriest trash," and Louisa May Alcott said that if Twain could not "think of something better to tell our pure-minded lads and lasses he had best stop writing for them." Later objections have centred on its racial language and stereotypes, and it has been repeatedly restricted in American schools.

The 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* judged the three novels of the Mississippi, *Tom Sawyer*, *Huckleberry Finn* and *Pudd'nhead Wilson*, more significant than his travels and sketches, finding in them "a vanished civilization, peopled with typical figures, and presented with inexorable veracity," and in their author "a scorching hatred of humbug and hypocrisy." In later work such as *The Mysterious Stranger*, which was not published in his lifetime, he became a chronicler of the vanities, hypocrisies and cruelties of mankind. William Faulkner called him "the father of American literature."

## In this library

- [The Celebrated Jumping Frog of Calaveras County](jumping-frog) — At a mining camp the narrator is told by Simon Wheeler about Jim Smiley, a gambler who would bet on anything, and his jumping frog. First printed in the New York *Saturday Press* on November 18, 1865, as "Jim Smiley and His Jumping Frog," and the title story of Twain's first book (1867).
- [The Adventures of Tom Sawyer](tom-sawyer) — Tom Sawyer, living with his Aunt Polly in the Mississippi river town of St. Petersburg, tricks other boys into whitewashing a fence, courts Becky Thatcher, and runs off to play pirate with Huckleberry Finn. Published in London by Chatto and Windus and in the United States by the American Publishing Company.
- [Adventures of Huckleberry Finn](huckleberry-finn) — Huck fakes his own death to escape his violent father and travels down the Mississippi on a raft with Jim, a man escaping slavery. First published in the United Kingdom in December 1884 and in the United States in February 1885.
- [A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur's Court](connecticut-yankee) — Hank Morgan, an engineer from Connecticut, is knocked on the head and wakes in King Arthur's England, where he sets himself up as a rival to Merlin and tries to modernize the kingdom. Published in 1889 by Charles L. Webster and Company.
- [The £1,000,000 Bank-Note](million-pound-bank-note) — Henry Adams, a penniless American in London, is handed a single banknote for a million pounds by two rich brothers who have made a bet on what he can do with it. Published in 1893 and collected in *The £1,000,000 Bank-Note and Other New Stories* that year.
- [The Man That Corrupted Hadleyburg](hadleyburg) — A stranger offended by a town proud of its incorruptibility takes revenge with a sack of gold coins, and nineteen of its most prominent couples expose themselves as liars in trying to claim it. First published in *Harper's Monthly* in December 1899 and collected by Harper and Brothers in 1900.
