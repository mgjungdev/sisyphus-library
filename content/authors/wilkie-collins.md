Wilkie Collins (1824–1889) was an English novelist and playwright, a friend and collaborator of Charles Dickens and a master of the Victorian sensation novel. He is known above all for *The Woman in White* and *The Moonstone*, the second of which established many of the conventions of the modern detective novel.

## Life

William Wilkie Collins was born on 8 January 1824 at 11 New Cavendish Street, London, the elder son of William Collins, a Royal Academician and landscape painter, and his wife, Harriet Geddes. He was named after his father but known by his middle name, which honoured his godfather, the painter David Wilkie. His younger brother, Charles Allston Collins, was born in 1828. The boys were first taught at home by their mother, and the family was strictly religious. When Wilkie was twelve the family went to live in Italy and France, where he learned Italian and began to learn French. At the Reverend Cole's boarding school in Highbury he was bullied by a boy who made him tell a story every night before he could sleep, and Collins later said that this "brute" had first awakened in him his power as a storyteller.

At the end of 1840 he left school and was apprenticed as a clerk to the tea merchants Antrobus & Co. His first story, "The Last Stage Coachman," appeared in the *Illuminated Magazine* in August 1843. His father wanted him to have a steady profession, and in 1846 he entered Lincoln's Inn to study law; he was later called to the bar but never practised, though his legal knowledge served him in many of his novels. After his father's death in 1847 he wrote his first book, a memoir of the painter's life, published in 1848. His first novel, *Antonina, or the Fall of Rome*, was published by Richard Bentley in February 1850.

In March 1851 the painter Augustus Egg introduced him to Charles Dickens, who became his lifelong friend and mentor. Collins acted in Dickens's amateur theatricals, and his story "A Terribly Strange Bed" appeared in Dickens's weekly *Household Words* in April 1852. *Basil* followed in 1852 and *Hide and Seek* in 1854. During the writing of *Hide and Seek* he suffered what was probably his first attack of gout, from which he suffered for the rest of his life; he began to take laudanum for it regularly and became addicted. He joined the staff of *Household Words* in 1856, wrote the play *The Frozen Deep* in close collaboration with Dickens, and travelled with him in Switzerland and Italy and through the north of England.

*The Woman in White*, serialised in Dickens's *All the Year Round* from November 1859 to August 1860, was a great success and reached an eighth edition in book form by November 1860. *No Name*, *Armadale* and *The Moonstone*, all written within the decade, brought him an international reputation and financial security. In 1867 he and Dickens wrote *No Thoroughfare* together for the Christmas number of *All the Year Round*, and its stage version ran for two hundred nights at the Adelphi Theatre. The death of Dickens in 1870 was a great sorrow to him; in 1873 his brother Charles, who had married Dickens's daughter Kate, also died. In 1873–74 Collins toured the United States and Canada giving readings, and met Oliver Wendell Holmes and Mark Twain.

Collins was a critic of the institution of marriage. From 1858 he lived with Caroline Graves, a widow, whose daughter Harriet he treated as his own; apart from one short separation they lived together for the rest of his life. In 1868 he met Martha Rudd, then nineteen, at Winterton-on-Sea in Norfolk, and they had three children, born in 1869, 1871 and 1874. When with her he called himself William Dawson.

His later novels included *Man and Wife* (1870), *The New Magdalen*, *The Law and the Lady*, *The Black Robe* (1881) and *Heart and Science* (1883). In 1884 he was elected vice-president of the Society of Authors. His health and eyesight failed in his last years, and he encouraged younger writers such as Hall Caine. He died at 65 Wimpole Street, London, after a paralytic stroke, on 23 September 1889, and was buried in Kensal Green Cemetery; his last novel, *Blind Love*, was completed by Walter Besant.

## Writing

Collins's novels were classed in his own day as sensation novels, a genre that led to detective and suspense fiction. Like many writers of his time he first published most of his novels as magazine serials, and he was known for holding readers in suspense from week to week. The 1911 *Britannica* judged that his gift "was of the melodramatic order" and that "if his colours were occasionally crude and his methods violent, he was at least a master of situation and effect"; it thought Count Fosco in *The Woman in White* perhaps his masterpiece. Both that novel and *The Moonstone* are told through the accounts of several characters in turn, a method that gives the unravelling of a mystery the air of actual evidence.

He also wrote about the position of women and the social questions of his day: *Hide and Seek* has one of the first deaf characters in English fiction, and *No Name* attacks the law's treatment of children born outside marriage. After *The Moonstone* his novels turned more to social argument, and his popularity declined; Swinburne mocked the change: "What brought good Wilkie's genius nigh perdition? / Some demon whispered—'Wilkie! have a mission.'" T. S. Eliot called *The Moonstone* "the first, the longest, and the best of modern English detective novels," and Dorothy L. Sayers called it "probably the very finest detective story ever written."

## In this library

- [The Moonstone](the-moonstone) — A sacred Indian diamond, taken by theft and murder at the siege of Seringapatam, is left to Rachel Verinder for her eighteenth birthday and vanishes from her room that night, and the narratives of several witnesses follow Sergeant Cuff of Scotland Yard, her cousin Franklin Blake and others as they try to find the thief while three Hindu guardians watch for the stone. Serialised in *All the Year Round* from 4 January to 8 August 1868, and at the same time in *Harper's Magazine* in America.
