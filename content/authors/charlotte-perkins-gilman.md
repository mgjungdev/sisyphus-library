Charlotte Perkins Gilman (1860–1935) was an American writer, lecturer, sociologist and advocate of social reform, a leading figure in the women's rights movement of her time. She is best known for the short story "The Yellow Wallpaper," drawn from her own breakdown after the birth of her daughter, for the treatise *Women and Economics*, and for the utopian novel *Herland*.

## Life

Charlotte Anna Perkins was born on July 3, 1860, in Hartford, Connecticut, the daughter of Mary Fitch Westcott and Frederic Beecher Perkins, a member of the prominent Beecher family. Her father left the family while she was an infant, and she grew up in poverty, much of the time in Providence, Rhode Island. Her mother kept her children from close friendships and from reading fiction, but the girl found support among her father's aunts, among them the suffragist Isabella Beecher Hooker and Harriet Beecher Stowe, the author of *Uncle Tom's Cabin*. Her schooling was irregular: she attended seven schools for a total of about four years and left at fifteen, and she taught herself in the public library, where she read history and what was then called natural philosophy. In 1878 she enrolled in classes at the Rhode Island School of Design, and afterwards supported herself by designing trade cards, by tutoring and by painting.

In 1884 she married the artist Charles Walter Stetson, and their only child, Katharine Beecher Stetson, was born the following year. After the birth Gilman fell into a severe depression and was placed under the "rest cure" of the physician Silas Weir Mitchell, who ordered her to "live as domestic a life as possible," to have "but two hours' intellectual life a day," and never to "touch pen, brush or pencil as long as you live." Following this advice for a few months brought her close to collapse. In 1888 she separated from her husband, then a rare step, and moved with her daughter to Pasadena, California. The divorce came in 1894, and Stetson then married her close friend Grace Ellery Channing.

In Pasadena she became active in reform movements, joining the Nationalist Clubs, which sought to end class distinctions and the greed of capitalism. In 1890 she wrote a burst of essays and poems, a satirical poem, "Similar Cases," that won praise, and, on June 6 and 7, "The Yellow Wallpaper," which appeared in *The New England Magazine* in January 1892. Her first volume of poems, *In This Our World* (1893), brought her wider notice, and she began to earn her living as a lecturer. In 1894–95 she edited *The Impress*, a weekly of the Pacific Coast Women's Press Association. In 1896 she represented California at the convention of the National American Woman Suffrage Association in Washington and at the International Socialist and Labor Congress in London.

*Women and Economics* (1898), which argued that women could not be free until they were economically independent, became a best seller, was translated into several languages and made her internationally known. In 1900 she married her cousin Houghton Gilman, a Wall Street lawyer, and the couple lived in New York City until 1922. She followed with *Concerning Children* (1900), *The Home: Its Work and Influence* (1903), *Human Work* (1904) and *The Man-Made World* (1911), and in 1903 she addressed the International Congress of Women in Berlin. From 1909 to 1916 she wrote and edited her own monthly, *The Forerunner*, which ran to eighty-six issues and serialized her novels *What Diantha Did*, *The Crux*, *Moving the Mountain* and *Herland*.

After World War I her ideas seemed to many out of date. In 1922 she and Houghton moved to his family homestead in Norwich, Connecticut, and after his sudden death in 1934 she returned to Pasadena, where her daughter lived. She had been diagnosed with incurable breast cancer in January 1932. An advocate of the right of the terminally ill to end their lives, she took her own life with chloroform on August 17, 1935, writing that she "chose chloroform over cancer." Her autobiography, *The Living of Charlotte Perkins Gilman*, was published after her death.

## Writing

Gilman wrote poems, nearly two hundred short stories, novels, plays and thousands of essays, and almost all of it served her arguments about society. Her central theme was the economic dependence of women on men and the way it confined them to the home and stunted their development. She proposed kitchenless houses, communal cooking and professional child care so that women could work outside the home, and she argued that "there is no female mind. The brain is not an organ of sex." She called herself a humanist and is counted among the early founders of sociology. Carrie Chapman Catt called her "the most original and challenging mind which the (women's) movement produced."

Her fiction often imagines women escaping their expected roles, most fully in *Herland*, a society without men in which motherhood is honored and the conventions of the outside world look absurd. Her thinking was also shaped by the racial and eugenic ideas of her era, which appear in her essays and in novels such as *The Crux*.

"The Yellow Wallpaper" met a mixed reception: one reader wrote to the *Boston Transcript* that it "could hardly, it would seem, give pleasure to any reader." By the end of World War I Gilman's work had fallen out of fashion, and many of her short stories have been little studied. "The Yellow Wallpaper," however, has become the best-selling book of the Feminist Press, is widely anthologized in collections of American and women's literature, and has been the subject of extensive feminist and psychoanalytic criticism. *Herland* returned to print in 1979 with an introduction by the scholar Ann J. Lane.

## In this library

- [The Yellow Wallpaper](yellow-wallpaper) — A young mother confined by her physician husband to an upstairs nursery in a rented mansion records in her journal her growing obsession with the room's yellow wallpaper, behind whose pattern she comes to see a woman creeping. It was first published in *The New England Magazine* in January 1892 under the title "The Yellow Wall-paper."
- [Herland](herland) — Three American men find an isolated country inhabited only by women, who have borne children without men for two thousand years, and are slowly drawn to question the customs of their own society. It was serialized in monthly installments in Gilman's magazine *The Forerunner* in 1915 and first appeared in book form in 1979, published by Pantheon Books.
