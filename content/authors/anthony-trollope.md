Anthony Trollope (1815–1882) was an English novelist and civil servant of the Victorian era who wrote forty-seven novels and spent many years as an official of the Post Office. He is best known for the six Chronicles of Barsetshire, which begin with *The Warden* and *Barchester Towers*, for the political Palliser novels, and for the satire *The Way We Live Now*.

## Life

Anthony Trollope was born in London on 24 April 1815, a son of Thomas Anthony Trollope, a barrister and former fellow of New College, Oxford, and of Frances Milton Trollope, who later became a successful novelist and travel writer. His father failed at the bar and at farming, and the family's means were far below its gentlemanly expectations. Anthony was sent to Harrow as a day pupil, to Winchester and then to Harrow again; poor, shabby and friendless, he was bullied and miserable, and he left school with very little learning. While his mother went to America and tried without success to open a business in Cincinnati, he stayed in England. Her *Domestic Manners of the Americans* (1832) made her name, and her earnings afterwards supported the family. In 1834 his father fled to Belgium to escape his creditors, and the family settled near Bruges, where he died the following year.

After a few weeks as an usher in a school in Brussels, Trollope obtained in 1834 a clerkship in the General Post Office in London. By his own account his first seven years there were "neither creditable to myself nor useful to the public service": he was unpunctual, in debt and in constant trouble with his superiors. In 1841 he escaped by volunteering for an unwanted post as a surveyor's clerk in Ireland, based at Banagher. The travelling work suited him, he proved a capable official, and he took up fox hunting, which he pursued for decades. In June 1844 he married Rose Heseltine, the daughter of a Rotherham bank manager, and was moved to Clonmel; they had two sons.

His first novels, *The Macdermots of Ballycloran* (1847) and *The Kellys and the O'Kellys* (1848), were set in Ireland and failed, as did the historical novel *La Vendée* (1850). A commission to reorganise rural mail deliveries in the west of England and South Wales then took him across the country for two years. While wandering round Salisbury Cathedral one summer evening he conceived *The Warden*, published in 1855. It earned little, but it brought him notice, and *Barchester Towers* (1857) established his reputation. In 1858 he sold *The Three Clerks* for £250.

In 1859 Trollope was transferred to the eastern district of England and settled at Waltham Cross, within reach of London. For the new *Cornhill Magazine* he wrote *Framley Parsonage*, which began in January 1860, was paid £1,000 and became enormously popular. Thereafter his novels appeared in quick succession, and his acquaintance spread through literary London, including William Makepeace Thackeray and George Eliot. He helped found the *Fortnightly Review* in 1865, and postal history credits him with introducing the pillar box. Passed over for promotion, he resigned from the service in 1867, and he then edited *St Paul's Magazine*, which serialised *Phineas Finn*.

Long ambitious for a seat in Parliament, Trollope stood as a Liberal candidate for Beverley in 1868. He finished last, and the corruption of the election led to the disfranchisement of the borough; he later used the experience in his fiction. He travelled widely, visiting the United States several times, Australia, where his younger son farmed sheep, and South Africa, and wrote travel books on all of them. In 1880 he moved to the village of Harting in Sussex. He went on writing until he was disabled by a stroke, and he died in London on 6 December 1882. He was buried in Kensal Green Cemetery.

## Writing

Trollope wrote forty-seven novels, forty-two short stories, five travel books and studies of Thackeray, Cicero and Lord Palmerston. His *Autobiography*, published in 1883, explained that he wrote to a fixed daily quota, timing himself to produce 250 words every quarter of an hour, and that he wrote for money. The frankness damaged his reputation, since readers expected writers to wait for inspiration, and his popularity had already declined in his last years.

His novels deal with the clergy and gentry of the imaginary county of Barsetshire, with politics, money and marriage, and with the daily business of offices and institutions. The narrator often breaks in to remark that the story could take whatever turn its author pleased. Henry James disliked these intrusions but praised Trollope's "complete appreciation of the usual." The *Dictionary of National Biography* judged that "if to represent reality is to be a realist, Trollope is one of the greatest realists that ever wrote." George Eliot said that she could not have attempted *Middlemarch* without the example of his Barsetshire novels, and Leo Tolstoy read him with enthusiasm. George Orwell called *The Warden* "one of his best works," while observing that its author was "a shrewd critic, but no reformer." His following revived by the middle of the twentieth century, helped later by television adaptations, and his best novels and his autobiography continue to be widely read.

## In this library

- [The Warden](the-warden) — Septimus Harding, the gentle, music-loving warden of an almshouse in the cathedral city of Barchester, finds his comfortable income attacked by the young reformer John Bold and by a powerful London newspaper, and resigns rather than keep money he can no longer feel is his. It was published by Longman in 1855 in an edition of 1,000 copies, and it is the first of the Chronicles of Barsetshire.
