Virginia Woolf (1882–1941) was an English novelist, essayist and critic, one of the most influential modernist writers of the twentieth century and a pioneer of stream-of-consciousness narration. She is best known for the novels *Mrs Dalloway*, *To the Lighthouse*, *Orlando* and *The Waves*, and for the feminist essay *A Room of One's Own*.

## Life

Adeline Virginia Stephen was born on 25 January 1882 at Hyde Park Gate, South Kensington, London, the daughter of the writer and critic Leslie Stephen and of Julia Stephen, née Jackson. Both parents had children from earlier marriages, and she grew up in a large household with her sister Vanessa, the future painter, and her brothers Thoby and Adrian. She was educated at home, with the free run of her father's large library, and from 1897 took private lessons in Greek and Latin and attended lectures at the Ladies' Department of King's College London. As a child she wrote a family newspaper, the *Hyde Park Gate News*. Every summer until 1894 the family stayed at Talland House in St Ives, Cornwall, with a view of the Godrevy Lighthouse, a place she later drew on in *Jacob's Room*, *To the Lighthouse* and *The Waves*.

Her mother's death in 1895 brought on her first breakdown, and her half-sister Stella Duckworth, who had taken charge of the household, died in 1897. After her father's death in 1904 she suffered a second, more severe collapse. The Stephen children then left Kensington for 46 Gordon Square in Bloomsbury, where from 1905 Thoby's Cambridge friends gathered on Thursday evenings; this circle, which came to include Lytton Strachey, Clive Bell, John Maynard Keynes, Roger Fry and E. M. Forster, became known as the Bloomsbury Group. In 1904 she began to publish reviews and essays, first in *The Guardian*, a Church of England paper, and from 1905 in *The Times Literary Supplement*. Thoby died of typhoid in 1906 after a family journey to Greece, and Vanessa married Clive Bell. Virginia and Adrian moved to Fitzroy Square and later to Brunswick Square, and she began work on her first novel, which she first called *Melymbrosia*.

On 10 August 1912 she married Leonard Woolf, a friend of Thoby's from Trinity College, Cambridge, who had given up a post in the civil service in Ceylon. While she revised and corrected the proofs of her first novel she fell ill again, and in September 1913 she attempted suicide with an overdose of Veronal. *The Voyage Out* was published by Duckworth on 26 March 1915. The Woolfs moved to Hogarth House in Richmond, and in 1917 they bought a hand press and founded the Hogarth Press, whose first publication, *Two Stories*, contained her "The Mark on the Wall." The press went on to publish Katherine Mansfield, T. S. Eliot and E. M. Forster, the first English translation of the complete works of Sigmund Freud, and most of her own books. In 1919 they bought Monk's House in Rodmell, Sussex, which remained their country home.

*Night and Day* (1919) was followed by the experimental *Jacob's Room* (1922). In 1922 she met the writer Vita Sackville-West, who became a close friend and inspired *Orlando* (1928). From 1924 the Woolfs lived at 52 Tavistock Square in Bloomsbury, running the press from the basement. *Mrs Dalloway* appeared in 1925 and *To the Lighthouse* in 1927. Lectures she gave on women and fiction at Cambridge in 1928 became *A Room of One's Own* (1929), and she went on to publish *The Waves* (1931), *The Years* and *Three Guineas* (1938).

In 1940 the Woolfs' London houses were destroyed in the Blitz, and they settled at Rodmell. She was disappointed by the reception of her biography of Roger Fry, and after completing her last novel, *Between the Acts*, she sank into a severe depression. On 28 March 1941 she drowned herself in the River Ouse near her home. Leonard buried her ashes beneath an elm in the garden of Monk's House, and *Between the Acts* was published later that year.

## Writing

Woolf was one of the pioneers, with Marcel Proust, Dorothy Richardson and James Joyce, of the stream-of-consciousness technique. Her novels often have little conventional plot; events, many of them ordinary, are refracted through the minds of the characters, and her prose is intensely lyrical and full of sights and sounds. The sea and the waves are recurring images in her work, and the critic James Antoniou has noted how she "made a virtue of the semicolon, the shape and function of which resembles the wave, her most famous motif." Her fiction also takes up war and its aftermath, as in the shell-shocked soldier Septimus Smith of *Mrs Dalloway*. Her early novels, *The Voyage Out* and *Night and Day*, are comparatively conventional in form, while *Jacob's Room*, which followed her short fiction in *Monday or Tuesday* (1921), marked her move into experiment. She also wrote more than five hundred essays and reviews, and in her criticism and in *A Room of One's Own* and *Three Guineas* she examined the place of women in literature and society, as well as war and social class. In *A Room of One's Own* she imagined the lost women writers of the past, suggesting that behind the record of a witch being ducked lay "a lost novelist, a suppressed poet."

Her reputation was at its height in the 1930s and declined after the Second World War, until the growth of feminist criticism in the 1970s restored it. Her work has been translated into more than fifty languages, and writers including Margaret Atwood, Michael Cunningham, Gabriel García Márquez and Toni Morrison have acknowledged her influence.

## In this library

- [Jacob's Room](jacobs-room) — The life of Jacob Flanders, from his childhood through Cambridge and London to journeys in Italy and Greece, is shown almost entirely through the impressions of others, especially the women who know him, and his name recalls the generation lost in Flanders. It was published by the Hogarth Press on 26 October 1922.
- [The Voyage Out](voyage-out) — Rachel Vinrace, a sheltered young Englishwoman, sails to South America on her father's ship, where among a varied company of British travellers she begins to discover herself and the world of ideas, until she falls ill and dies of a fever. Her first novel, it was published in London by Duckworth in 1915.
