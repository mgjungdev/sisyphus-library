Thomas Hardy (1840–1928) was an English novelist and poet whose fiction is set largely in Wessex, a partly imagined region of south-west England built around his native Dorset. He is known for the novels *Far from the Madding Crowd* (1874), *The Mayor of Casterbridge* (1886), *Tess of the d'Urbervilles* (1891) and *Jude the Obscure* (1895), after which he published only poetry and verse drama.

## Life

Thomas Hardy was born on 2 June 1840 at Higher Bockhampton, a hamlet in the parish of Stinsford east of Dorchester in Dorset, where his father, also Thomas, worked as a stonemason and local builder. His mother, Jemima Hand, was well read and taught him herself until he went to school at Bockhampton at the age of eight. He then attended Mr Last's Academy for Young Gentlemen in Dorchester, where he learned Latin. The family could not pay for a university education, and in 1856, at sixteen, he was apprenticed to a Dorchester architect. In these years he became friends with the poet William Barnes and with Horace Moule, who introduced him to new scientific writing that cast doubt on literal readings of the Bible.

In 1862 he moved to London, enrolled at King's College London, and joined the practice of Arthur Blomfield as an assistant architect. In 1863 he won the medal of the Royal Institute of British Architects for an essay on coloured brick and terracotta architecture, and a prize of the Architectural Association for design. In the mid-1860s he supervised the excavation of part of the graveyard of St Pancras Old Church before the Midland Railway was extended across it. He never felt at home in London and was acutely conscious of class divisions; he read John Stuart Mill, whose *On Liberty* he counted among his cures for despair. His first short story appeared in *Chambers's Journal* in March 1865, and he wrote a good deal of verse while he was still uncertain whether to follow architecture or literature. In 1867 he left London for Weymouth.

His first novel, *The Poor Man and the Lady*, was read for the publishers Chapman and Hall by George Meredith, who advised him not to print it; it was never published, and Hardy later destroyed the manuscript. *Desperate Remedies* (1871) and *Under the Greenwood Tree* (1872) appeared anonymously, and *A Pair of Blue Eyes* (1873) under his own name. In 1870, while working on the restoration of the parish church of St Juliot in Cornwall, he met Emma Lavinia Gifford, and they married on 17 September 1874 at St Peter's Church, Paddington. That year *Far from the Madding Crowd*, serialised anonymously in the *Cornhill Magazine*, was attributed by many readers to George Eliot, and its success allowed him to give up architecture.

He lived for a time at Sturminster Newton, where he wrote *The Return of the Native* (1878), and later at Wimborne. In 1885 he and Emma settled at Max Gate, outside Dorchester, a house Hardy designed and his brother built. There he wrote *The Mayor of Casterbridge*, *The Woodlanders* (1887) and *Tess of the d'Urbervilles*. *Jude the Obscure* (1895), his last novel, met hostile reviews, and Walsham How, Bishop of Wakefield, was reputed to have burned his copy. From then on Hardy published poetry: *Wessex Poems* (1898), *Poems of the Past and the Present* (1901), and *The Dynasts*, an epic drama of the Napoleonic Wars that appeared in three parts between 1904 and 1908. He was appointed to the Order of Merit in 1910.

The Hardys had become estranged, but Emma's death in 1912 affected him deeply; he revisited the Cornish places of their courtship and wrote the elegies of *Poems 1912–13*. In 1914 he married his secretary, Florence Emily Dugdale, who was 39 years his junior, and in the same year he signed the "Authors' Declaration" supporting Britain's part in the First World War. From 1908 he worked with a Dorchester amateur company, later known as the Hardy Players, on stage versions of his work. By 1927 he had received 25 nominations for the Nobel Prize in Literature. He fell ill with pleurisy in December 1927 and died at Max Gate on 11 January 1928. After a dispute over his burial, his ashes were placed in Poets' Corner in Westminster Abbey and his heart was buried at Stinsford, in Emma's grave.

## Writing

Hardy's novels examine the social constraints on rural and working lives in Victorian England, especially those bound up with marriage, education and religion. Most are set in Wessex, the name of an early Saxon kingdom, which he first used for the region of his fiction in *Far from the Madding Crowd*; it eventually took in Dorset, Wiltshire, Somerset, Devon, Hampshire and much of Berkshire. Chance and fate drive his plots: a valentine sent as a joke or a missed wedding can turn a life, and his characters often meet at crossroads. His early novels borrow the legal complications of the sensation fiction of the 1860s, and the serial of *A Pair of Blue Eyes*, which left one of its characters hanging from a cliff, is considered the origin of the term "cliffhanger." He kept notebooks of newspaper stories, and the wife sale that opens *The Mayor of Casterbridge* came from press reports.

Arthur Symons, writing in the 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, called him "a fatalist, perhaps rather a determinist," and singled out *The Return of the Native* as "the most sombre" of his novels and *Jude the Obscure* as "his most thoughtful and least popular book." The late novels divided readers. *Tess* was at first refused publication and received mixed reviews, and Henry James wrote to Robert Louis Stevenson agreeing that it was "vile." One reviewer called *Jude* "Jude the Obscene," and Hardy later said that the bishop had burned the book "probably in his despair at not being able to burn me." Hardy regarded himself chiefly as a poet. His verse, written in many forms and often in invented stanzas, influenced Robert Frost, W. H. Auden, Dylan Thomas and Philip Larkin, who saw "suffering, or sadness" as central to his work, and D. H. Lawrence and Virginia Woolf were among the younger writers who admired him.

## In this library

- [Far from the Madding Crowd](far-from-the-madding-crowd) — Bathsheba Everdene, who inherits a farm at Weatherbury, is courted by the shepherd Gabriel Oak, the farmer William Boldwood and the soldier Sergeant Frank Troy. It was serialised anonymously in the *Cornhill Magazine* and published as a book on 23 November 1874.
- [The Mayor of Casterbridge](mayor-of-casterbridge) — Drunk at a country fair, the hay-trusser Michael Henchard sells his wife and baby daughter to a sailor, and years later, as mayor of Casterbridge, he is overtaken by the consequences. It was first issued in weekly parts from January 1886 and as a book in May 1886.
- [Tess of the d'Urbervilles](tess) — Tess Durbeyfield, a poor country girl sent to claim kinship with a rich family, is seduced by Alec d'Urberville and later abandoned by her husband, Angel Clare, when she tells him her past. It appeared in censored form as a serial in *The Graphic* in 1891 and as a three-volume book the same year.
- [Jude the Obscure](jude-the-obscure) — The stonemason Jude Fawley, who longs to study at Christminster, a city modelled on Oxford, lives unmarried with his cousin Sue Bridehead. A bowdlerised version ran in *Harper's New Monthly Magazine* from December 1894 to November 1895, and the book appeared in London dated 1896.
