George MacDonald (1824–1905) was a Scottish novelist, poet and Congregational minister who is often regarded as the founding father of modern fantasy writing. He is best known for the fantasies *Phantastes*, *At the Back of the North Wind*, *The Princess and the Goblin* and *Lilith*, and for fairy tales such as "The Light Princess" and "The Golden Key."

## Life

George MacDonald was born on 10 December 1824 at Huntly, Aberdeenshire, the youngest of five sons of George MacDonald, a farmer, and Helen MacKay, a sister of the Gaelic scholar Mackintosh MacKay. His father's family descended from the MacDonalds of Glencoe who suffered in the massacre of 1692. He grew up in the Congregational Church, in an atmosphere strongly marked by Calvinism, and he was troubled from childhood by weak lungs; tuberculosis, which the family regarded as a family disease, later killed two of his brothers, his mother and several of his own children.

From 1840 MacDonald studied at King's College, Aberdeen, winning prizes in chemistry and natural philosophy, and he graduated in 1845. He then earned his living as a tutor in London, and in 1848 he entered Highbury College to train for the Congregational ministry. In 1850 he became minister of Trinity Congregational Church at Arundel, Sussex, but his sermons, which preached God's universal love and the possibility of redemption for everyone, displeased his congregation, which proposed to cut his small stipend, and he resigned in 1853. In 1851 he had married Louisa Powell; they had six sons and five daughters.

MacDonald moved to Manchester, where he preached to a group of working men, but his health failed, and in 1856 he wintered in Algiers, encouraged by his friend Lady Byron. He then turned to literature. His first book was a dramatic poem, *Within and Without*; a volume of *Poems* followed in 1857, and in 1858 the prose "faerie romance" *Phantastes*. Settling in London, he wrote a long series of novels, many of them set among the Scottish poor, including *David Elginbrod*, *Alec Forbes of Howglen* (1865) and *Robert Falconer* (1868), and fairy tales that he gathered in *Dealings with the Fairies* (1867). His friendship with F. D. Maurice drew him into the Church of England as a lay member, though he went on preaching in nonconformist pulpits. He knew Ruskin, Tennyson and the Carlyles, and he was a mentor to Lewis Carroll: his advice, and his children's delight in the story, persuaded Carroll to publish *Alice*.

MacDonald lectured on Shakespeare and other literary subjects, taught for a time in London and edited the children's magazine *Good Words for the Young*. *At the Back of the North Wind* appeared as a book in 1871 and *The Princess and the Goblin* in 1872. In 1872–1873 he made a lecture tour of the United States, speaking to large audiences in Boston and meeting Longfellow among other writers. Despite his output his income stayed small, and in 1877 he was granted a civil list pension.

For the sake of his own and his family's health, MacDonald spent the greater part of each year for some twenty years at Bordighera on the Italian Riviera, in a house called Casa Coraggio that became a gathering place for British and Italian visitors, with readings of Dante and Shakespeare and performances of classic plays. There he wrote much of his later work, including the dark fantasy *Lilith* (1895). Several of his children died of tuberculosis, among them his eldest daughter Lilia. His wife died in 1902, soon after their golden wedding, and was buried at Bordighera. In his last years he lived at Haslemere, Surrey, in a house built for him by his sons. He died on 18 September 1905 at Ashtead, Surrey; his body was cremated at Woking and his ashes were buried in the English cemetery at Bordighera.

## Writing

MacDonald wrote two kinds of fiction: realistic novels of Scottish life, and fantasies and fairy tales of a mystical and symbolic kind. The novels were among the first realistic Scottish novels, and he has been credited with founding the "kailyard school" of Scottish writing; the 1911 *Britannica* called him "the precursor of a large school" of fiction about Scottish peasant life. In his fairy tales, imagery suggests a spiritual meaning without becoming a plain allegory. He said that he wrote "not for children, but for the child-like, whether they be of five, or fifty, or seventy-five."

His religious thought runs through all his work. He could never accept the Calvinist doctrine that God's love is limited to the elect, and he taught that God punishes only in order to heal and that all souls will finally be restored; his sermons were collected in three volumes of *Unspoken Sermons*.

MacDonald's fantasy shaped a later generation of writers. C. S. Lewis, who made him a character in *The Great Divorce*, wrote: "I have never concealed the fact that I regarded him as my master." J. R. R. Tolkien and Madeleine L'Engle also learned from him, and G. K. Chesterton said that *The Princess and the Goblin* had "made a difference to my whole existence."

## In this library

- [The Golden Key](golden-key) — Mossy, a boy who finds a golden key at the end of a rainbow, and Tangle, a girl who has fled into the fairy forest, are sent together by a wise woman called Grandmother to look for the country from which the shadows fall, and they grow old on the way. It was published in MacDonald's collection *Dealings with the Fairies* (London: Alexander Strahan, 1867).
- [The Light Princess](light-princess) — Cursed at her christening by her spiteful aunt to have no gravity, a princess floats through life unable to cry or to take anything seriously, except when she swims in the lake, until a prince offers his life to save it. It first appeared in 1864 as a story told within MacDonald's novel *Adela Cathcart*, and was reprinted in *Dealings with the Fairies* (1867).
