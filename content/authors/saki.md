Saki (1870–1916) was the pen name of Hector Hugh Munro, a British short-story writer, journalist and satirist whose witty, mischievous and often macabre stories mock the manners of Edwardian society. He is best known for his tales of country houses, aunts and clever, malicious children, many of them featuring the young idlers Reginald and Clovis Sangrail.

## Life

Hector Hugh Munro was born on 18 December 1870 in Akyab (now Sittwe), in British Burma, the youngest of three children of Charles Augustus Munro, an officer of the Indian Imperial Police, and his wife Mary Frances Mercer, the daughter of a rear admiral. In 1872, on a visit to England, his mother was charged by a cow; the shock caused a miscarriage, and she died soon afterwards. The children were sent home to Broadgate Villa in Pilton, near Barnstaple in North Devon, to be raised by their grandmother and two aunts, Charlotte and Augusta, in a strict and puritanical household. His sister Ethel later said that the aunt in his story "The Lumber Room" was an almost perfect portrait of Aunt Augusta, and the guardian in "Sredni Vashtar" is thought to be drawn from the same source. Educated at first by governesses, he was sent at about twelve to Pencarwick School in Exmouth and then as a boarder to Bedford School.

After his father retired from Burma in 1887, the family spent several years travelling in Europe. In 1893 Munro followed his father into the Indian Imperial Police and was posted to Burma, but repeated bouts of fever sent him back to England after about fifteen months. He then turned to journalism, writing for *The Westminster Gazette*, the *Daily Express*, *The Morning Post*, the *Bystander* and the *Outlook*. His first book, *The Rise of the Russian Empire* (1900), a historical study modelled on Gibbon, was the only one published under his own name. His earliest known stories appeared in February 1899, and in 1900 he began writing the text for a series of political sketches drawn by Francis Carruthers Gould, *Alice in Westminster*, for which he first used the name "Saki." The series lampooned the politicians of the day in the Liberal *Westminster Gazette* and was collected as *The Westminster Alice* in 1902. The pen name comes from the cupbearer in Edward FitzGerald's translation of the *Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám*.

In 1902 he joined *The Morning Post* as a foreign correspondent, reporting first from the Balkans and then from Russia, where he witnessed the revolution of 1905 in St Petersburg, and later from Paris. Meanwhile *Reginald* (1904), a book of sketches first printed in *The Westminster Gazette*, had appeared. He returned to London in 1908 and lived simply in a flat in Mortimer Street, writing sketches for the *Morning Post*, the *Bystander* and the *Westminster Gazette* and playing bridge at the Cocoa Tree Club. *Reginald in Russia* followed in 1910, *The Chronicles of Clovis* in 1911 and *Beasts and Super-Beasts* in 1914, together with two novels, *The Unbearable Bassington* (1912) and *When William Came* (1913), the second a fantasy of Britain under German occupation. Munro was homosexual at a time when sex between men was a crime in Britain, and that side of his life had to be kept secret.

When the First World War began, Munro was 43 and over the age for enlistment, but he refused a commission and in August 1914 joined the 2nd King Edward's Horse as a trooper. He soon transferred to the 22nd (Service) Battalion of the Royal Fusiliers, where he became a lance sergeant, and went to France with it in November 1915. More than once he returned to the front when officially too sick or injured to fight. In November 1916, during the Battle of the Ancre, he was sheltering in a shell crater near Beaumont-Hamel when he was killed by a German sniper; by several accounts his last words were "Put that bloody cigarette out!" He has no known grave and is commemorated on the Thiepval Memorial. After his death Ethel destroyed most of his papers and published her own account of their childhood. *The Toys of Peace* (1919) and *The Square Egg* (1924) collected stories and sketches left uncollected in his lifetime.

## Writing

Saki wrote short stories first for newspapers and collected them afterwards; he also wrote two novels, a full-length play, *The Watched Pot*, with Charles Maude, two one-act plays and his history of Russia. Many of the stories end with a sudden reversal, as when the approaching figures at the close of "The Interlopers" turn out to be wolves. Many set the conventions and hypocrisies of Edwardian England against the plain, ruthless life of nature. Writing for *The Guardian* on the centenary of his death, Stephen Moss observed: "In many of his stories, stuffy authority figures are set against forces of nature—polecats, hyenas, tigers. Even if they are not eaten, the humans rarely have the best of it." In several stories children ally themselves with such forces against the adults who govern them, as Conradin does with his polecat-ferret in "Sredni Vashtar," and the niece in "The Open Window" undoes a visitor with an invented tale of ghosts: "Romance at short notice was her speciality."

*The Chronicles of Clovis* was widely seen by his contemporaries as his best and most characteristic work, and the *Saturday Review* thought "Tobermory" "worthy of F. Anstey." He is often compared with O. Henry and Dorothy Parker. Oscar Wilde, Lewis Carroll and Rudyard Kipling influenced him, and he in turn influenced A. A. Milne, Noël Coward and P. G. Wodehouse. Collected editions of his work have appeared since the 1920s, among them *The Best of Saki* (1950), edited by Graham Greene, and his stories have been adapted many times for radio, television and the stage.

## In this library

- [Tobermory](tobermory) — At a country-house party the scientist Cornelius Appin teaches his hosts' cat, Tobermory, to speak, and the cat alarms the guests by repeating everything he has overheard. A first version appeared in *The Westminster Gazette* on 27 November 1909; Munro revised it, adding Clovis Sangrail as a guest, for *The Chronicles of Clovis* (1911).
- [The Open Window](open-window) — The nervous Framton Nuttel, paying a call in the country for the sake of his health, is told by his hostess's fifteen-year-old niece why the French window stands open on an October afternoon. It appeared in *The Westminster Gazette* on 18 November 1911 and was collected in *Beasts and Super-Beasts* (1914).
- [The Interlopers](interlopers) — Ulrich von Gradwitz and Georg Znaeym, whose families have feuded for generations over a strip of forest in the Carpathians, meet there on a winter night and are pinned together under a fallen tree. It appeared in the *Bystander* on 17 January 1912 and was collected after Munro's death in *The Toys of Peace* (1919).
