Herman Melville (1819–1891) was an American novelist, short story writer and poet of the period now called the American Renaissance. He drew on his years as a sailor and whaler for his early books and is best known for *Moby-Dick* (1851), "Bartleby, the Scrivener" and the posthumously published *Billy Budd*. Little read at his death, he became the subject of a revival that began around the centenary of his birth in 1919.

## Life

Herman Melville was born in New York City on August 1, 1819, the third of eight children of Allan Melvill, a merchant who imported French dry goods, and Maria Gansevoort Melvill. Both of his grandfathers had fought in the American Revolutionary War. The family lived in comfort during the 1820s on money Allan Melvill borrowed, and in 1830, deep in debt, he moved them to Albany and went into the fur business. He died in January 1832. Herman left school to work as a bank clerk and then in his elder brother Gansevoort's cap and fur store, returning to the Albany Academy for short periods. After the Panic of 1837 bankrupted Gansevoort, he taught at a district school near Lenox, Massachusetts, and studied surveying at Lansingburgh Academy without finding work in it.

In June 1839 he signed aboard the merchant ship *St. Lawrence* as a "boy" for a voyage from New York to Liverpool, an experience he later used in *Redburn*. In January 1841 he sailed from Massachusetts on the new whaler *Acushnet*, bound around Cape Horn for the Pacific. During the voyage he met the son of Owen Chase, first mate of the whaleship *Essex*, which a sperm whale had sunk in 1820, and was given a copy of Chase's account of the wreck. In the summer of 1842 he and a shipmate, Richard Tobias Greene, jumped ship at Nuku Hiva in the Marquesas Islands. He left the island on the Australian whaler *Lucy Ann*, joined a mutiny at Tahiti and was briefly jailed, and after a month as a beachcomber shipped on another whaler to Hawaii. In August 1843 he enlisted in the United States Navy as an ordinary seaman on the frigate *United States*, and he was discharged after the ship reached Boston in October 1844.

His family urged him to write down his adventures. *Typee*, based on his stay in the Marquesas, was published in London by John Murray in February 1846 and in New York the next month, and became a best seller; its sequel, *Omoo*, followed in 1847. On August 4, 1847, he married Elizabeth Knapp Shaw, daughter of Lemuel Shaw, Chief Justice of Massachusetts. They had four children. *Mardi* (1849), his first book not based on his own experience, was poorly received, and *Redburn* (1849) and *White-Jacket* (1850) were reviewed respectably but did not sell well enough to support his family.

In the summer of 1850 he met Nathaniel Hawthorne at a gathering of writers in the Berkshires and wrote the essay "Hawthorne and His Mosses" for *The Literary World*. With money borrowed from his father-in-law he bought a farm at Pittsfield, Massachusetts, which he named Arrowhead. There he finished *Moby-Dick*, dedicated to Hawthorne, which appeared as *The Whale* in London in October 1851 and as *Moby-Dick* in New York in November. It found few readers, and critics scorned *Pierre* (1852). From 1853 to 1856 he published short fiction in *Putnam's* and *Harper's* magazines, much of it collected in *The Piazza Tales* (1856). In 1856 and 1857 he toured Europe and the Mediterranean, meeting Hawthorne in England and visiting the Holy Land, and *The Confidence-Man* (1857) was the last novel he published.

He lectured on lyceum tours from 1857 to 1860, and in 1863 he moved his family to New York City. After visiting the Virginia battlefields of the Civil War in 1864, he published the poems of *Battle-Pieces and Aspects of the War* (1866). That year he became a customs inspector for the city, a post he held for nineteen years. His elder son, Malcolm, died of a gunshot wound at home in 1867, and his younger son, Stanwix, died in San Francisco in 1886. *Clarel* (1876), an 18,000-line poem about a pilgrimage to the Holy Land, was printed with money left by his uncle. He retired at the end of 1885, issued two books of poems, *John Marr and Other Sailors* (1888) and *Timoleon* (1891), in editions of twenty-five copies, and worked on the manuscript of *Billy Budd*. Melville died in New York City on September 28, 1891, and was buried in Woodlawn Cemetery in the Bronx.

## Writing

Melville's first books turned his voyages into narrative. Reviewing *Typee* anonymously in the *Salem Advertiser*, Hawthorne called it "lightly but vigorously written." With *Mardi* Melville moved toward allegory and philosophical speculation, and *Moby-Dick* joins exact accounts of whaling and shipboard work to questions of good and evil, class and the existence of God. Its sources include Shakespeare, Thomas Carlyle, Sir Thomas Browne and the Bible, and besides narrative prose it uses songs, catalogs, stage directions, soliloquies and asides. Scholars name the Bible, Shakespeare and Milton as the chief sources of his style, which over time moved from the superabundant vocabulary of *Moby-Dick* to the long sentences and short paragraphs of *The Confidence-Man* and *Billy Budd*.

British reviewers mostly admired *The Whale*, though Henry Chorley in the *Athenaeum* called it "an ill-compounded mixture of romance and matter-of-fact," and American readers were fewer still. His writing earned him just over $10,000 in his lifetime, and by 1876 all of his books were out of print. The *Encyclopaedia Britannica* of 1911 judged his later tales "turgid, eccentric, opinionative, and loosely written," while granting that his sea books portrayed seafaring life "with vigour and originality."

The revival began with an article by Carl Van Doren in 1917 and Raymond Weaver's biography *Herman Melville: Mariner and Mystic* (1921), followed by D. H. Lawrence's *Studies in Classic American Literature* (1923) and Lewis Mumford's *Herman Melville* (1929). Lawrence called *Moby-Dick* "the greatest book of the sea ever written," and it came to be counted among the Great American Novels. Melville wrote mainly poetry for about twenty-five years, and his verse was later championed by Robert Penn Warren.

## In this library

- [Moby-Dick](moby-dick) — The sailor Ishmael tells of the whaling voyage of the *Pequod*, whose captain, Ahab, hunts the white sperm whale that took his leg. Published in three volumes as *The Whale* in London in October 1851 and as *Moby-Dick; or, The Whale* in New York in November 1851.
- [Bartleby, the Scrivener](bartleby) — A Wall Street lawyer hires a copyist, Bartleby, who begins to answer every request with "I would prefer not to" and in the end does nothing at all. First published anonymously in *Putnam's Monthly Magazine* in November and December 1853 and collected in *The Piazza Tales* (1856).
- [Billy Budd](billy-budd) — In 1797 the young sailor Billy Budd, pressed into service on the warship *Bellipotent*, strikes and kills the master-at-arms John Claggart, who has falsely accused him of mutiny, and Captain Vere holds that the law requires him to hang. Left unfinished at Melville's death, it was first published in 1924 in a transcription by Raymond Weaver.
