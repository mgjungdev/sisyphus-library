Charlotte Brontë (1816–1855) was an English novelist and poet, the eldest of the three Brontë sisters who survived into adulthood. She is best known for *Jane Eyre*, published under the pseudonym Currer Bell, which was a great success on its appearance and is now acknowledged as a classic of English literature; her other novels include *Shirley*, *Villette* and the posthumous *The Professor*.

## Life

Charlotte Brontë was born on 21 April 1816 at Thornton, near Bradford in Yorkshire, the third of six children of Patrick Brontë, an Irish-born clergyman of the Church of England who had studied at St John's College, Cambridge, and his Cornish wife, Maria Branwell. In 1820 the family moved to Haworth, a village on the edge of the Yorkshire moors, where Patrick became perpetual curate and the family lived in the parsonage beside the churchyard. Maria Brontë died on 15 September 1821, and her sister Elizabeth Branwell came to look after the children.

In 1824 Charlotte was sent with her sisters Maria, Elizabeth and Emily to the Clergy Daughters' School at Cowan Bridge, where the food was poor and disease was frequent. Maria and Elizabeth fell ill and died at home in 1825, and Charlotte later portrayed the school as Lowood in *Jane Eyre*. Taught at home thereafter, the surviving children read widely and invented imaginary worlds; with her brother Branwell, Charlotte wrote a great many tiny books of stories and verse about the Glass Town Confederacy and later the kingdom of Angria.

In 1831 she became a pupil at Margaret Wooler's school at Roe Head, where she made lifelong friends of Ellen Nussey and Mary Taylor. She left the next year to teach her sisters at home, and from 1835 to 1838 she returned to Roe Head as a teacher, lonely and unhappy. In December 1836 she wrote to the Poet Laureate, Robert Southey, about her ambitions as a poet and was told that "literature cannot be the business of a woman's life." In 1839 she refused a proposal of marriage from Ellen's brother Henry Nussey, and she worked briefly and unhappily as a governess with two Yorkshire families.

Hoping to open a school of their own, Charlotte and Emily went in 1842 to the Pensionnat Heger in Brussels, run by Constantin Heger and his wife. They were called home that autumn by the death of their aunt. Charlotte returned to Brussels alone in 1843 to teach, but her attachment to Heger, a married man, became an unrequited passion, and lonely and depressed she came back to Haworth in 1844. The sisters' plan for a school at the parsonage attracted no pupils.

In 1846 the three sisters paid for the publication of *Poems by Currer, Ellis and Acton Bell*; only two copies were sold. Charlotte's first novel, *The Professor*, went from publisher to publisher, until Smith, Elder & Co. declined it but asked for a longer book. She sent them *Jane Eyre*, which appeared in October 1847 and was an immediate success. Within eight months of 1848 and 1849 she lost her brother Branwell, Emily and Anne. She finished *Shirley*, a novel of industrial unrest, after Anne's death, and it was published in October 1849.

Once her identity was known, she visited London as the guest of her publisher George Smith, met William Makepeace Thackeray, to whom she had dedicated *Jane Eyre*, and in August 1850 met Elizabeth Gaskell, who became a close friend. She disappointed London society with her shyness and rarely left Haworth for long. *Villette*, drawing on her years in Brussels, was published in 1853. On 29 June 1854 she married her father's curate, Arthur Bell Nicholls, in Haworth church, and they spent their honeymoon in Ireland. She became pregnant soon afterwards, her health failed, and she died on 31 March 1855, a few weeks before her thirty-ninth birthday. She was buried in the family vault in Haworth church. *The Professor* and Gaskell's *Life of Charlotte Brontë* were both published in 1857.

## Writing

Charlotte Brontë believed that fiction was most convincing when grounded in personal experience, and her novels transform her own life, from Cowan Bridge in *Jane Eyre* to Brussels in *The Professor* and *Villette*. *Jane Eyre* combined Romanticism, naturalism and Gothic melodrama, but broke new ground with its intense first-person account of a plain, poor and independent woman. G. H. Lewes called it "an utterance from the depths of a struggling, suffering, much-enduring spirit." *Shirley*, a third-person novel of industrial unrest and the position of women, was less warmly received; a reviewer in *The Times* called it "at once the most high-flown and the stalest of fictions." As critics came to suspect that Currer Bell was a woman, her work was attacked as "coarse," and *Villette* was criticised for being insufficiently "feminine" in its portrayal of Lucy Snowe's desires, though Lewes praised it among the few books of "persons thinking and speaking for themselves."

The 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* found her verse undistinguished but judged that *Jane Eyre* and *Villette* "will always command attention whatever the future of English fiction, by virtue of their intensity, their independence, their rough individuality." Gaskell's biography fixed the image of the sisters' lonely life at Haworth, and Charlotte's letters remain the chief source for the family's history. Modern feminist critics have given particular attention to her heroines and to *Villette* as a study of isolation and repression.

## In this library

- [Jane Eyre](jane-eyre) — The orphan Jane Eyre survives a harsh childhood and the charity school of Lowood, becomes governess at Thornfield Hall and falls in love with its master, Mr Rochester, who hides a terrible secret in the attic. It was published by Smith, Elder & Co. of London in October 1847, in three volumes, under the pseudonym Currer Bell.
- [Villette](villette) — After an unexplained family disaster, the solitary Englishwoman Lucy Snowe crosses to the kingdom of Labassecour and teaches at Madame Beck's girls' school in the city of Villette, where she falls in love with the irascible professor Paul Emanuel. It was published in 1853 in three volumes by Smith, Elder & Co., the last of her novels to appear in her lifetime.
