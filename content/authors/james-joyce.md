James Joyce (1882–1941) was an Irish novelist, short story writer and poet, and one of the leading writers of literary modernism. Though he lived abroad for most of his adult life, nearly all of his fiction is set in his native Dublin, from the stories of *Dubliners* (1914) and the novel *A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man* (1916) to *Ulysses* (1922) and *Finnegans Wake* (1939).

## Life

James Augustine Aloysius Joyce was born on 2 February 1882 at 41 Brighton Square, Rathgar, Dublin, the eldest of the ten surviving children of John Stanislaus Joyce and Mary Jane "May" Murray. In 1887 his father became a rate collector for Dublin Corporation, and the family moved to Bray. Joyce entered Clongowes Wood College, a Jesuit boarding school in County Kildare, in 1888, but had to leave in 1891 when his father could no longer pay the fees. That year he wrote "Et Tu, Healy," a poem on the death of Charles Stewart Parnell, which his father had printed, and the family began to slide into poverty; John Joyce was dismissed from his post in 1893. Through the Jesuit John Conmee, James and his brother Stanislaus were admitted without fees to Belvedere College in Dublin, where he won first place for English composition in his last two years.

In 1898 he entered University College, Dublin, to study English, French and Italian. His first publication, a review of Henrik Ibsen's *When We Dead Awaken*, appeared in *The Fortnightly Review* in 1900, and in 1901 he attacked the Irish Literary Theatre in the essay "The Day of the Rabblement." He graduated in 1902 and went to Paris to study medicine, but soon gave it up. In April 1903 he returned to Dublin because his mother was dying; she died that August. In 1904 he began the autobiographical novel *Stephen Hero*, published in the *Irish Homestead* three stories that became the beginning of *Dubliners*, and on 16 June went out for the first time with Nora Barnacle, a young woman from Galway. *Ulysses* takes place on that date.

In October 1904 Joyce and Nora left Ireland together. He taught English at the Berlitz school in Pola and, from March 1905, in Trieste, then part of Austria-Hungary. Their son Giorgio was born in 1905 and their daughter Lucia in 1907. Apart from some months as a bank clerk in Rome in 1906 and 1907 and visits to Ireland, Trieste remained his home until 1915. Publishers balked at *Dubliners* for its language and for fear of libel: Grant Richards in London withdrew from an agreement, and in 1912 the Dublin firm of Maunsel had the printed sheets destroyed. A book of poems, *Chamber Music*, appeared in London in 1907, and in 1909 Joyce opened the Volta, the first cinema in Ireland. After the 1912 visit he never returned to Dublin.

Grant Richards finally published *Dubliners* on 15 June 1914. Ezra Pound, who had written to Joyce on W. B. Yeats's advice, arranged for *A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man* to be serialized in *The Egoist*, and B. W. Huebsch published it in New York in 1916. When Italy entered the First World War in 1915, Joyce took his family to neutral Zurich, where he lived on grants and on the support of patrons, chiefly Harriet Shaw Weaver of *The Egoist*, while he wrote *Ulysses* and underwent many eye operations. His only play, *Exiles*, was published in 1918. That year *The Little Review* in New York began to serialize *Ulysses*, until a prosecution for obscenity stopped it in 1921.

After a short return to Trieste, Joyce moved to Paris in 1920, and the city was his home for twenty years. Sylvia Beach published *Ulysses* through her bookshop, Shakespeare and Company, in 1922. It was banned in the United Kingdom and could not be published legally in the United States until 1934. In 1923 Joyce began *Finnegans Wake*, which appeared in parts as "Work in Progress" and was published complete by Faber and Faber in 1939. His sight continued to fail, and by 1930 he was nearly blind in his left eye. He and Nora married in London on 4 July 1931, and in the following years he often travelled to Switzerland for eye surgery and for the treatment of Lucia, who was diagnosed with schizophrenia. After the fall of France in 1940 the family returned to Zurich. Joyce died there on 13 January 1941, two days after surgery for a perforated duodenal ulcer, and was buried in the Fluntern Cemetery.

## Writing

Joyce's fiction stays close to the Dublin he left, and many of its characters resemble his family, friends and enemies there. He is quoted as saying: "For myself, I always write about Dublin, because if I can get to the heart of Dublin I can get to the heart of all the cities of the world. In the particular is contained the universal." *Dubliners* is a naturalistic picture of the city's middle-class life, ordered by his own division into "childhood, adolescence, maturity, and public life," and its stories often turn on an epiphany, a moment of sudden self-understanding. Joyce said he wrote them "in a style of scrupulous meanness," withholding comment on what is seen and heard. *A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man* follows Stephen Dedalus's growth into artistic self-consciousness through free indirect speech that lets the reader follow his developing consciousness. *Ulysses* parallels the episodes of Homer's *Odyssey* in eighteen episodes, each in its own style, and makes wide use of interior monologue.

Early readers were divided. In a 1916 publisher's report Edward Garnett found *A Portrait* "too discursive, formless, unrestrained." In 1917 Pound wrote that "James Joyce produces the nearest thing to Flaubertian prose that we now have in English," and H. G. Wells wrote that "one believes in Stephen Dedalus as one believes in few characters in fiction," while warning of Joyce's "cloacal obsession." T. S. Eliot called "The Dead" one of the greatest short stories in English. Joyce's work, a small canon of three novels, one story collection, one play and two short books of poems, has since generated more than 15,000 articles, books, theses and editions, and every 16 June readers mark the day of *Ulysses* as Bloomsday.

## In this library

- [Araby](araby) — A boy on North Richmond Street in Dublin, in love with his friend Mangan's sister, promises to bring her a gift from the Araby bazaar and reaches it only as the stalls are closing. The third story in *Dubliners* (1914).
- [The Dead](the-dead) — At the annual Christmastime party given by his aunts, the Morkan sisters, Gabriel Conroy worries over his speech, and later that night his wife Gretta tells him of Michael Furey, a boy who loved her in Galway and died young. Conceived in Rome and finished in Trieste in 1907, it is the last and longest story in *Dubliners* (1914).
- [A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man](portrait-of-the-artist) — Stephen Dedalus grows up in Catholic Dublin, passes through religious crisis, and rejects the conventions of church and nation to leave Ireland as an artist. Serialized in *The Egoist* from February 1914 to September 1915 and published as a book by B. W. Huebsch in New York on 29 December 1916.
