O. Henry (1862–1910) was the pen name of William Sydney Porter, an American writer known chiefly for his short stories. Many of them are set among the working people, criminals and outcasts of New York City, and they are known for their witty narration and surprise endings.

## Life

William Sidney Porter was born in Greensboro, North Carolina, on September 11, 1862, during the Civil War; he changed the spelling of his middle name to Sydney in 1898. His father, Algernon Sidney Porter, was a physician. His mother died when he was three, and he and his father moved into the home of his paternal grandmother. He was taught at a school run by his aunt, who continued to tutor him until he was fifteen. He read constantly, and his favourite books were Lane's translation of *The Arabian Nights* and Burton's *Anatomy of Melancholy*. He then worked as a clerk in his uncle's drugstore in Greensboro, and in 1881 he was licensed as a pharmacist.

In March 1882, hoping that a change of air would cure a persistent cough, he went to Texas, where he worked on a sheep ranch in La Salle County for two years. In 1884 he moved to Austin. He was known there for his wit and storytelling and sang with a group called the "Hill City Quartette." In 1887 he eloped with Athol Estes, and their daughter Margaret was born in 1889. He worked as a draftsman in the Texas General Land Office, drawing maps from surveys, and from 1891 as a teller and bookkeeper at the First National Bank of Austin. The bank was loosely run, and Porter was careless with his books. He also produced a humorous weekly, *The Rolling Stone*, writing and illustrating much of it himself; it failed in 1895. That year he moved to Houston to write for the *Houston Post*.

Federal auditors then found shortages in his accounts at the Austin bank, and he was arrested on charges of embezzlement. Due to stand trial in July 1896, he fled instead, by way of New Orleans, to Honduras, where he began the stories that became *Cabbages and Kings*, the book in which he coined the phrase "banana republic." When he learned that his wife was dying, he returned to Austin early in 1897 and gave himself up. Athol died of tuberculosis in July 1897. In 1898 he was convicted and sent to the Ohio Penitentiary in Columbus, where, as a licensed pharmacist, he worked as night druggist in the prison hospital. While in prison he published fourteen stories under various pseudonyms, sent out through a friend in New Orleans so that editors did not know where the writer was. The name "O. Henry" first appeared over "Whistling Dick's Christmas Stocking" in *McClure's Magazine* in December 1899. He was released for good behaviour on July 24, 1901, and joined his daughter in Pittsburgh.

In 1902 he moved to New York City to be near his publishers, and the years that followed were the most productive of his life. Under an agreement made in December 1903 he supplied the *New York World* with a story every week, and in New York he wrote 381 short stories in all. *Cabbages and Kings* was his first collection, and *The Four Million* followed in April 1906. In 1907 he married Sarah Lindsey Coleman, known as Sallie, whom he had known in his youth in North Carolina; she left him in 1909. A heavy drinker, he was in failing health from about 1908. He died in New York on June 5, 1910, and was buried in Riverside Cemetery in Asheville, North Carolina.

## Writing

Most of Porter's stories are set in his own time, and many in New York, which he called "Bagdad-on-the-Subway." His characters are often working people, such as policemen and waitresses, along with criminals and outcasts, and some stories take place in small towns or in other cities. He gathered material by loitering in hotel lobbies and talking with the people he found there. The title of *The Four Million* answered Ward McAllister's claim that only four hundred people in New York were worth noticing. In his day he was called the American answer to Guy de Maupassant, whose stories likewise dealt with common people and often turned on a twist at the end.

His wit and plot twists delighted readers but were often dismissed by critics. Their "gorgeousness of imagination" recalled *The Arabian Nights*, but their "constant striving for effect" and heavy use of slang led many to see in them a degeneration into "literary vaudeville." He was thought to be at his best in describing the endlessly varied types of New York City. His stories went early to the screen: D. W. Griffith adapted "The Cop and the Anthem" as *Trying to Get Arrested* in 1909, and the film *O. Henry's Full House* (1952) was made from five of them. He was especially popular in Russia in the 1920s. The O. Henry Award, an annual prize for outstanding short stories, is named after him, and his story "The Caballero's Way" introduced the Cisco Kid.

## In this library

- [The Cop and the Anthem](cop-and-the-anthem) — Soapy, a homeless man in New York, tries every petty offence he can think of to get himself sent to Blackwell's Island for the winter, and is arrested only when he has resolved to reform. It dates from December 1904 and was collected in *The Four Million* (1906).
- [The Romance of a Busy Broker](romance-of-a-busy-broker) — The New York broker Harvey Maxwell spends a frantic day on the Exchange, then stops to propose to his stenographer, Miss Leslie, having forgotten that he married her the evening before. It was collected in *The Four Million* (1906).
- [The Furnished Room](furnished-room) — A young man searching New York for a missing singer, Eloise Vashner, takes a shabby rented room where he catches the scent of her mignonette. It was first published in the *New York World* on August 14, 1904, and collected in *The Four Million*.
- [The Gift of the Magi](gift-of-the-magi) — On Christmas Eve, Della sells her hair to buy a chain for her husband Jim's watch, while Jim sells the watch to buy combs for her hair. First published as "Gifts of the Magi" in the *New York Sunday World* on December 10, 1905, and collected in *The Four Million* in 1906.
- [After Twenty Years](after-twenty-years) — Bob comes back from the West to keep an appointment made twenty years before with his friend Jimmy Wells, and is met by a policeman instead. It appeared in *The Four Million* in 1906.
- [The Last Leaf](last-leaf) — In Greenwich Village during a pneumonia epidemic, a sick young artist believes she will die when the last leaf falls from an ivy vine outside her window. It first appeared in the *New York World* on October 15, 1905, and was collected in *The Trimmed Lamp* (1907).
- [The Ransom of Red Chief](ransom-of-red-chief) — Two small-time crooks kidnap the son of a prominent citizen in Alabama and find the boy so unbearable that they end up paying his father to take him back. It was first published in *The Saturday Evening Post* on July 6, 1907.
