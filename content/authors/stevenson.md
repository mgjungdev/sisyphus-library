Robert Louis Stevenson (1850–1894) was a Scottish novelist, essayist, poet and travel writer, best known for *Treasure Island*, *Kidnapped* and *Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde*. Chronically ill for most of his life, he moved between Scotland, France, England, America and the South Pacific, and spent his last years in Samoa.

## Life

Stevenson was born on 13 November 1850 at 8 Howard Place, Edinburgh, the only child of Thomas Stevenson, a civil engineer from a family of lighthouse builders, and Margaret Isabella Balfour, daughter of the minister Lewis Balfour. He was a sickly child with weak lungs, and his schooling at Edinburgh preparatory schools, the Edinburgh Academy and a brief spell at an English boarding school was often interrupted by illness. His nurse, Alison Cunningham, filled his childhood with religious instruction and stories of the Scottish Covenanters. At sixteen he saw his first pamphlet printed, *The Pentland Rising* (1866), an account of a Covenanter rebellion.

In 1867 he entered the University of Edinburgh to train as an engineer in the family profession. He won a silver medal in 1871 for a paper on lighthouse improvements, but in the same year turned to law instead. He was called to the Scottish bar in July 1875 and never practised. A quarrel with his father over religion in 1873 caused lasting distress in the family. That year he began publishing essays in London periodicals and came to know the critic Sidney Colvin, the poet W. E. Henley, Edmund Gosse and Andrew Lang.

A canoe trip through Belgium and France gave him his first book, *An Inland Voyage* (1878), followed by *Travels with a Donkey in the Cevennes* (1879). In 1876, in the artists' colony at Grez-sur-Loing near Fontainebleau, he met Fanny Van de Grift Osbourne, an American woman separated from her husband. After she returned to California, Stevenson crossed the Atlantic in August 1879, travelling by emigrant ship and train, and arrived seriously ill. He recovered in Monterey and San Francisco, and in May 1880, after her divorce, they married. They spent their honeymoon in an abandoned mining camp on Mount Saint Helena, the subject of *The Silverado Squatters* (1883), and returned to Britain that August with Fanny's son Lloyd Osbourne.

The next years were a search for a healthy climate, including winters at Davos in Switzerland. In the summer of 1881 at Braemar, in the Scottish Highlands, he began *Treasure Island* to entertain his stepson, starting from the idea of a secret map. It ran as a serial in *Young Folks* and appeared as a book in 1883. In 1884 the family settled in Bournemouth, where his father bought them a house that Stevenson named Skerryvore, after a lighthouse built by his uncle Alan. Though often bedridden, he wrote much of his best-known work there, including *A Child's Garden of Verses* (1885), *Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde* (1886) and *Kidnapped* (1886). Henry James, then living nearby, became a close friend.

Thomas Stevenson died in May 1887, and in August his son left Europe for good. He spent the winter of 1887–88 at Saranac Lake in the Adirondacks, writing essays for *Scribner's Magazine* and beginning *The Master of Ballantrae*. In June 1888 he sailed from San Francisco in the chartered yacht *Casco* with his family, visiting the Marquesas, Tahiti and Hawaii, and in 1889 travelled to the Gilbert Islands on a trading schooner. The family reached Samoa in December 1889. Stevenson bought land above Apia, named the estate Vailima, and settled there in November 1890. Samoans called him Tusitala, "teller of tales." He took up the Samoan side against the colonial powers in letters to *The Times* and in *A Footnote to History* (1892), and kept writing: *Catriona* (1893), the sequel to *Kidnapped*, *The Ebb-Tide* (1894) and the unfinished *Weir of Hermiston*. He died suddenly of a cerebral haemorrhage at Vailima on 3 December 1894, aged 44, and was carried by Samoans to be buried on the summit of Mount Vaea.

## Writing

Stevenson worked in many forms: the personal essay, the travel book, verse for children, the historical romance, the adventure story and the tale of terror. Across all of them he was known as a careful stylist, and his contemporaries compared the precision, lucidity and grace of his prose to the best French writing. His adventure stories are told with great economy, through vivid scenes and memorable figures such as Long John Silver and Alan Breck Stewart. Recurring concerns run beneath the action: divided loyalty, complicity and guilt, and the doubleness of human nature. In "A Chapter on Dreams" (1888) he described trying for years to find a story for "that strong sense of man's double being," until a dream gave him the key scenes of *Jekyll and Hyde*. Scottish history and landscape, especially the Covenanters and the aftermath of the Jacobite rising of 1745, shaped *Kidnapped* and much of his later fiction.

*Treasure Island* made him a popular author for the first time. *Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde* sold slowly until a favourable review in *The Times* in January 1886; within six months about 40,000 copies had been sold, and the story was quoted in sermons and religious papers. *Kidnapped* was praised for its characters and its handling of Highland history. In his lifetime Stevenson was a celebrity, admired by writers including Henry James, Arthur Conan Doyle and Rudyard Kipling. Much twentieth-century criticism later set him aside as a writer for children or of horror, before his reputation recovered.

## In this library

- [Treasure Island](treasure-island) — Jim Hawkins's account of a voyage in search of buried treasure and of the ship's cook Long John Silver, first serialized in *Young Folks* (1881–82) and published as a book in 1883.
- [The Body Snatcher](body-snatcher) — A short tale of terror published in the *Pall Mall Gazette* in December 1884, about two medical students drawn into the trade in corpses at the time of the Burke and Hare murders.
- [Kidnapped](kidnapped) — The adventures of the young David Balfour, cheated by his uncle and kidnapped at sea, who flees across the Highlands with the Jacobite Alan Breck Stewart after the Appin murder of 1752.
- [Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde](jekyll-and-hyde) — A London lawyer's inquiry into the link between his respected friend Dr Jekyll and the brutal Edward Hyde, published in January 1886; Stevenson's essay "A Chapter on Dreams" describes how the story came to him.
