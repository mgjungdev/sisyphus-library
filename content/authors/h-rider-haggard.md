H. Rider Haggard (1856–1925) was an English novelist whose adventure romances, most of them set in Africa, made him one of the best-selling writers of the late Victorian age. He is known above all for *King Solomon's Mines*, the first of his stories about the hunter Allan Quatermain, and for *She*, and he is counted among the founders of the "lost world" genre. He was also active in agricultural and land reform.

## Life

Henry Rider Haggard was born on 22 June 1856 at Bradenham, Norfolk, the eighth of ten children of William Meybohm Rider Haggard, a barrister, and Ella Doveton, an author and poet. Unlike his elder brothers, who went to private schools, he was sent first to study under a clergyman at Garsington Rectory in Oxfordshire and then to Ipswich Grammar School, since his father could no longer afford an expensive education for him. After he failed the army entrance examination he was sent to a London crammer to prepare for the Foreign Office examination, which he never sat.

In 1875, at the age of nineteen, his father sent him to South Africa as unpaid assistant to the secretary of Sir Henry Bulwer, Lieutenant-Governor of the Colony of Natal. In 1876 he joined the staff of Sir Theophilus Shepstone, Special Commissioner for the Transvaal, and in April 1877 he was in Pretoria for the proclamation of the British annexation of the Transvaal, reading out much of the proclamation himself after the official chosen for the task lost his voice. He then took a post in the High Court of the Transvaal. In these years he fell in love with Mary Elizabeth "Lilly" Jackson, but his father forbade the marriage until he had made a career, and she married another man. Haggard instead married Marianna Louisa Margitson, a Norfolk heiress and a friend of his sister, and the couple went out to Africa together, where he saw the Transvaal handed back to the Boers. They had a son, Jack, who died of measles at the age of ten, and three daughters; the youngest, Lilias, later wrote a life of her father, *The Cloak That I Left* (1951).

Haggard returned to England in 1882 and settled at his wife's family home at Ditchingham in Norfolk. He studied law and was called to the bar in 1884, but practised little. His first book, *Cetywayo and His White Neighbours* (1882), defended Shepstone's policy in South Africa, and it was followed by two novels that sold poorly, *Dawn* (1884) and *The Witch's Head* (1885). While living at 69 Gunterstone Road in Hammersmith he completed *King Solomon's Mines*, which became a best seller; he had chosen a ten per cent royalty rather than a payment of £100 for the copyright. *She* (1887) and *Allan Quatermain* (1888) followed, along with *Cleopatra* (1889), *The World's Desire* (1890), a romance of Helen of Troy written with his friend Andrew Lang, *Eric Brighteyes* (1891) and *Nada the Lily* (1892), a tale of the Zulus. He became a lifelong friend of Rudyard Kipling after Kipling arrived in London in 1889.

From the 1890s Haggard gave more and more of his time to rural questions. He farmed his Norfolk estate, wrote *A Farmer's Year* (1899), and in *Rural England* (2 vols., 1902) described the depopulation of the countryside. In 1895 he stood unsuccessfully for Parliament in East Norfolk as a Conservative, losing by 197 votes. In 1905 the Colonial Office sent him to report on the Salvation Army's farm settlements in the United States, and his report appeared in enlarged form as *The Poor and the Land* (1905). He served on many commissions on land use, work that took him on several journeys to the colonies and dominions. He was knighted in 1912 and made a Knight Commander of the Order of the British Empire in 1919. Haggard died in Marylebone, London, on 14 May 1925, and his ashes were buried at St Mary's Church, Ditchingham.

## Writing

According to his autobiography, Haggard wrote *King Solomon's Mines* after reading a glowing notice of Robert Louis Stevenson's *Treasure Island* and deciding to try "a book for boys." He drew on what he had seen in Africa: its mineral wealth, ruined cities such as Great Zimbabwe, and hunters and scouts such as Frederick Selous, on whom Allan Quatermain was largely based. Quatermain went on to appear in fourteen novels and four short stories. *She*, with its immortal queen Ayesha, "She Who Must Be Obeyed," became one of the best-selling books ever published, and the psychologist Carl Jung saw in Ayesha an image of the anima.

His stories carry many of the stereotypes of the colonial age, and the Kenyan writer Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o called him one of the "geniuses of racism." Yet they are also unusual for the sympathy shown to African characters, such as the Zulu warrior Umslopogaas and Ignosi, the rightful king in *King Solomon's Mines*. Andrew Lang praised the "gift of 'vision'" in his first great success, and Graham Greene wrote that he "fixed pictures in our minds that thirty years have been unable to wear away." His lost-world romances influenced Edgar Rice Burroughs, Arthur Conan Doyle and many later writers of adventure fiction.
