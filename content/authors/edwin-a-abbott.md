Edwin Abbott Abbott (1838–1926) was an English schoolmaster, clergyman, theologian and scholar of Shakespeare's English. He is best known today as the author of *Flatland: A Romance of Many Dimensions*, a short satirical fantasy about a world of two dimensions.

## Life

Edwin Abbott Abbott was born on 20 December 1838 in Marylebone, London, the eldest son of Edwin Abbott, headmaster of the Philological School in Marylebone, and his wife Jane, who was also her husband's first cousin. His younger brother Evelyn Abbott became a well-known tutor at Balliol College, Oxford, and a historian of Greece. Edwin was educated at the City of London School and at St John's College, Cambridge, which he entered in 1857. He took the highest honours in classics, mathematics and theology, graduating in 1861 as senior classic and Chancellor's medallist, and was elected a fellow of his college.

In 1862 he was ordained deacon, and he became a priest the following year. In 1863 he married Mary Elizabeth Rangeley of Unstone in Derbyshire; they had a son and a daughter. After teaching at King Edward's School in Birmingham and at Clifton College, he succeeded G. F. Mortimer as headmaster of the City of London School in 1865, at the early age of twenty-six. He led the school for twenty-four years and raised it to the front rank of English schools. He was said to have "a genius for teaching" and to "keep discipline without effort," and he introduced new methods of instruction and new subjects, making an elementary knowledge of chemistry compulsory throughout the upper school. Among his pupils was the future Prime Minister H. H. Asquith.

Alongside his teaching Abbott was active as a preacher and scholar. He was select preacher at Cambridge and at Oxford, received the degree of Doctor of Divinity from the Archbishop of Canterbury in 1872, and gave the Hulsean lectures at Cambridge in 1876. As a preacher he was described as "a bold and original exponent of advanced broad church doctrines." His *Shakespearian Grammar*, a study of the differences between Elizabethan and modern English written for schools, became a lasting work of English philology. He also wrote textbooks such as *Via Latina*, a first Latin book, and studies of Francis Bacon, including a life of Bacon in 1885. He published two religious romances anonymously, *Philochristus* (1878), the memoirs of an imagined disciple of Jesus, and *Onesimus* (1882), and later a third, *Silanus the Christian*.

*Flatland* appeared in 1884 under the pseudonym "A Square," and a second, revised edition followed the same year. It attracted little notice at the time.

Abbott retired from the City of London School in 1889, at the age of fifty, and devoted the rest of his life to literary and theological work. His anonymous *The Kernel and the Husk* (1886), *Philomythus* (1891) and *The Anglican Career of Cardinal Newman* (1892) set out his liberal views, and his article "The Gospels" in the ninth edition of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* caused a considerable stir in the English theological world. His later years were given to close study of the language of the Gospels, published in the ten volumes of *Diatessarica* between 1900 and 1917, among them *Johannine Vocabulary* (1905) and *Johannine Grammar* (1906), and to *The Fourfold Gospel*. He died of influenza at his home in Well Walk, Hampstead, on 12 October 1926, and was buried in Hampstead cemetery.

## Writing

Abbott's scholarly writing ranges from English grammar and Shakespeare's language to Francis Bacon and the textual study of the Gospels, and his liberal inclinations in theology shaped both his educational views and his books. His religious romances, set in the first age of the Church, were meant to stir fresh interest in reading the Gospels.

*Flatland*, his one work of fiction still widely read, is narrated by a square living in a two-dimensional world. Its first part satirises the rigid class and gender hierarchies of Victorian society, in which a man's rank depends on the number and regularity of his sides and women are mere lines; its second part uses the Square's encounters with Lineland, Pointland and a visiting Sphere to lead the reader to imagine higher dimensions by analogy. In the words of the MacTutor History of Mathematics archive, Abbott "tries to popularise the notion of multidimensional geometry but the book is also a clever satire on the social, moral, and religious values of the period." When critics accused it of misogyny, the preface to the second edition answered that the narrator had identified himself "(perhaps too closely)" with the views of his world's historians.

The book had little success at first. Interest revived after Einstein's general theory of relativity made the idea of a fourth dimension famous; a letter in *Nature* in 1920 presented Abbott almost as a prophet. Often classed as science fiction, or more precisely "mathematical fiction," *Flatland* has inspired sequels such as Dionys Burger's *Sphereland* (1957) and several films.

## In this library

- [Flatland](flatland) — A. Square, a respectable citizen of a two-dimensional world of lines and polygons, describes the laws and customs of his country and then tells how a visiting Sphere revealed to him the third dimension, a truth for which he is imprisoned. It was first published in London in 1884 by Seeley & Co. under the pseudonym "A Square," and a revised second edition appeared later that year.
