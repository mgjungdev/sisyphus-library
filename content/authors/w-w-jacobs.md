W. W. Jacobs (1863–1943) was an English writer of short stories, novels and plays. Most of his work was humorous, made up of tales of sailors ashore and the dockside and village life of England, but he is best remembered for a handful of macabre stories, above all "The Monkey's Paw."

## Life

William Wymark Jacobs was born on 8 September 1863 at 5 Crombie's Row, Mile End Old Town, in the East End of London, the son of William Gage Jacobs and his wife Sophia. His father was manager of the South Devon wharf in Lower East Smithfield, by St Katharine Docks, and the boy spent much of his time on the Thames-side, where, in the words of the *Oxford Dictionary of National Biography*, he "ran wild in Wapping." His mother died while the children were still young, and his father then married his housekeeper and had seven more children, among them the illustrator Helen Jacobs. Jacobs attended a private school in London and then the Birkbeck Literary and Scientific Institution, now Birkbeck College of the University of London, where he became friends with the writer William Pett Ridge.

In 1879 he became a clerk in the Post Office Savings Bank. By 1885 he had published his first short story, but success came slowly. His first collection, *Many Cargoes*, became a popular success, and the reviewer in *Punch* summed up his subject as "men who go down to the sea in ships of moderate tonnage." The novel *The Skipper's Wooing* followed in 1897 and the collection *Sea Urchins* in 1898. Most of the stories in *Sea Urchins* had first appeared in Jerome K. Jerome's magazine *The Idler*, and from October 1898 his work was published in *The Strand*, which gave him financial security almost to the end of his life. By 1899 he was earning enough to leave the Post Office.

In 1900 Jacobs married Agnes Eleanor Williams at West Ham, Essex; she later became a noted suffragette. They had two sons and three daughters. The family lived at Buckhurst Hill and then at Loughton in Essex, which is the "Claybury" of some of his village stories, and he also had a London home at 15 Gloucester Gate, Regent's Park. "The Monkey's Paw" appeared in *Harper's Monthly* in September 1902 and was collected the same year in *The Lady of the Barge*. Among his other books were *Light Freights* (1901), *At Sunwich Port* (1902), *Odd Craft* (1903), *Captains All* (1905), *Salthaven* (1908) and *Sailors' Knots* (1909).

His first stage work, *The Ghost of Jerry Bundler*, opened in London in 1899, and he wrote eighteen plays in all, some in collaboration with other writers. Around the time of the First World War his output of stories declined, and his later work was mainly adapting his stories for the stage. In 1914 he sat on the jury in a mock trial of John Jasper for the murder of Edwin Drood, at which G. K. Chesterton was judge and George Bernard Shaw foreman of the jury. In 1928 he took part in the filming of his works. Though he had held left-wing views when young, he later described his politics as "Conservative and Individualistic." Jacobs died at Hornsey Lane, Islington, London, on 1 September 1943. *The Times* remembered him as "quiet, gentle and modest."

## Writing

The critic Michael Sadleir said that Jacobs "wrote stories of three kinds: describing the misadventures of sailor-men ashore; celebrating the artful dodger of a slow-witted village; and tales of the macabre." The best known of his comic figures is the night-watchman on a Wapping wharf, who tells of the misadventures of his acquaintances Ginger Dick, Sam Small and Peter Russet, sailors whose pay is soon taken from them by the crafty people of dockland London and by their own credulity. Jacobs handled the vernacular of the East End with a delicacy that won the respect of P. G. Wodehouse, and John Drinkwater placed his fiction "in the Dickens tradition." Ian Hay wrote that he "invented an entirely new form of humorous narrative," whose chief qualities were "compression and understatement." Many of his stories first appeared in magazines such as *The Idler* and *The Strand* before being gathered into books.

His macabre tales, among them "The Monkey's Paw," "The Toll House" and "Jerry Bundler," are few, but they include the story for which he is chiefly remembered. "The Monkey's Paw," with its three wishes granted at a terrible price, was staged as a one-act play at the Haymarket Theatre in London in October 1903 and has since been retold many times in plays, films, radio, television and comics.

## In this library

- [The Monkey's Paw](monkeys-paw) — Mr and Mrs White and their son Herbert are given a mummified monkey's paw that will grant three wishes, and a wish for two hundred pounds is answered by Herbert's death in an accident at work and a compensation payment of exactly that sum, until Mrs White makes her husband wish their son back from the grave.
