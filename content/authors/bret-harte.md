Bret Harte (1836–1902) was an American short-story writer, poet and editor best remembered for his tales of miners, gamblers and other romantic figures of the California Gold Rush. "The Luck of Roaring Camp" and "The Outcasts of Poker Flat," first published in the *Overland Monthly*, made him famous across the United States and in Europe, and he was regarded as an early master of the short story and of local color.

## Life

Francis Brett Hart was born in Albany, New York, on 25 August. His father, Henry, who taught Greek at a college in Albany and changed the spelling of the family name to Harte, died while the boy was young; his grandfather Bernard Hart had been a merchant and one of the founders of the New York Stock Exchange. Later he preferred to be known by his middle name, spelled with a single "t." An avid reader, he published a satirical poem at the age of eleven, and his family's ridicule of it stung him for years. His formal schooling ended when he was thirteen.

In his teens he went with his mother to California, drawn by the gold excitement, and worked there in turn as a teacher, a miner, a Wells Fargo messenger guarding treasure boxes on stagecoaches, a printer and a journalist. In the coastal town of Union, now Arcata, on Humboldt Bay, he was a tutor and then a printer's devil and assistant editor on the *Northern Californian*. After white settlers massacred Wiyot Indians at Tuluwat in February 1860, he used a period in charge of the paper to publish an account condemning the killings; his life was threatened, and about a month later he fled to San Francisco.

There he joined *The Golden Era*, where he had already published poems and prose sketches, and wrote as "The Bohemian." On 11 August 1862 he married Anna Griswold in San Rafael; the marriage was troubled from the start. *The Atlantic Monthly* published his first short story in October 1863, and in 1864 he helped start a new journal, *The Californian*, where his parodies of famous novelists appeared, later collected as *Condensed Novels* (1867). He also held a post as secretary of the United States branch mint in San Francisco, and he befriended and encouraged younger writers such as the poet Ina Coolbrith.

In 1868 Harte became editor of the new *Overland Monthly*. "The Luck of Roaring Camp" appeared in its second issue, in August 1868, and "The Outcasts of Poker Flat" followed in January 1869; together they brought him national and European fame. His comic poem "Plain Language from Truthful James," published in September 1870 and better known as "The Heathen Chinee," was meant to win sympathy for a Chinese immigrant, but many readers sided with his attacker, and it was reprinted across the country. In 1871 Harte moved east with his family and signed a contract with the publisher of *The Atlantic Monthly* for $10,000 a year, an unprecedented sum.

His popularity soon waned. By the end of 1872 he had no publishing contract, and he spent several hard years lecturing on the Gold Rush and struggling to place new work; he also quarreled bitterly with Mark Twain, who had once been a friend. In 1878 he accepted the post of United States consul at Krefeld, in Germany, and in 1880 he was moved to Glasgow. From 1885 he lived in London and wrote steadily for the rest of his life, still mostly about California. He sent money to his wife and children every month but never brought them to Europe or returned to see them; the couple lived together only sixteen of their forty years of marriage. He died of throat cancer in England in May 1902 and was buried at Frimley.

## Writing

Harte published forty-four volumes of stories, sketches, novels and verse between 1867 and 1898, but his early California tales remained his best known and most often reprinted. They combine humor and pathos in portraits of gamblers, prostitutes, drunkards and rough miners who prove capable of sudden tenderness or sacrifice, set against the mining gulches and picturesque landscapes of the gold country. His characters are drawn with a few strokes and revealed through speech and action rather than long analysis. The 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* called him "a sort of later Fenimore Cooper," the best delineator of the new land of the mining camps. *The Luck of Roaring Camp, and Other Sketches* was later named to the Zamorano 80, a list of distinguished books on California.

Critics in his own time and later observed that he cast an idealizing glamour over the camps, and Mark Twain, after their quarrel, attacked his miners' dialect as an invention. His later work was often thought to repeat his early themes. Yet his stories were adapted many times for stage and screen, "The Outcasts of Poker Flat" alone being filmed at least five times, and Rudyard Kipling told a reporter that San Francisco was "hallowed ground" to him "because of Bret Harte." His parodies, including the Sherlock Holmes burlesque "The Stolen Cigar-Case," were also widely praised.

## In this library

- [The Outcasts of Poker Flat](outcasts-of-poker-flat) — Expelled from a California mining town in November 1850, the gambler John Oakhurst, two disreputable women and a drunken thief are joined on the mountain trail by a pair of innocent young lovers, and a snowstorm traps them all in a half-built cabin. It was first published in the *Overland Monthly* in January 1869 and collected in *The Luck of Roaring Camp, and Other Sketches* (1870).
- [The Luck of Roaring Camp](luck-of-roaring-camp) — When Cherokee Sal, the only woman in a rough gold-mining camp, dies giving birth, the miners raise her baby boy themselves, name him Thomas Luck and reform their ways for his sake. It appeared in the second issue of the *Overland Monthly* in August 1868 and became the title story of a collection published by Fields, Osgood & Co. in April 1870.
