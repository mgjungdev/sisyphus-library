Edgar Allan Poe (1809–1849) was an American poet, short story writer, editor and literary critic. He is best known for his tales of mystery and the macabre and for poems such as "The Raven," and he is generally considered one of the pioneers of detective fiction.

## Life

Edgar Poe was born in Boston, Massachusetts, on January 19, 1809, the son of the actor David Poe and the English-born actress Eliza Poe, née Arnold. His mother died in 1811, and the small boy was taken into the home of John Allan, a merchant of Richmond, Virginia, and his wife Frances. The Allans gave him the name "Edgar Allan Poe" but never formally adopted him. In 1815 the family sailed for Britain, where Poe went to school in Scotland and in London, and then to the Reverend John Bransby's school at Stoke Newington. They returned to Richmond in 1820.

In 1826 Poe entered the University of Virginia at Charlottesville. There he ran up gambling debts and quarrelled with his foster father over money, and he left after a year. In 1827 he went to Boston, where his first book, *Tamerlane and Other Poems*, appeared under the name "A Bostonian" and received almost no attention. In May of that year he enlisted in the United States Army under the name "Edgar A. Perry," and he served for two years, reaching the rank of sergeant major. Frances Allan died in 1829, and John Allan helped him to an appointment to the United States Military Academy at West Point. A second collection, *Al Aaraaf, Tamerlane and Minor Poems*, was published in Baltimore in 1829. At West Point Poe neglected his duties, and in 1831 he was court-martialled and dismissed. That year a further volume, *Poems*, came out in New York.

Poe then lived in Baltimore with his aunt Maria Clemm and her daughter Virginia. In 1833 his story "MS. Found in a Bottle" won a prize offered by the Baltimore *Saturday Visiter*, which brought him to the notice of editors. He joined the *Southern Literary Messenger* in Richmond in 1835 and stayed with it until January 1837, writing poems, stories and a great many reviews. In the mid-1830s he married his young cousin Virginia Clemm.

In 1838 Poe moved to Philadelphia. His only novel, *The Narrative of Arthur Gordon Pym of Nantucket*, was published that year. In 1839 he became assistant editor of *Burton's Gentleman's Magazine*, and *Tales of the Grotesque and Arabesque* appeared in two volumes. He then worked as an editor at *Graham's Magazine*, where he published "The Murders in the Rue Morgue" in 1841. For years he planned a magazine of his own, first called *The Penn* and later *The Stylus*. In January 1842 Virginia showed the first signs of tuberculosis. Poe moved to New York, worked on the *Evening Mirror*, and became editor and then owner of the *Broadway Journal*. "The Raven," printed in the *Evening Mirror* on January 29, 1845, made him famous almost at once. The *Broadway Journal* failed in 1846, and the family moved to a cottage at Fordham, in what is now the Bronx, where Virginia died on January 30, 1847.

After her death Poe courted the poet Sarah Helen Whitman of Providence, Rhode Island, but their engagement was broken off. He returned to Richmond and renewed his attachment to Sarah Elmira Royster, his sweetheart of his youth, who had since married Alexander Shelton. On October 3, 1849, he was found in Baltimore in great distress, and he died in hospital there on October 7, 1849. The cause of his death has never been established. His rival Rufus Wilmot Griswold wrote a hostile obituary and a memoir, prefixed to the collected works of 1850, that long shaped Poe's reputation.

## Writing

Poe made a living by his pen alone, one of the first American writers to do so, and most of what he wrote was journalism and criticism. His best-known fiction belongs to Gothic horror. Death, decay, premature burial and the return of the dead recur through the tales, which are often narrated by murderers or men on the edge of madness. He also wrote satires, hoaxes and stories that anticipate science fiction, and his interest in cryptography produced "The Gold-Bug," the most widely read of his prose works during his lifetime. In his critical essays he argued against didacticism and held that a work should aim at a single effect, to which every detail must contribute. In "The Philosophy of Composition" he described how he had built "The Raven."

In his lifetime he was known chiefly as a critic. James Russell Lowell called him "the most discriminating, philosophical, and fearless critic upon imaginative works who has written in America," and his severe reviews earned him the name of "tomahawk man." He accused Longfellow of plagiarism. Ralph Waldo Emerson said of "The Raven," "I see nothing in it."

The three stories of C. Auguste Dupin set the pattern for later fictional detectives. Arthur Conan Doyle asked, "Where was the detective story until Poe breathed the breath of life into it?" Poe was esteemed in France through the translations of Charles Baudelaire, his novel *Pym* influenced Jules Verne, and H. P. Lovecraft called him his "God of Fiction." The Mystery Writers of America named their annual awards the Edgars after him.

## In this library

- [The Fall of the House of Usher](fall-of-the-house-of-usher) — A narrator visits his boyhood friend Roderick Usher in a decaying mansion, where Roderick's twin sister Madeline is buried alive and the house finally collapses on the last of the family. First published in *Burton's Gentleman's Magazine* in September 1839 and collected in *Tales of the Grotesque and Arabesque*.
- [The Murders in the Rue Morgue](murders-in-the-rue-morgue) — In Paris, C. Auguste Dupin solves the brutal murder of a mother and daughter, whose killer the witnesses heard but could not identify. It first appeared in *Graham's Magazine* in April 1841.
- [A Descent into the Maelström](descent-into-the-maelstrom) — On a mountain in Lofoten, Norway, an old man tells how his fishing boat was drawn into a great whirlpool and how close observation of the vortex saved him. It first appeared in the May 1841 issue of *Graham's Magazine*.
- [The Masque of the Red Death](masque-of-the-red-death) — Prince Prospero shuts himself and a thousand nobles in an abbey against a plague and holds a masked ball in seven coloured rooms, until a masked stranger appears. First published in *Graham's Magazine* in May 1842 as "The Mask of the Red Death."
- [The Tell-Tale Heart](tell-tale-heart) — A narrator who insists on his own sanity describes how he murdered an old man for his "vulture-eye" and hid the body beneath the floor. It was first published in January 1843 in the first issue of *The Pioneer*, edited by James Russell Lowell.
- [The Black Cat](black-cat) — A man ruined by drink tortures and kills his black cat, then murders his wife and walls up her body in the cellar. It first appeared in *The Saturday Evening Post* on August 19, 1843.
- [The Gold-Bug](gold-bug) — On Sullivan's Island, South Carolina, William Legrand deciphers a secret message that leads him to buried treasure. It won a prize from the Philadelphia *Dollar Newspaper* and was published there in installments beginning in June 1843.
- [The Purloined Letter](purloined-letter) — Dupin recovers a compromising letter stolen by a minister, which the Paris police have failed to find in an exhaustive search. It first appeared in the annual *The Gift* for 1845, published in December 1844.
- [The Cask of Amontillado](cask-of-amontillado) — During Carnival in an Italian city, Montresor lures Fortunato into his family vaults with the promise of a rare wine and walls him up alive. First published in *Godey's Lady's Book* in November 1846.
