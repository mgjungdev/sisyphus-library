Anthony Hope (1863–1933), the pen name of Sir Anthony Hope Hawkins, was an English novelist and playwright. He wrote many adventure novels, society novels and plays, but he is remembered chiefly for *The Prisoner of Zenda* and its sequel *Rupert of Hentzau*, set in the imaginary kingdom of Ruritania.

## Life

Anthony Hope Hawkins was born on 9 February 1863, the second son of the Reverend E. C. Hawkins, vicar of St Bride's, Fleet Street, in London. He was educated at St John's School, Leatherhead, at Marlborough College and at Balliol College, Oxford, where he was president of the Union Society and took first-class honours in Classical Moderations in 1882 and in Literae Humaniores in 1885. He then trained for the law and was called to the bar at the Middle Temple. He served his pupillage under H. H. Asquith, the future Liberal prime minister, who thought him a promising barrister.

His early practice left him time to write, and he lived with his widowed father at St Bride's. He contributed stories and sketches to periodicals, including the *St James's Gazette*, but had to pay for the publication of his first novel, *A Man of Mark* (1890), which is set in an imaginary country, Aureataland, and anticipates *Zenda* in its mixture of political upheaval and comedy. *Father Stafford* (1891) and *Mr Witt's Widow* (1892) followed. In 1892 he stood for Parliament as the Liberal candidate for Wycombe and was not elected. In 1893 he wrote a series of witty sketches of London society for the *Westminster Gazette*, collected in 1894 as *The Dolly Dialogues*.

The idea for *The Prisoner of Zenda* came to him at the end of 1893 while he was walking in London. He finished a first draft in a month, and the book appeared in 1894. It was an immediate success, praised by Andrew Lang and Robert Louis Stevenson, and its hero, Rudolf Rassendyll, became a well-known figure. A stage version by Edward Rose opened in New York in 1895 and in London the next year, with George Alexander in the lead at the St James's Theatre. The success of the novel persuaded Hope to give up the law and live by writing. Also in 1894 came *The God in the Car*, a political novel suggested by the career of Cecil Rhodes.

Hope wrote 32 volumes of fiction and kept a large popular following. *Phroso* (1897) is an adventure set on a Greek island, and *Simon Dale* (1898) a historical novel in which Nell Gwyn appears. *Rupert of Hentzau*, the sequel to *Zenda*, was serialised in *The Pall Mall Magazine* and *McClure's Magazine* before its publication as a book in 1898. In late 1897 he made a publicity tour of the United States. His play *The Adventure of Lady Ursula* was produced in 1898, and *The King's Mirror* (1899) was a novel he counted among his best. In 1900 he was elected chairman of the committee of the Society of Authors. Later novels, such as *Quisanté* (1900), *Tristram of Blent* (1901), *Double Harness* (1904) and *A Servant of the Public* (1905), turned to contemporary society, and *Sophy of Kravonia* (1906) returned to the manner of *Zenda*.

In 1903 he married Elizabeth Somerville Sheldon of New York, and they had two sons and a daughter. During the First World War he wrote political pamphlets, some for the Ministry of Information, and he was knighted in 1918 for his work for wartime propaganda. His memoir, *Memories and Notes*, appeared in 1927. He died of throat cancer on 8 July 1933, aged seventy, at his country home, Heath Farm, at Walton-on-the-Hill in Surrey. A blue plaque marks his former house in Bedford Square, London.

## Writing

Hope's early fame rested on two very different kinds of book. *The Dolly Dialogues* are short, epigrammatic conversations in a London drawing room; the novelist A. E. W. Mason praised their "delicate wit" and said they were so truly set in the London of their day that "the social historian would be unwise to neglect them." *The Prisoner of Zenda* is a swift romance of impersonation, honour and renunciation told by its English hero. The *Encyclopaedia Britannica* of 1911 noted that it owed something to Stevenson's *Prince Otto* and that it established a fashion for what was christened "Ruritanian romance."

That fashion lasted. Ruritania gave its name to a genre of stories set in small invented kingdoms of Central Europe, followed by George Barr McCutcheon's Graustark novels and many others, and the word itself came to mean any quaint minor European country. *Zenda* has been filmed many times, most famously in 1937 with Ronald Colman. Hope's later novels grew graver and dealt with marriage, politics and society. The 1911 *Britannica* judged that his art kept "its wit and urbanity" while gaining in grasp of contemporary life, but he "never again achieved such complete artistic success as in this one book." Roger Lancelyn Green called him "a first-class amateur but only a second-class professional writer."
