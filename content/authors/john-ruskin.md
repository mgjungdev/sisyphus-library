John Ruskin (1819–1900) was an English art critic, social thinker and writer, the leading critic of art and architecture in Victorian Britain. He is known for *Modern Painters*, his defence of J. M. W. Turner, for *The Stones of Venice*, and for *Unto This Last*, his attack on the economics of his day.

## Life

John Ruskin was born on 8 February 1819 at 54 Hunter Street, Brunswick Square, in London, the only child of John James Ruskin, a sherry and wine importer and a founding partner of the firm of Ruskin, Telford and Domecq, and his wife Margaret, a devout evangelical Christian. His parents were first cousins. From 1823 the family lived at Herne Hill in south London. Ruskin was educated mostly at home by his parents and by private tutors, among them the watercolour painters Copley Fielding and J. D. Harding, and attended a school in Peckham for a time. In 1837 he took up residence at Christ Church, Oxford, where in 1839 he won the Newdigate Prize for poetry, and he took his degree in 1842.

The first volume of *Modern Painters* appeared in 1843, published by Smith, Elder and Co. under the name "A Graduate of Oxford." It was a defence of Turner against his critics and argued that the first duty of the artist was truth to nature. The work grew to five volumes, the last published in 1860. *The Seven Lamps of Architecture* (1849), the first book to bear his name, was followed by *The Stones of Venice* (1851-1853), whose chapter "The Nature of Gothic" praised the free and joyful labour of the medieval craftsman and condemned the division of labour in modern industry.

On 10 April 1848 Ruskin married Euphemia (Effie) Gray at her family's home in Perth. Some years earlier, when she was twelve, she had asked him to write a fairy story, and he had responded with *The King of the Golden River*. The marriage was never consummated, and in 1854 it was annulled; the following year Effie married the painter John Everett Millais. In 1851 Ruskin had written letters to *The Times* in defence of the young Pre-Raphaelite painters, and he went on to support Millais, William Holman Hunt, Dante Gabriel Rossetti and Edward Burne-Jones with criticism and money. After Turner's death in 1851 he served as an executor of the painter's bequest and sorted and catalogued thousands of his drawings. In the 1850s he taught drawing at the Working Men's College in London.

From about 1860 Ruskin turned increasingly to social and economic questions. *Unto This Last*, four essays printed in the *Cornhill Magazine* from August to November 1860, rejected laissez-faire economics and declared that "There is no wealth but life." The essays caused such an outcry that the editor, William Makepeace Thackeray, stopped the series. After his father's death in 1864 Ruskin used his inheritance to support Octavia Hill's housing scheme and other practical ventures. In 1869 he became the first Slade Professor of Fine Art at Oxford. From 1871 he issued *Fors Clavigera*, a series of monthly letters "to the workmen and labourers of Great Britain," and he founded the Guild of St George, a society meant to put its ideals into practice. In 1871 he also bought Brantwood, a house on Coniston Water in the Lake District, which remained his home for the rest of his life.

His later years were darkened by grief and illness. Rose La Touche, whom he had loved since she was a child, died in 1875. In 1878 he suffered his first serious attack of mental illness, and in the same year the painter James McNeill Whistler sued him for libel over a review of *Nocturne in Black and Gold: The Falling Rocket*; Whistler won but was awarded only a farthing in damages. Ruskin resigned his professorship in 1879, held it again from 1883, and resigned for good in 1884. Between 1885 and 1889 he wrote his autobiography, *Praeterita*, which was left unfinished as his attacks grew more frequent. He died of influenza at Brantwood on 20 January 1900 and, as he had wished, was buried in the churchyard at Coniston.

## Writing

Ruskin wrote across an unusually wide range: art criticism, architecture, geology and botany, mythology, education, economics and autobiography. His early books taught a generation to look closely at nature and at paintings, and his insistence that art is bound up with the moral health of the society that makes it ran through all his later work. "The Nature of Gothic" was reprinted by Christian socialists and by William Morris, and it became a founding text of the Arts and Crafts movement.

*Unto This Last* was attacked by the press on its first appearance, but it was praised by Thomas Carlyle and later influenced the economist John A. Hobson, founders of the British Labour Party, and Mahatma Gandhi, who praised it in Gujarati. Ruskin's reputation declined in the decades after his death, but since the 1960s his work has been widely studied again, and his ideas have been read as anticipating modern concern with the environment, sustainability and craft.

*The King of the Golden River* is an early and unusual work: a Christian moral fable set in an Alpine valley, in which goodness and generosity are rewarded and greed is punished. It became an early Victorian classic, sold out three editions, and remains the most translated of all his writings.
