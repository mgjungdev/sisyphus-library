Jonathan Swift (1667–1745) was an Irish satirist, essayist, poet and Anglican cleric who served for more than thirty years as dean of St Patrick's Cathedral, Dublin. He is regarded as one of the foremost prose satirists in the English language, and is best known for *Gulliver's Travels*, *A Tale of a Tub* and *A Modest Proposal*.

## Life

Jonathan Swift was born in Dublin on 30 November 1667, a few months after the death of his father, also Jonathan Swift, a lawyer of English family; his mother, Abigail Erick, came from Leicestershire. As an infant he was taken to Whitehaven in Cumberland by his nurse and brought back to Ireland a few years later. His education was paid for by his uncle Godwin Swift. At six he was sent to Kilkenny School, and in 1682 he entered Trinity College, Dublin, where he was an undistinguished student and received his degree in 1686 "by special grace."

The political troubles that followed the Revolution of 1688 drove him to England, where he became secretary to the retired diplomat Sir William Temple at Moor Park, near Farnham in Surrey. There he met Esther Johnson, then a girl of eight, whom he taught to write and whom he called "Stella"; their close and puzzling friendship lasted until her death. At Moor Park he also first suffered the fits of giddiness, now thought to have been Ménière's disease, that troubled him all his life. He took an M.A. at Oxford, was ordained in the Church of Ireland and spent an unhappy period as prebendary of Kilroot, near Belfast, before returning to Temple, with whom he stayed until Temple's death in January 1699. *The Battle of the Books*, written in this period in defence of Temple's side of a learned quarrel about ancient and modern learning, was published in 1704 together with *A Tale of a Tub*, a satire on religious divisions.

Swift then became chaplain to the Earl of Berkeley in Ireland and vicar of Laracor in County Meath, while making frequent and long visits to London. Esther Johnson settled in Ireland with her companion Rebecca Dingley. In London he grew close to Alexander Pope, John Gay and John Arbuthnot, with whom he formed the Scriblerus Club. At first a Whig, he went over to the Tories when they came to power in 1710, edited their paper *The Examiner* and wrote *The Conduct of the Allies* (1711) against the war with France. His letters to Stella from these years were later published as the *Journal to Stella*. In London he also became involved with Esther Vanhomrigh, the "Vanessa" of his poem *Cadenus and Vanessa*. His hopes of a church appointment in England came to nothing, partly because Queen Anne disliked *A Tale of a Tub*, and in 1713 he was made dean of St Patrick's Cathedral, Dublin. With the Queen's death in 1714 and the fall of the Tories, he withdrew to Ireland.

In Dublin Swift turned his pen to Irish causes. In *The Drapier's Letters* (1724), written in the character of a Dublin draper, he attacked a patent granted to William Wood to mint copper coins for Ireland; the government offered a reward for the author's name, but nobody betrayed him, and the patent was withdrawn in 1725. He became a popular hero in Ireland. In 1726 he visited London with the manuscript of *Gulliver's Travels*, which his friends helped him to publish anonymously that year; it was an immediate success. Esther Johnson died in January 1728, and in 1729 he published *A Modest Proposal*.

In his last years Swift wrote his own mock obituary, *Verses on the Death of Dr. Swift*, and lost many of his friends, Gay, Arbuthnot and Pope among them, to death. From about 1738 his mind and health declined, and guardians were appointed to manage his affairs. He died on 19 October 1745 and was buried in St Patrick's Cathedral beside Esther Johnson, under a Latin epitaph of his own composition, which W. B. Yeats later rendered in verse: "Savage indignation there / Cannot lacerate his breast." He left most of his fortune to found a hospital for the mentally ill in Dublin, St Patrick's Hospital, which opened in 1757.

## Writing

Swift published almost everything under pseudonyms such as Isaac Bickerstaff, M. B. Drapier and Lemuel Gulliver, or anonymously, and he put his name to only one of his writings. His prose is plain, exact and deadpan; he lets a calm and reasonable-sounding narrator propose monstrous things, so that the irony works on readers before they notice it, a manner now called "Swiftian." He mastered both the genial Horatian and the bitter Juvenalian kinds of satire, and his targets ranged from religious quarrels and scientific projectors to party politics, English rule in Ireland and human pride itself.

*A Modest Proposal* is widely held to be one of the greatest examples of sustained irony in English, and its title has become a common name for any deadpan satirical proposal. He said that he had written *Gulliver's Travels* "to vex the world rather than divert it." Readers were delighted from the start; Gay reported that it was "universally read, from the cabinet council to the nursery." Some later critics, among them Thackeray, condemned the fourth voyage as misanthropic, and the book has often been printed in shortened form for children. The 1911 *Britannica* wrote that "discipleship to Swift is recognized as one of the surest passports to a prose style," and writers including John Ruskin and George Orwell have been counted among those he influenced.

## In this library

- [A Modest Proposal](modest-proposal) — A sober-voiced projector, after describing the misery of the Irish poor, proposes that their children be sold at a year old as food for the rich, and works out the profits in careful figures before listing, as remedies not worth discussing, the real reforms Swift wanted. It was published in Dublin in 1729 by Sarah Harding under the full title *A Modest Proposal for Preventing the Children of Poor People from Being a Burthen to Their Parents or Country, and for Making Them Beneficial to the Publick*.
- [Gulliver's Travels](gullivers-travels) — Lemuel Gulliver, a ship's surgeon and later a captain, tells of his voyages to Lilliput, whose people are less than six inches tall, to the giants of Brobdingnag, to the flying island of Laputa and its neighbours, and to the land of the rational horses, the Houyhnhnms, and the brutish, human-like Yahoos. It was published anonymously in London by Benjamin Motte in two volumes in 1726 as *Travels into Several Remote Nations of the World*, with passages altered by the publisher, and a fuller text appeared in George Faulkner's Dublin edition of Swift's works in 1735.
