Katherine Mansfield (1888–1923) was a New Zealand short-story writer and critic who lived most of her adult life in England and Europe and became an important figure in the modernist movement. She is known for stories such as "Prelude," "At the Bay," "Bliss" and "The Garden Party," many of them drawn from her childhood in Wellington.

## Life

Kathleen Mansfield Beauchamp was born on 14 October 1888 in Thorndon, Wellington, the third child of Harold Beauchamp, who became chairman of the Bank of New Zealand, and his wife Annie Burnell Beauchamp. She had two elder sisters, a younger sister and a younger brother, Leslie. In 1893 the family moved to the country suburb of Karori, where she spent the happiest years of her childhood, and they returned to Wellington in 1898. She went to school in Karori and at Wellington Girls' College, and her first stories appeared in school magazines. She was also an accomplished cellist.

In 1903 she went to London with her sisters to study at Queen's College, where she edited the college newspaper and met Ida Baker, who became a lifelong friend. After travelling in Europe she returned to New Zealand and began to write in earnest, publishing sketches in the Australian *Native Companion*, her first paid work, under the name K. Mansfield. Weary of provincial life, she left for London again in 1908, with an allowance from her father that continued for the rest of her life. In 1909 she became pregnant by the musician Garnet Trowell; when their relationship ended she married George Bowden, a singing teacher, on 2 March 1909, but left him the same evening. Her mother sent her to the spa town of Bad Wörishofen in Bavaria, where she miscarried. In Bavaria she first read Anton Chekhov, and her experiences there became the basis of her first collection, *In a German Pension* (1911).

Back in London from January 1910, she wrote for A. R. Orage's magazine *The New Age*. Through the avant-garde magazine *Rhythm* she met its editor, John Middleton Murry, who accepted her story "The Woman at the Store." They began a relationship in 1911 that, with several separations, lasted the rest of her life. In October 1915 her brother Leslie was killed in a grenade training accident in the Ypres Salient, aged 21, and his death turned her toward memories of their childhood in New Zealand. Virginia and Leonard Woolf asked her for a story for their new Hogarth Press, and she gave them "Prelude," begun in 1915 as "The Aloe," which they published in 1918.

In December 1917 Mansfield was diagnosed with pulmonary tuberculosis. Refusing to enter a sanatorium, which would have cut her off from writing, she spent the following winters in the south of France and Italy. Her divorce from Bowden was finalised in 1918 and she married Murry, who in 1919 became editor of *The Athenaeum*, for which she wrote more than a hundred book reviews. Her collection *Bliss* appeared in 1920. In 1921 and early 1922 she lived with Murry at a chalet in Montana in Switzerland, one of the most productive periods of her life; "At the Bay," "The Doll's House" and "The Garden Party" were written there. *The Garden Party and Other Stories* was published in 1922.

Searching for a cure, she underwent an expensive X-ray treatment in Paris without success, and on 16 October 1922 she moved to G. I. Gurdjieff's Institute for the Harmonious Development of Man at Fontainebleau, where she lived as a guest. She died there of a pulmonary haemorrhage on 9 January 1923, aged 34, and was buried at Avon, near Fontainebleau. Murry edited and published much of her unpublished work, including two further volumes of stories, *The Doves' Nest* (1923) and *Something Childish* (1924), poems, and her letters and journals.

## Writing

Mansfield worked almost entirely in the short story and wrote with great care for the rhythm of her prose. She said that she chose "not only the length of every sentence, but even the sound of every sentence," and that there must not be "one single word out of place." In a journal entry of 1916 she wrote that "the plots of my stories leave me perfectly cold." Her stories have little conventional plot; instead they present characters from within, moving between their points of view and catching a single day or moment in their lives, often one that brings a flash of insight that is never fully put into words. Small objects often carry symbolic weight, as the dead father's stick and top hat do in "The Daughters of the Late Colonel." In "The Garden Party" a single day takes a girl from the preparations for a party to her first sight of death.

Her stories have been regarded as the first in English to show the influence of Chekhov, and she is counted among the chief innovators of the modernist short story. Many of her characters are isolated or divided within themselves, and several stories turn on differences of class. The New Zealand stories of her later years, such as "Prelude," "At the Bay" and "The Garden Party," return to the houses and family of her childhood, which she never saw again after leaving for Europe.

## In this library

- [The Garden Party](garden-party) — On the day of the wealthy Sheridan family's garden party, young Laura learns that a poor carter who lived in the cottages below their house has been killed, and after the party she carries a basket of leftovers to his widow and sees the dead man.
