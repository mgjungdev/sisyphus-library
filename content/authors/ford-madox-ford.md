Ford Madox Ford (1873–1939) was an English novelist, poet, critic and editor, who wrote for much of his career under the name Ford Madox Hueffer. His magazines *The English Review* and *The Transatlantic Review* published many of the leading writers of the early twentieth century. He is remembered above all for *The Good Soldier*, the *Parade's End* tetralogy and *The Fifth Queen* trilogy.

## Life

He was born Ford Hermann Hueffer on 17 December 1873 in Merton, Surrey, the eldest of three children of Francis Hueffer, a German-born music critic for *The Times*, and Catherine Madox Brown. He was named after his maternal grandfather, the Pre-Raphaelite painter Ford Madox Brown, whose biography he later wrote; William Michael Rossetti was his uncle by marriage. After his father's death in 1889 he and his brother Oliver went to live with their grandfather in London. He attended University College School but never went to university, and in 1892, at eighteen, he became a Catholic.

In 1894 he eloped with Elsie Martindale, a girlfriend from his school days; they married in Gloucester and settled in Kent, and later moved to Winchelsea in Sussex. They had two daughters. Among their neighbours were Joseph Conrad, Stephen Crane, W. H. Hudson, H. G. Wells and, in nearby Rye, Henry James. With Conrad he wrote the novels *The Inheritors* (1901) and *Romance* (1903). His trilogy of historical novels about Katharine Howard, *The Fifth Queen* (1906–1908), was called by Conrad "the swan song of historical romance." Money troubles and an unhappy marriage brought on an agoraphobic breakdown in 1904, for which he went to Germany for treatment.

In 1908 he founded *The English Review*, which printed work by Thomas Hardy, Henry James, Conrad, Wells, John Galsworthy and W. B. Yeats, and gave early chances to Ezra Pound, Wyndham Lewis and D. H. Lawrence. In 1909 he left his wife to live with the writer Violet Hunt. Elsie refused a divorce, and when an illustrated paper referred to Hunt as "Mrs. Ford Madox Hueffer," Elsie sued for libel and won in 1913.

*The Good Soldier* appeared in 1915. After the outbreak of the First World War Ford wrote two propaganda books for the government's War Propaganda Bureau, and on 30 July 1915, at the age of forty-one, he enlisted in the Welsh Regiment and was sent to France. His poem "Antwerp" (1915) was praised by T. S. Eliot as "the only good poem I have met with on the subject of the war." In 1919 he changed his name to Ford Madox Ford, partly, he said, "because a Teutonic name is in these days disagreeable." His relationship with Violet Hunt did not survive the war, and from 1918 to 1927 he lived with the Australian painter Stella Bowen, with whom he had a daughter in 1920.

In Paris in 1924 he founded *The Transatlantic Review*, which published James Joyce, Ernest Hemingway, Gertrude Stein, Pound and Jean Rhys; Basil Bunting was his assistant. Hemingway later described him in his memoir *A Moveable Feast* as "upright as an ambulatory, well clothed, up-ended hogshead." His experience of the war shaped the four novels of *Parade's End*: *Some Do Not...* (1924), *No More Parades* (1925), *A Man Could Stand Up* (1926) and *Last Post* (1928). He also wrote criticism and memoirs, among them *The English Novel* (1929). In the 1930s his companion was the Polish-born painter Janice Biala, who illustrated several of his books. In the United States he befriended Allen Tate, Caroline Gordon, Katherine Anne Porter and the young Robert Lowell, and in his last years he taught at Olivet College in Michigan. He fell ill in Honfleur in June 1939 and died shortly afterwards at Deauville, France, on 26 June 1939.

## Writing

Ford wrote dozens of novels as well as poetry, memoirs, essays and criticism. Besides his modern novels he wrote historical fiction such as *The Fifth Queen* trilogy and a time-travel novel, *Ladies Whose Bright Eyes* (1911), in the manner of Mark Twain's *A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur's Court*. He was a pioneer of literary impressionism, in which a story is told not in chronological order but through a narrator's shifting memories and impressions. *The Good Soldier* is the fullest example, an account of adultery and ruin told through flashbacks by a narrator whose understanding of events cannot be trusted. Ford called himself a "Tory mad about historic continuity" and held that a novelist should be the historian of his own time, an aim realised in *Parade's End*, set in England and on the Western Front before, during and after the First World War.

As an editor he was a lifelong champion of new writing and literary experiment, and Pound and other modernist poets valued his own verse for its treatment of modern subjects in everyday language. As a critic he advised readers to "open the book to page ninety-nine and read, and the quality of the whole will be revealed to you." Anthony Burgess called him the "greatest British novelist" of the twentieth century, and Graham Greene and Julian Barnes have been among his admirers. *The Good Soldier* is included in the Modern Library's list of the hundred best English-language novels of the twentieth century.
