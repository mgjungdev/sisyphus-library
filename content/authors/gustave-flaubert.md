Gustave Flaubert (1821–1880) was a French novelist, regarded as the leading exponent of literary realism in France. He is known above all for his first published novel, *Madame Bovary*, for his letters, and for his scrupulous devotion to style.

## Life

Gustave Flaubert was born in Rouen on 12 December 1821, the second son of Achille-Cléophas Flaubert, director and senior surgeon of the city's main hospital, and Anne Justine Caroline Fleuriot. Many traits of his father are said to be reproduced in the character of Charles Bovary. He began writing early and was educated at the lycée in Rouen, which he did not leave until 1840, when he went to Paris to study law. He was an indifferent student and disliked the city, though he made some acquaintances there, among them Victor Hugo. Toward the end of 1840 he travelled in the Pyrenees and Corsica.

In 1846 he gave up both Paris and the law. That year, after the deaths of his father and his sister Caroline, he made a home with his mother at Croisset, on the Seine close to Rouen, and the house there remained his home for the rest of his life, apart from occasional visits to Paris and England. From 1846 to 1854 he had a relationship with the poet Louise Colet, and their letters survive; it was the only serious love affair of his life, and he never married. His closest friend at this time was Maxime Du Camp, with whom he travelled in Brittany in 1846 and through the East in 1849–1850, visiting Greece and Egypt and spending five weeks in Istanbul.

His first finished work was the novella *November* (1842), which was not published until 1928. In September 1849 he completed a first version of *The Temptation of Saint Anthony* and read it aloud to Du Camp and the poet Louis Bouilhet over four days; they told him to throw it in the fire and to write about everyday life instead. On his return from the East in 1850 he began *Madame Bovary*, on which he worked for years. When it appeared in the *Revue de Paris*, the government prosecuted the publisher and the author for offences against public morals; both were acquitted in February 1857, and the novel was published as a book that April, meeting with a warm reception.

In 1858 Flaubert visited Carthage to gather material for *Salammbô*, which after four years of work he finished in 1862. *L'Éducation sentimentale* (*Sentimental Education*), drawing on his own youth and conceived as "the moral history of the men of my generation," took seven years and was published in 1869; it was his last completed novel. He was close to his niece Caroline Commanville and had a long friendship and correspondence with George Sand, and in Paris he saw Émile Zola, Alphonse Daudet, Ivan Turgenev and the Goncourt brothers. He also acted as literary mentor to the young Guy de Maupassant.

The 1870s were hard years. Prussian soldiers occupied his house during the war of 1870, his mother died in 1872, and the business failures of his niece's husband left him in financial difficulty. He published a reworked *Temptation of Saint Anthony* in 1874, saw his play *Le Candidat* fail, and broke off his work on *Bouvard et Pécuchet* only to write *Three Tales* (1877). He died of a stroke at Croisset on 8 May 1880, aged 58, and was buried in the family vault in the cemetery of Rouen. *Bouvard et Pécuchet*, unfinished, was published in 1881.

## Writing

Flaubert's name has become a byword for the labour of style. He pursued *le mot juste*, the right word, avoided the cliché and the vague expression, and sometimes spent a week on a single page. In a letter to George Sand he said that he spent his time "trying to write harmonious sentences, avoiding assonances." He held that "an author in his book must be like God in the universe, present everywhere and visible nowhere," and the impersonal narration that follows from this became central to the modern novel. Walter Pater called him the "martyr of style." He published far less than contemporaries such as Balzac or Zola.

His work divides between the realist and the romantic. *Madame Bovary* and *Sentimental Education* are scrupulously truthful portraits of modern French life, while *Salammbô*, set in ancient Carthage, and *The Temptation of Saint Anthony* indulge his taste for the exotic and the fantastic. The publication of *Madame Bovary* was at first followed by more scandal than admiration, but by his death he was widely regarded as the most influential French realist, with a strong effect on Maupassant, Edmond de Goncourt, Daudet and Zola. His correspondence is counted among his principal works, and his lean, precise prose had a large influence on later writers such as Franz Kafka and J. M. Coetzee.

## In this library

- [Madame Bovary](madame-bovary) — Emma, the romantic wife of the dull country doctor Charles Bovary, seeks escape from provincial boredom in love affairs and in luxuries bought on credit, until her debts drive her to poison herself with arsenic. First serialised as *Madame Bovary: Mœurs de province* in the *Revue de Paris*; after Flaubert's acquittal on charges of obscenity on 7 February 1857, it was published in two volumes in April 1857.
