Susan Glaspell (1876–1948) was an American playwright, novelist and journalist, a founder with her husband George Cram Cook of the Provincetown Players. She is known for the one-act play *Trifles* and its story version, "A Jury of Her Peers," and won the Pulitzer Prize for Drama for *Alison's House*.

## Life

Susan Keating Glaspell was born on 1 July 1876 in Davenport, Iowa, the daughter of Elmer Glaspell, a hay farmer, and Alice Keating, a schoolteacher; she later often gave her year of birth as 1882. She grew up on a rural homestead near the bluffs of the Mississippi and graduated from the Davenport public schools in 1894. By eighteen she was already working as a journalist. At twenty-one she enrolled at Drake University in Des Moines, where she studied philosophy and won debating contests, and after graduating in 1899 she joined the *Des Moines Daily News*, writing a column called "The News Girl" and reporting on the state legislature and on murder trials, an unusual assignment for a woman at the time.

The most important of these cases began in December 1900, when Margaret Hossack was arrested on the charge of killing her husband, John, with an axe as he slept on their farm. Glaspell filed twenty-six stories on the case. After visiting the Hossack farmhouse her reports turned from depicting Margaret as fully capable of the crime to a more sympathetic view. Margaret Hossack was brought to trial, and after a retrial in April 1903 ended in a hung jury she was released.

At twenty-four Glaspell resigned from the paper and returned to Davenport to write fiction. Her stories appeared in *Harper's* and the *Ladies' Home Journal*, and her first novel, *The Glory of the Conquered* (1909), became a best-seller; *The New York Times* hailed "a new author of fine and notable gifts." The success allowed her to spend a year in the Latin Quarter of Paris. In 1913 she married George Cram Cook, a fellow Iowan who had taught English literature, and the couple moved into the avant-garde circles of Greenwich Village, where she became a leading member of the feminist club Heterodoxy.

In 1914 Glaspell and Cook wrote the comedy *Suppressed Desires* together, and in the summer of 1915 they founded the Provincetown Players, staging plays in a refurbished fishing wharf at Provincetown, Massachusetts. The company became the first modern American experimental theatre, and Glaspell and Eugene O'Neill were its leading dramatists. Over seven years she wrote twelve plays for it, among them *Trifles*, first performed at the Wharf Theatre on 8 August 1916 with Glaspell herself as Mrs. Hale, *The Outside* (1917), *Bernice*, and *Inheritors* and *The Verge* (both 1921).

In 1924 Cook died of glanders in Greece. She returned to Cape Cod and wrote *The Road to the Temple* (1927), a biography of her husband. From 1924 to 1932 she lived with the writer Norman Matson. Her play *Alison's House* (1930) won the Pulitzer Prize for Drama in 1931. During the Depression she directed the Midwest Bureau of the Federal Theatre Project in Chicago, beginning in 1936. She returned to the novel in her last years with *The Morning Is Near Us* (1939), *Norma Ashe* (1942) and *Judd Rankin's Daughter* (1945). She died of viral pneumonia in Provincetown on 28 July 1948.

## Writing

Glaspell's fiction and plays are often semi-autobiographical and set in her native Midwest, and they take up the social questions of her time, above all the place of women. In *Trifles*, drawn from the murder trial she had covered as a reporter, two women solve the case through their understanding of other women's lives and of domestic details that the men dismiss, and they choose justice over the letter of the law by concealing what they find. In the story version she added an ironic point about the exclusion of women from juries, which meant that no accused woman could face a jury of her peers. The play has become one of the most frequently anthologized works in American literature textbooks and an early classic of feminist drama. "A Jury of Her Peers" was later adapted for the television series *Alfred Hitchcock Presents*, and a short film made from it in 1980 was nominated for an Academy Award.

She published nine novels as well as short stories and plays, but after her death her work was largely neglected. From the late 1970s feminist critics began to reassess it, and she is now regarded as a pioneering feminist writer and has been called "the Mother of American Drama."
