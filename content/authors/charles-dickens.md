Charles Dickens (1812–1870) was an English novelist and journalist, the most popular novelist of his time. Most of his novels first appeared in monthly or weekly instalments, and characters such as Ebenezer Scrooge, Fagin, Miss Havisham and Sam Weller became part of popular culture. His fiction joined comedy and caricature to an attack on the poverty, cruelty and failing institutions of Victorian England.

## Life

Charles John Huffam Dickens was born on 7 February 1812 at Mile End Terrace, Landport, in Portsea (Portsmouth), the second of eight children of John Dickens, a clerk in the Navy Pay Office, and Elizabeth Barrow. The family moved to London and then to Chatham in Kent, where he spent the happiest years of his childhood, reading his father's small collection of novels, among them *Tom Jones* and *Robinson Crusoe*, and attending a school kept by William Giles. In 1822 his father was recalled to London, and the family settled in Camden Town.

Living beyond his means, John Dickens was committed for debt to the Marshalsea prison in Southwark in 1824. Charles, then twelve, was taken from school and sent to work at Warren's blacking warehouse at Hungerford Stairs, pasting labels on pots of boot blacking for six shillings a week, and spent Sundays with his family in the prison. A legacy to his father ended the imprisonment, and the boy was sent to Wellington House Academy in Camden Town. He worked as a junior clerk in the Gray's Inn office of the attorneys Ellis and Blackmore from May 1827 to November 1828, taught himself Gurney's shorthand, and became a freelance reporter at Doctors' Commons and then a political journalist, reporting Parliament and election campaigns for the *Morning Chronicle*.

His first story, "A Dinner at Poplar Walk," appeared in the *Monthly Magazine* in 1833, and his newspaper sketches were collected in 1836 as *Sketches by Boz*, "Boz" being a family nickname. The publishers Chapman and Hall then asked him to supply text for a monthly series of engravings by Robert Seymour; the result was *The Pickwick Papers*, begun in 1836, which became a sensation after the Cockney servant Sam Weller was introduced in the fourth number. On 2 April 1836 he married Catherine Hogarth, daughter of the editor George Hogarth; they had ten children. In November 1836 he became editor of *Bentley's Miscellany*, where *Oliver Twist* was serialised. *Nicholas Nickleby*, *The Old Curiosity Shop* and *Barnaby Rudge* followed in instalments. His friend John Forster became his adviser, first reader and later his biographer.

In January 1842 Dickens and his wife arrived in Boston for a tour of the United States and Canada, during which he spoke out on the pirating of his books in America; Soon after his return he wrote *A Christmas Carol* (1843), the first of his Christmas books, and *Martin Chuzzlewit*. He lived for a time in Italy and Switzerland, began *Dombey and Son* in 1846, and in the same year, with the heiress Angela Burdett Coutts, set up Urania Cottage, a home for "fallen women" of the working class, which he managed for ten years. *David Copperfield* (1849–50), the most autobiographical of his novels, was his own favourite.

From 1850 he was publisher, editor and a major contributor of the weekly *Household Words*, and from 1859 of its successor, *All the Year Round*. At Tavistock House he wrote *Bleak House*, *Hard Times* and *Little Dorrit*, and in 1856 he bought Gads Hill Place near Rochester, a house he had admired as a child. In 1857, acting in *The Frozen Deep*, a play written with Wilkie Collins, he met the young actress Ellen Ternan, and the next year he separated from his wife. From 1858 he made paid reading tours, which together with his journalism took up much of his last decade. *A Tale of Two Cities* (1859) and *Great Expectations* (1861) appeared in *All the Year Round*.

On 9 June 1865, returning from France with Ellen Ternan, he survived the Staplehurst railway accident in Kent, tended the injured for hours, and went back into his hanging carriage for the unfinished manuscript of *Our Mutual Friend*. He made a second reading tour of America in 1867–68. A farewell tour in Britain was cut short in April 1869 by a stroke. He began *The Mystery of Edwin Drood*, but on 8 June 1870 he suffered another stroke at Gads Hill and died there the next day, leaving the novel unfinished. He was buried in Poets' Corner in Westminster Abbey.

## Writing

Dickens learned from the picaresque novels of Smollett and Fielding, from stage melodrama, from the *Arabian Nights* and above all from Shakespeare. His style is marked by caricature, satire and verbal invention, and he gave his characters names that suggest their natures, such as Murdstone in *David Copperfield*. London itself, which he knew from long walks through its streets, runs through nearly all his books. Most of his novels were planned for publication in parts, and the cliffhanger endings of the instalments kept a mass readership waiting for the next number; he wrote that a story "has to be planned for presentation in these fragments, and yet for afterwards fusing together as an uninterrupted whole."

He was also a social critic. *Oliver Twist* exposed the workhouse and the London underworld, *Bleak House* the endless delays of the law courts, and *Hard Times* the industrial town and the philosophy of "Facts." Karl Marx wrote that Dickens "issued to the world more political and social truths than have been uttered by all the professional politicians, publicists and moralists put together."

His popularity was enormous from the start, though some reviewers of the 1850s found the later, darker novels a decline. *The Spectator* called *Bleak House* "dull and wearisome as a serial," and *Fraser's Magazine* thought *Little Dorrit* "decidedly the worst of his novels." Among writers, Tolstoy and Dostoyevsky admired him, while Henry James called him "the greatest of superficial novelists" and Oscar Wilde mocked the death of Little Nell. From the 1940s critics such as George Orwell and Edmund Wilson led a reassessment, and later criticism found much of his finest work in *Bleak House*, *Little Dorrit* and *Great Expectations*.

## In this library

- [Oliver Twist](oliver-twist) — An orphan raised in a workhouse escapes to London, falls among a gang of child pickpockets led by Fagin, and discovers the secret of his parentage. Serialised in *Bentley's Miscellany* from February 1837 to April 1839 and published in three volumes by Richard Bentley in 1838.
- [A Christmas Carol](christmas-carol) — On Christmas Eve the miser Ebenezer Scrooge is visited by the ghost of his partner Jacob Marley and by three spirits, and wakes a changed man. Published by Chapman and Hall on 19 December 1843, with illustrations by John Leech; the first edition sold out by Christmas Eve.
- [Hard Times](hard-times) — In the mill town of Coketown, Thomas Gradgrind raises his children on "nothing but Facts," and the mill owner Josiah Bounderby and the mill hand Stephen Blackpool are drawn into the consequences. Serialised in twenty weekly parts in *Household Words* from 1 April to 12 August 1854.
- [A Tale of Two Cities](tale-of-two-cities) — Doctor Manette, released after eighteen years in the Bastille, lives in London with his daughter Lucie, and the lives of Charles Darnay and Sydney Carton are caught up in the French Revolution. Published in 31 weekly instalments in *All the Year Round* from 30 April to 26 November 1859.
- [Great Expectations](great-expectations) — The orphan Pip, raised by his sister and the blacksmith Joe Gargery on the Kent marshes, learns that an anonymous patron will make him a gentleman and goes to London to become a gentleman. Serialised in *All the Year Round* from December 1860 to August 1861 and published in three volumes by Chapman and Hall in 1861.
- [The Signal-Man](signal-man) — A railway signalman in a lonely cutting tells a visitor of a spectre at the tunnel mouth whose appearances have each been followed by disaster on the line. First published in *Mugby Junction*, the 1866 Christmas number of *All the Year Round*.
