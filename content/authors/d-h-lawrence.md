D. H. Lawrence (1885–1930) was an English novelist, short-story writer, poet, playwright, critic, travel writer and painter, the son of a Nottinghamshire coal miner. His fiction explores industrial society, the relations between men and women, and the claims of the body and instinct against the mind. He is best known for the novels *Sons and Lovers*, *The Rainbow*, *Women in Love* and *Lady Chatterley's Lover*, all of which met with charges of obscenity.

## Life

David Herbert Lawrence was born on 11 September 1885 at 8a Victoria Street in Eastwood, Nottinghamshire, the fourth child of Arthur John Lawrence, a barely literate miner at Brinsley Colliery, and Lydia Lawrence, née Beardsall, a former pupil-teacher who had been forced by her family's poverty to work in a lace factory. The tensions between his parents later gave him the material for his early fiction. His father wanted his sons to go down the pit, but his mother was determined that they should not do manual work, and in Lawrence's case she prevailed. He roamed the countryside around Eastwood, the remnants of Sherwood Forest, which he called "the country of my heart." In 1898 he became the first local boy to win a county council scholarship to Nottingham High School. After leaving school in 1901 he worked briefly as a clerk in a surgical appliances factory until pneumonia ended the job. While convalescing he often visited Haggs Farm, home of the Chambers family, where he formed a close friendship with Jessie Chambers, who shared his love of books.

From 1902 to 1906 he was a pupil-teacher in Eastwood, and in 1908 he took a teaching certificate at University College, Nottingham. That autumn he moved to London to teach at Davidson Road School in Croydon. Jessie Chambers sent some of his poems to Ford Madox Hueffer, editor of *The English Review*, who published them and commissioned the story "Odour of Chrysanthemums"; its appearance led the publisher Heinemann to ask for more of his work. His mother died of cancer in 1910, shortly after the final proofs of his first novel, *The White Peacock*, appeared, and he called the months that followed his "sick year." In 1911 he met the publisher's reader Edward Garnett, who became his mentor, and began revising "Paul Morel," the novel that became *Sons and Lovers*. After another attack of pneumonia late in 1911 he gave up teaching to live by writing.

In March 1912 he met Frieda Weekley, née von Richthofen, the wife of his former professor Ernest Weekley and the mother of three children. The two eloped to Germany, where Lawrence was briefly arrested as a suspected British spy, and then walked across the Alps into Italy. There he finished *Sons and Lovers*, allowing Garnett to cut it substantially before its publication in 1913. Frieda obtained a divorce, and they returned to England and were married on 13 July 1914. *The Rainbow* was prosecuted for obscenity in 1915 and suppressed. During the war the couple, suspected because of Frieda's German birth and Lawrence's open contempt for militarism, lived in poverty at Zennor in Cornwall, where he finished *Women in Love*; in late 1917 they were accused of signalling to German submarines and expelled from the county under the Defence of the Realm Act.

In November 1919 Lawrence left England on what he called his "savage pilgrimage," returning only for brief visits. The Lawrences lived in Italy and Sicily, and he won the James Tait Black Memorial Prize for *The Lost Girl* (1920). In 1922 they sailed to Ceylon and Australia, where he wrote *Kangaroo*, and in September of that year reached the United States, settling near Taos, New Mexico. Mabel Dodge Luhan gave them a ranch there in exchange for the manuscript of *Sons and Lovers*. In 1923 he published *Studies in Classic American Literature*. In March 1925, in Mexico, he fell gravely ill with malaria and tuberculosis, and he returned to Europe. Near Florence he wrote *Lady Chatterley's Lover*, published privately in Florence and Paris in 1928. In 1929 police raided an exhibition of his paintings in London. He died of tuberculosis on 2 March 1930 at Vence in the south of France.

## Writing

Lawrence's novels examine what kinds of human relationship are possible in an industrial society. He wished to restore the body and the senses against what he saw as modern civilization's overemphasis on the mind, and to enable men and women "to think sex, fully, completely, honestly, and cleanly." He wrote against what he saw as modern civilization's drift "towards a greater and greater abstraction from the physical." He drew heavily on his own life, above all in *Sons and Lovers*, and, though often called a realist, used his characters to give form to his own philosophy. He also wrote almost eight hundred poems, many of them in free verse influenced by Walt Whitman, as well as travel books and criticism; his *Studies in Classic American Literature* helped revive the reputation of Herman Melville.

At his death most obituaries were hostile, but E. M. Forster called him "the greatest imaginative novelist of our generation." The critic F. R. Leavis later championed his artistic integrity and moral seriousness and placed *The Rainbow* and *Women in Love* among the major works of English fiction. The British trial of *Lady Chatterley's Lover* in 1960, which ended in a verdict of not guilty, made his work known to a much wider public. His early novels give central place to strong, independent young women such as Ursula Brangwen, yet feminist critics, notably Kate Millett, have attacked his sexual politics. He is also often counted among the finest travel writers in English, for books such as *Sea and Sardinia* and *Mornings in Mexico*.

- [Sons and Lovers](sons-and-lovers) — Gertrude Morel, a refined woman married to a rough Nottinghamshire miner, turns her love to her sons, and the younger, Paul, a budding artist, is torn between his devotion to her and his relationships with Miriam, a farmer's daughter, and Clara Dawes, a woman separated from her husband.
