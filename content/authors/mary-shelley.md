Mary Shelley (1797–1851) was an English novelist, travel writer and editor, the daughter of the philosophers William Godwin and Mary Wollstonecraft and the wife of the poet Percy Bysshe Shelley. She is best known for *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus*, which she began at nineteen during a summer near Geneva and published in 1818.

## Life

Mary Wollstonecraft Godwin was born on 30 August 1797 in Somers Town, London. Her mother, the writer and advocate of women's rights Mary Wollstonecraft, died of puerperal fever eleven days after the birth. She was raised by her father, the political philosopher and novelist William Godwin, who gave her an informal but advanced education, and from December 1801 by a stepmother, Mary Jane Clairmont, with whom she did not get on. In June 1812 she was sent to stay with the radical family of William Baxter near Dundee, in Scotland, where she spent about ten months in all; she later remembered these years as the time when her imagination took shape.

By March 1814 the young poet Percy Bysshe Shelley, then married to Harriet Shelley, was a regular visitor at her father's house. Mary was sixteen and he was twenty-one. They met in secret at her mother's grave in the churchyard of St Pancras Old Church, and on 28 July 1814, against her father's wishes, they left for France together, taking her stepsister Claire Clairmont with them. They crossed war-damaged France into Switzerland and were back in England by 13 September. A daughter born prematurely in February 1815 died soon after birth; a son, William, was born in January 1816.

In May 1816 the couple, their son and Claire travelled to Geneva to spend the summer near Lord Byron, who had with him his physician John William Polidori. In a season of constant rain the company read German ghost stories, and Byron proposed that each of them write one. After days without an idea, Mary conceived the story that became *Frankenstein*. She first meant it as a short tale; Percy urged her to expand it into a novel.

The autumn brought two deaths. Her half-sister Fanny Imlay killed herself in October 1816, and in December Harriet Shelley was found drowned in the Serpentine in London. Mary and Percy married on 30 December 1816 at St Mildred's Church, Bread Street, and Godwin was reconciled with them. *Frankenstein* appeared anonymously on 1 January 1818, with a preface by Percy and a dedication to Godwin.

In March 1818 the Shelleys left England for Italy. There they moved from city to city, and they lost both of their surviving children: Clara died in Venice in September 1818 and William in Rome in June 1819. A son, Percy Florence, born in November 1819, was the only one of her children to grow up. In Italy she wrote the novella *Matilda*, the historical novel *Valperga* and two verse dramas. On 8 July 1822 Percy Shelley sailed from Livorno with Edward Williams and was drowned when the boat failed to arrive; his body was washed ashore near Viareggio and cremated on the beach.

Mary returned to England in 1823 and lived by her pen while raising her son. Percy's father, Sir Timothy Shelley, allowed her a small income on condition that she publish no biography of his son. She wrote *The Last Man* (1826), *The Fortunes of Perkin Warbeck* (1830), *Lodore* (1835) and *Falkner* (1837), contributed lives of Italian, Spanish, Portuguese and French writers to Dionysius Lardner's *Cabinet Cyclopaedia*, and in 1831 published a revised *Frankenstein* with a new introduction on its origins. In 1839 she brought out an edition of her husband's poems with her own notes. Percy Florence was educated at Harrow and Cambridge and succeeded to the baronetcy in 1844, which eased her finances. Her travels with him produced *Rambles in Germany and Italy in 1840, 1842, and 1843* (1844). From 1839 she suffered headaches and spells of paralysis. She died in London in February 1851, aged fifty-three, and was buried at St Peter's Church, Bournemouth.

## Writing

Mary Shelley wrote novels, short stories, travel books, biography and verse drama, and her work moves between the Gothic, the historical and the domestic. A recurring concern is the fragility of the self: characters whose place in the world is overturned by an inner emotional crisis or by an event beyond nature, as when Victor Frankenstein makes a living being and then abandons it, or when the narrator of *The Last Man* survives a plague that destroys the rest of humanity. Her fiction engages the political philosophy of her parents' circle, and later readers have found in it a criticism of solitary ambition and an argument for sympathy, cooperation and domestic ties.

*Frankenstein* was received with mixed reviews. Because it appeared anonymously, with Percy's preface and a dedication to Godwin, many reviewers took it to be his. Walter Scott, writing in *Blackwood's Edinburgh Magazine*, called it "an extraordinary tale, in which the author seems to us to disclose uncommon powers of poetic imagination," while John Wilson Croker in the *Quarterly Review* dismissed it as "a tissue of horrible and disgusting absurdity." The book found a large public all the same, helped by the stage version *Presumption; or, the Fate of Frankenstein* (1823), whose success led to a second edition under her own name that year. In the 1831 introduction she recalled Byron's challenge, "We will each write a ghost story," and sent the book out again: "I bid my hideous progeny go forth and prosper." In her lifetime she was taken seriously as a writer, though reviewers often missed the political edge of her work. For more than a century afterward she was remembered chiefly for *Frankenstein* and as her husband's editor; from the 1970s, feminist criticism led a wider recovery of her novels and other writing.
