F. Marion Crawford (1854–1909) was an American novelist, born and long resident in Italy, who was one of the most widely read writers of fiction of his day. He is remembered for his many romantic novels, especially those of Italian life such as the *Saracinesca* series, and for a handful of classic ghost stories, above all "The Upper Berth."

## Life

Francis Marion Crawford was born on 2 August 1854 at Bagni di Lucca, in Tuscany, the only son of the American sculptor Thomas Crawford and Louisa Cutler Ward, a sister of the poet Julia Ward Howe. His sister Mary later became a writer as well. After his father's death in 1857 his mother married Luther Terry, and the boy spent the greater part of his youth in Rome. He studied in turn at St Paul's School in Concord, New Hampshire, at Cambridge University, at Heidelberg and at the University of Rome, where he attended lectures on Sanskrit and comparative philology. As a young man he became a Roman Catholic.

In 1879 he went to India, where he studied Sanskrit and edited the *Indian Herald* at Allahabad. There he met a jewel trader, A. M. Jacob, who became the model for the hero of his first novel. Returning to America in 1881, he studied Sanskrit for a year at Harvard and wrote for periodicals, chiefly *The Critic*, while living largely in Boston at the house of his aunt Julia Ward Howe. His family worried about how he would earn a living, and an attempt to train him as an opera singer came to nothing when the conductor George Henschel decided that he would "never be able to sing in perfect tune." His uncle Sam Ward urged him instead to write a novel based on Jacob's adventures and helped him find publishers in New York.

The result, *Mr. Isaacs* (1882), a tale of Anglo-Indian life touched with Oriental mystery, was an immediate success, and *Dr. Claudius* (1883) confirmed his promise. In 1883 he returned to Italy, where he made his permanent home. In 1884 he married Elizabeth Berdan, daughter of the Union general Hiram Berdan; they had two sons and two daughters. The family settled near Sorrento, on the Bay of Naples, in a villa he bought and remodelled, later known as Villa Crawford. His third novel, *A Roman Singer* (1884), serialised in the *Atlantic Monthly*, opened up what became his true field, the life of Italian society and its English and American visitors.

Year after year Crawford published successful novels, some forty in all, and his books sold more copies than those of any other writer of fiction of his time in England or America. *Saracinesca* (1887) began a series about a Roman noble family continued in *Sant' Ilario* (1889), *Don Orsino* (1892) and *Corleone* (1897). He also wrote histories and descriptions of Italy, among them *Ave Roma Immortalis* (1898) and *Gleanings from Venetian History* (1905), and a book on *Constantinople* (1895). His novel *A Cigarette-Maker's Romance* (1890) was successfully dramatised, and in 1902 his play *Francesca da Rimini* was produced in Paris by his friend Sarah Bernhardt.

During an American lecture tour in the winter of 1897–98, while gathering material on glass-making for his Venetian novel *Marietta* (1901), he inhaled toxic fumes at a glassworks in Colorado and suffered a serious lung injury. He continued to write, and at the end of his life was well advanced with a history of Rome in the Middle Ages. He died at his home near Sorrento on 9 April 1909, Good Friday, and his requiem was held in the neighbouring Franciscan chapel. His ghost stories were collected after his death as *Wandering Ghosts* in the United States and *Uncanny Tales* in Britain (1911).

## Writing

Crawford was above all a storyteller. Because he lived in Italy, his books stand apart from any distinctively American current in literature, and more than half of his novels are set there. His fiction, full of historical color and dramatic characters, appealed to readers who preferred romance to the realism of social problems or the subtleties of psychological analysis. In his essay *The Novel: What It Is* he defended this approach, calling the novel "a marketable commodity" whose "intention is to amuse and please, and certainly not to teach and preach." *Corleone* was the first major treatment of the Mafia in literature, and Crawford himself was fondest of *Khaled* (1891), a fantasy about a genie who becomes human. The novelist Norman Douglas said that Crawford's financial success had encouraged him to write, though he disliked Crawford's habit of breaking into his stories with first-person editorial comments.

Among his shorter works, his supernatural tales, including "The Upper Berth," "The Dead Smile," "For the Blood Is the Life" and "The Screaming Skull," became often-anthologized classics of horror. M. R. James praised "Marion Crawford and his horrid story of 'The Upper Berth'," which he judged the best in *Uncanny Tales* and said "stands high among ghost stories in general," and H. Russell Wakefield called it "the very best one" of such stories.
