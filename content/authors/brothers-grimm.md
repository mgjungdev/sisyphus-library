The Brothers Grimm, Jacob (1785–1863) and Wilhelm Grimm (1786–1859), were German scholars of language, literature and folklore who collected and published the *Kinder- und Hausmärchen* ("Children's and Household Tales"). Their collection made tales such as "Cinderella," "Hansel and Gretel," "Rapunzel" and "Snow White" known around the world, and their scholarship helped to found the study of folklore and the history of the German language.

## Life

Jacob Ludwig Carl Grimm was born on 4 January 1785 and Wilhelm Carl Grimm on 24 February 1786 at Hanau, in the landgraviate of Hesse-Kassel. Their father, Philipp Wilhelm Grimm, a lawyer, became district magistrate at Steinau in 1791, and the family lived there in the official residence until his death in January 1796. His widow and six children were left in poverty, and with help from her sister in Kassel Jacob and Wilhelm were sent in 1798 to the Lyceum Fridericianum there.

Both brothers went on to study law at the University of Marburg, Jacob first and Wilhelm a year later. The lectures of the legal historian Friedrich von Savigny awakened their love of historical research, and in Savigny's library Jacob first read old German poetry. Through Savigny's circle they came to know the Romantic writers Clemens Brentano and Achim von Arnim and the ideas of Johann Gottfried Herder about the poetry of the common people. In 1805 Jacob joined Savigny in Paris as his assistant, and on his return he took a poorly paid post in the Hessian war office to support the family. Their mother died in 1808, and that year Jacob became librarian to Jérôme Bonaparte, king of Westphalia, in Kassel. Wilhelm, weakened by a long illness, later joined him in the Kassel library.

At Brentano's request the brothers began collecting folk tales in 1807, largely from educated, town-dwelling acquaintances. The first volume of *Kinder- und Hausmärchen*, with 86 tales, appeared in 1812, and a second in 1815; *German Legends* followed in 1816–1818. Jacob also served the restored elector of Hesse as a diplomat, attending the Congress of Vienna as secretary of legation, and in 1819 he published the first part of his *Deutsche Grammatik*. A second, enlarged edition of the tales came out in the same year. In 1825 Wilhelm married Dortchen Wild, a pharmacist's daughter and childhood friend who had told them several tales; Jacob never married and lived with the couple all his life.

Disappointed in their hopes of promotion at Kassel, the brothers moved to the University of Göttingen in the Kingdom of Hanover, where Jacob became professor and librarian and Wilhelm librarian and later professor. Jacob published his *Deutsche Mythologie* there in 1835. In 1837 they were among the "Göttingen Seven," the professors who protested when King Ernest Augustus abolished the kingdom's constitution; all seven were dismissed, and Jacob was expelled from Hanover. Back in Kassel and without income, they began in 1838 their great historical dictionary of the German language, the *Deutsches Wörterbuch*.

In 1840 King Frederick William IV of Prussia invited them to Berlin, where they became members of the Academy of Sciences and could teach at the university. Jacob sat in the National Assembly after the revolutions of 1848 and published his *History of the German Language* the same year. The first volume of the dictionary appeared in 1854. Wilhelm died in Berlin on 16 December 1859, and Jacob, who continued working on the dictionary, died on 20 September 1863, leaving the dictionary unfinished.

## Writing

The brothers began the *Kinder- und Hausmärchen* as a scholarly record of traditional stories, which they saw as remnants of an old German national poetry in danger of being lost. Over seven editions between 1812 and 1857 the collection grew to more than two hundred tales. Wilhelm, who had what the 1911 *Britannica* called "a remarkable gift of story-telling," did most of the revising: he expanded the tales, smoothed their language, removed sexual elements and added moral touches, partly in answer to critics who found the first edition unsuitable for children. A small edition of fifty tales for children appeared in 1825. Although they presented the tales as German, the brothers also took in stories known in many versions across Europe, such as "Little Red Riding Hood," and some that derived from Charles Perrault's French collection of 1697.

Jacob's own reputation rests above all on his work as a philologist, in the *Deutsche Grammatik*, the *Deutsche Mythologie* and the dictionary, while Wilhelm edited medieval German texts. The tales, however, became their most famous work. They have been translated into many languages, among them a complete English version by Margaret Hunt (1884), and have been adapted in literature and film and studied from political, educational and psychological points of view. Their methods of collecting and recording oral stories set standards for the study of folklore.

## In this library

- [The Two Brothers](two-brothers) — The twin sons of a poor broom-maker eat the heart and liver of a golden bird, are cast out through the envy of their rich uncle and raised by a huntsman, and then part, each with a hare, a fox, a wolf, a bear and a lion; one kills a seven-headed dragon and wins a princess, and when a witch turns him to stone his twin comes to save him. "Die zwei Brüder" entered the *Kinder- und Hausmärchen* in the second edition of 1819 as tale number 60, the longest of the collection's magic tales, and appeared in English as "The Two Brothers" in Margaret Hunt's *Grimm's Household Tales* (1884).
