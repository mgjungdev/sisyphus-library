Frances Hodgson Burnett (1849–1924) was a British-American novelist and playwright who was born in Manchester and grew up as a writer in Tennessee. She is best known for the children's novels *Little Lord Fauntleroy*, *A Little Princess* and *The Secret Garden*, though in her day her novels for adults were also bestsellers.

## Life

Frances Eliza Hodgson was born on 24 November 1849 at 141 York Street, Cheetham, Manchester, the third of five children of Edwin Hodgson, an ironmonger from Doncaster, and his wife Eliza Boond. Her father died suddenly of a stroke on 1 September 1853, leaving the family without an income, and her mother took over the business. The family moved to a house in Salford with a large enclosed garden and later to Islington Square, on the edge of a district of severe poverty. Frances wrote stories in old notebooks, acted out scenes from *Uncle Tom's Cabin*, and told tales to her friends and cousins. The Lancashire cotton famine caused by the American Civil War ruined the business, which was sold in 1863, and her schooling ended.

In 1865 the family emigrated to the United States at the invitation of her uncle, who kept a dry goods store in Knoxville, Tennessee. His trade had declined after the war, and the Hodgsons spent their first winter in a log cabin at New Market before moving to Knoxville. To earn money Frances turned to writing. Her first story appeared in *Godey's Lady's Book* in 1868, and she was soon publishing regularly in *Scribner's Monthly*, *Peterson's Magazine* and *Harper's Bazaar*; she later called herself "a pen driving machine" in these years. Her mother died in 1870. In September 1873 she married a Knoxville neighbour and friend from the Burnett family, who later qualified as a doctor. Their son Lionel was born in 1874, and after a period in Paris, where her husband continued his medical studies and their second son, Vivian, was born, the family settled in Washington, D.C.

Her first novel, *That Lass o' Lowrie's* (1877), a story of Lancashire life, made her name. In Washington she held a weekly literary salon, and after meeting Louisa May Alcott and Mary Mapes Dodge, editor of the children's magazine *St. Nicholas*, she began to write for children. *Little Lord Fauntleroy*, whose hero was modelled on her son Vivian, was serialised in *St. Nicholas* from 1885 and published as a book in 1886; it became a bestseller in America and Britain. In 1888 she won a lawsuit in Britain over the dramatic rights to the story, and she wrote her own stage version.

From 1887 she crossed the Atlantic almost every year. In December 1890 Lionel died of consumption in Paris, a loss that plunged her into depression; afterwards she drew on Spiritualism, Christian Science and New Thought without joining any church, and in 1893 published a memoir of her own childhood, *The One I Knew the Best of All*. Her marriage had long been failing, and in 1898 she divorced her husband, a step that the press criticised. For about a decade she made her home at Great Maytham Hall in England, a manor with walled gardens. In 1900 she married Stephen Townsend, ten years her junior, who had collaborated with her on plays; the marriage was unhappy, and in 1902 she told him she would no longer live with him.

She became an American citizen in 1905, and in 1907 she returned permanently to the United States, building a house at Plandome on Long Island, where she lived for the rest of her life. *The Secret Garden* appeared in 1911 and *The Lost Prince* in 1915. She died at Plandome on 29 October 1924 and was buried in Roslyn Cemetery beside Lionel. In 1936 a memorial sculpture by Bessie Potter Vonnoh, showing two characters from *The Secret Garden*, was placed in the Conservatory Garden of Central Park.

## Writing

Burnett wrote for a living from her teens, and her output was large: magazine stories, plays, popular romances for adults and books for children. Her early reputation rested on *That Lass o' Lowrie's*, and several of her adult novels later stood high on the American bestseller lists, among them *A Lady of Quality* (1896), *The Shuttle* (1907), *T. Tembarom* (1913) and *The Head of the House of Coombe* (1922).

Her children's books won her the most lasting fame. *Little Lord Fauntleroy* set a fashion for velvet suits with lace collars and was sold alongside Fauntleroy merchandise of every kind. The story of Sara Crewe, first published as a short tale in 1888 and then staged, was rewritten as *A Little Princess* (1905). *The Secret Garden*, which grew out of her love of the gardens at Great Maytham, has kept its readers while *Fauntleroy* has lost much of its popularity. Her interest in Christian Science, Spiritualism and Theosophy made its way into her later fiction. In 1906 she began a series of four small fairy books "As Told by Queen Crosspatch," illustrated by Harrison Cady, in which the fairy queen narrates in a lively, chatty voice and speaks to the child reader directly.
