James Fenimore Cooper (1789–1851) was an American novelist and social critic whose historical romances brought him fame in America and Europe. He is known above all for the Leatherstocking Tales, five novels of the frontier featuring the scout Natty Bumppo, of which *The Last of the Mohicans* is the most famous, and he also wrote sea stories and a history of the United States Navy.

## Life

James Fenimore Cooper was born on 15 September 1789 in Burlington, New Jersey, the eleventh of twelve children of William Cooper and Elizabeth Fenimore. Shortly after his first birthday the family moved to the shore of Otsego Lake in central New York, where his father, a land developer who became a judge and a member of Congress, had founded the settlement of Cooperstown. He entered Yale at thirteen but was expelled in his third year after a series of pranks. He then went to sea as a common sailor on the merchant ship *Sterling*, which took him to England and Spain, and served as a midshipman in the United States Navy, helping to oversee the building of the brig *Oneida* on Lake Ontario. His father died in 1809, leaving an estate that soon became entangled in lawsuits.

On 1 January 1811 Cooper married Susan Augusta de Lancey, from a wealthy family that had stayed loyal to Britain in the Revolution, and left the navy. They had seven children, five of whom lived to adulthood; their daughter Susan Fenimore Cooper became a writer. In 1820, after reading a novel aloud to his wife, he decided he could write a better one, and published *Precaution* anonymously. His second novel, *The Spy* (1821), a tale of the Revolution set in Westchester County, became the first novel by an American to be a best seller both at home and abroad. *The Pioneers* (1823), the first of the Leatherstocking Tales, and the sea story *The Pilot* (1824) followed, and *The Last of the Mohicans* (1826) was written in New York City, where the family lived from 1822 to 1826. There he founded the Bread and Cheese Club, which brought together writers, artists and men of affairs.

In 1826 Cooper took his family to Europe. In Paris he published *The Prairie* and *The Red Rover*, and became a friend of the painter Samuel Morse and of the Marquis de Lafayette. In a series of letters to the Paris journal *Le National* he defended the United States against charges made in the *Revue Britannique*, and his novels *The Bravo* (1831), *The Heidenmauer* (1832) and *The Headsman* (1833) attacked aristocratic rule in Europe.

He returned to the United States in 1833 and published *A Letter to My Countrymen*, criticising American manners and politics. Books such as *Homeward Bound* and *Home as Found* (1838) and his volumes of European travel brought him into open conflict with the Whig press, and he brought a series of libel suits against Whig editors, winning most of them. From 1834 he restored his father's house, Otsego Hall, which became his permanent home. In 1839 he published his *History of the Navy of the United States of America*, and he returned to Natty Bumppo in *The Pathfinder* (1840) and *The Deerslayer* (1841). His later novels include the Littlepage trilogy, *Satanstoe*, *The Chainbearer* (both 1845) and *The Redskins* (1846), which dramatised the anti-rent disputes over long leases in the Hudson Valley, and *The Ways of the Hour* (1850), his last book.

Cooper was active in Christ Church, Cooperstown, and was confirmed in the Episcopal Church in July 1851. He died at Cooperstown on 14 September 1851, the day before his sixty-second birthday, and was buried in the churchyard of Christ Church. In February 1852 a memorial meeting in New York was held in his honour, with Daniel Webster speaking and Washington Irving and William Cullen Bryant among its chairmen.

## Writing

Cooper's lasting reputation rests on the five Leatherstocking Tales, which follow the woodsman Natty Bumppo, known as Leatherstocking, Hawkeye and the Pathfinder, and his friendship with the Delaware chief Chingachgook across the eighteenth-century frontier of New York. He was also a pioneer of the American sea story, drawing on his own years afloat, and in his social novels he set out to counter European prejudice and to foster an independent American culture.

His books were translated into nearly all the languages of Europe. Balzac admired him, Victor Hugo ranked him above Walter Scott, and readers called him "the American Scott." Critics were harsher. James Russell Lowell mocked his heroines as "all sappy as maples and flat as a prairie," and Mark Twain ridiculed his plots and style in "Fenimore Cooper's Literary Offenses" (1895). D. H. Lawrence, by contrast, called *The Deerslayer* "one of the most beautiful and perfect books in the world," and in Russia the critic Vissarion Belinsky described *The Pathfinder* as "a Shakespearean drama in the form of a novel." Leslie Fiedler judged his work outside the Leatherstocking novels dull, while more recent criticism reads all his novels as responses to the political, social and economic changes of his time. Cooper was among the first major American novelists to give Native American characters central roles, but he set them in a story of a vanishing people, contrasting the noble Chingachgook and Uncas with the vengeful Magua.
