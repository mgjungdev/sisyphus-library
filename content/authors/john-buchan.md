John Buchan (1875–1940) was a Scottish novelist, historian, publisher and Unionist politician who, as Lord Tweedsmuir, served as Governor General of Canada from 1935 until his death. He wrote more than a hundred books, but he is remembered chiefly for his thrillers, and above all for *The Thirty-Nine Steps*, the first adventure of Richard Hannay.

## Life

John Buchan was born on 26 August 1875 in York Place, Perth, the first child of John Buchan, a minister of the Free Church of Scotland, and Helen Jane Masterton. He grew up in Kirkcaldy, Fife, and spent many summer holidays with his mother's parents at Broughton in the Scottish Borders, where he came to love walking and the hill country that appears in many of his novels. After the family moved to Glasgow he attended Hutchesons' Grammar School, and at seventeen he won a scholarship to the University of Glasgow, where he studied classics under Gilbert Murray. In 1895 he went with a scholarship to Brasenose College, Oxford. There he won the Stanhope essay prize in 1897 and the Newdigate Prize for poetry the following year, became president of the Oxford Union, and published several books, including the short stories of *Grey Weather* (1899) and his first adventure novels.

Buchan was called to the bar at the Middle Temple in June 1901, and in September of that year he went to South Africa as a private secretary to Alfred Milner, the High Commissioner, at the end of the Boer War. He returned to London in 1903 and resumed work as a barrister and journalist. He then joined the publishers Thomas Nelson and Sons and became deputy editor of *The Spectator*. On 15 July 1907 he married Susan Charlotte Grosvenor, a cousin of the Duke of Westminster; they had four children. *Prester John* (1910), an adventure set in South Africa, was followed by his adoption in 1911 as Unionist candidate for Peebles and Selkirk. About this time he began to suffer from the duodenal ulcer that troubled him for the rest of his life.

When the First World War began, Buchan started a history of the war for Nelson's that ran to twenty-four volumes, and in 1915 he reported from France for *The Times*. *The Thirty-Nine Steps* appeared the same year and *Greenmantle* in 1916. In June 1916 he was attached to the Intelligence Section at British Army headquarters on the Western Front with a commission as second lieutenant. In 1917 he was made Director of Information, in charge of much of Britain's propaganda, and in early 1918 head of intelligence in the new Ministry of Information under Lord Beaverbrook.

After the war Buchan moved to Elsfield in Oxfordshire and wrote history and biography alongside his thrillers. In 1927 he was elected Member of Parliament for the Combined Scottish Universities. He was made a Companion of Honour in 1932 and served in 1933 and 1934 as the King's Lord High Commissioner to the General Assembly of the Church of Scotland. In the 1930s he spoke in Parliament against the persecution of Jews in Germany and supported the Zionist cause.

In 1935 Buchan was appointed Governor General of Canada and raised to the peerage as Baron Tweedsmuir; he was sworn in at Quebec on 2 November 1935. He travelled across the country as far as the Arctic, encouraged a distinct Canadian identity, and in 1936 approved the founding of the Governor General's Literary Awards. On 6 February 1940 he suffered a stroke and struck his head at Rideau Hall, and despite two operations by the neurosurgeon Wilder Penfield he died on 11 February 1940. After a state funeral in Ottawa his ashes were carried to Britain aboard the cruiser HMS *Orion* and buried at Elsfield.

## Writing

Buchan's output included nearly thirty novels, seven collections of short stories, and biographies of Sir Walter Scott, Augustus and Oliver Cromwell; his life of the Marquess of Montrose won the James Tait Black Memorial Prize in 1928. He called his thrillers "shockers," which he defined in the dedication of *The Thirty-Nine Steps* as romances "where the incidents defy the probabilities, and march just inside the borders of the possible." Their heroes include Richard Hannay, a mining engineer from southern Africa who appears in five novels and was partly based on Buchan's friend Edmund Ironside, and the lawyer Sir Edward Leithen, named after a tributary of the Tweed. His last novel, *Sick Heart River*, sends the dying Leithen into the Canadian wilderness.

*The Thirty-Nine Steps* is one of the earliest "man-on-the-run" thrillers, and Graham Greene wrote that its settings, pace and pursuits "were to be a pattern for adventure-writers ever since." Alfred Hitchcock's free adaptation, *The 39 Steps* (1935), made Hannay still more widely known. J. R. R. Tolkien admired his adventure stories, the military historian John Keegan called him "a writer touched by genius," and in 2015 *The Guardian* placed *The Thirty-Nine Steps* among the hundred best novels written in English. Some critics have found the social and racial attitudes in his early fiction objectionable, while others note that such remarks are often given to characters whose views the story does not endorse.

## In this library

- [The Thirty-Nine Steps](thirty-nine-steps) — In the weeks before the First World War, the mining engineer Richard Hannay finds a murdered American spy in his London flat and, wanted by the police and hunted by a German spy ring, flees across the Scottish moors to break the plot.
