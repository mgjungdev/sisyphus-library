Oscar Wilde (1854–1900) was an Irish poet, story writer, novelist, critic and playwright, the leading public figure of the aesthetic movement in late Victorian London. He is known for his comedies of society, his fairy tales and his one novel, *The Picture of Dorian Gray*, and for his imprisonment in 1895, which ended his career in England.

## Life

Oscar Fingal O'Flahertie Wills Wilde was born in Dublin in 1854, the second of three children. His father, Sir William Wilde, was a well-known eye and ear surgeon; his mother, Jane Elgee, wrote poetry and prose under the pen-name "Speranza" and was an Irish nationalist. He was taught at home until he was nine and then attended Portora Royal School at Enniskillen, where he excelled in classics. From 1871 to 1874 he studied at Trinity College, Dublin, under the classicist J. P. Mahaffy, and won the Berkeley Gold Medal in Greek.

In 1874 he went on to Magdalen College, Oxford. There he won the Newdigate Prize in 1878 for his poem "Ravenna" and took first-class honours in both Classical Moderations and Literae Humaniores. At Oxford he also made himself a spokesman of the aesthetic creed of "art for art's sake," decorating his rooms with blue china, lilies and peacock feathers and cultivating a languid manner that was widely mocked, most famously in Gilbert and Sullivan's *Patience* (1881).

After Oxford he settled in London. His *Poems* appeared in 1881 and found only a limited circle of admirers. In 1882 he spent most of the year on a lecture tour of the United States and Canada, speaking on "The English Renaissance" in art and decoration,. On 29 May 1884 he married Constance Lloyd at St James's Church, Paddington; they lived at 16 Tite Street, Chelsea, and had two sons, Cyril, born in 1885, and Vyvyan, born in 1886. From 1887 to 1889 he edited the magazine *The Woman's World*.

The years that followed were his most productive. "The Canterville Ghost" appeared in *The Court and Society Review* in 1887, and *The Happy Prince and Other Tales* in 1888. *The Picture of Dorian Gray* was published in *Lippincott's Monthly Magazine* in July 1890 and, much enlarged and with a new preface, as a book in 1891. In 1891 he also published *Lord Arthur Savile's Crime and Other Stories*, the essay collection *Intentions*, the essay "The Soul of Man under Socialism" and a second book of fairy tales, *A House of Pomegranates*. He then turned to the stage. *Lady Windermere's Fan* (1892) was his first success with a wide public, and it was followed by *A Woman of No Importance* (1893), *An Ideal Husband* (1895) and *The Importance of Being Earnest* (1895). His play *Salome*, written in French, was refused a licence for performance in England.

By then Wilde was in a close relationship with Lord Alfred Douglas, son of the Marquess of Queensberry. In 1895, after Queensberry left an insulting card for him, Wilde brought a libel action against the marquess. The case collapsed and brought to light evidence of Wilde's relationships with men. He was arrested and tried for gross indecency; the first jury could not agree, but at a second trial in May 1895 he was convicted and sentenced to two years' imprisonment with hard labour. He served the term in Pentonville, Wandsworth and Reading Gaol, where he was known as "C.3.3." In prison he wrote a long letter to Douglas, published in part in 1905 as *De Profundis*.

Released on 19 May 1897, Wilde crossed to France the same evening and never returned to Britain or Ireland. He lived abroad under the name "Sebastian Melmoth," first at Berneval in Normandy, then for some months with Douglas near Naples, and from 1898 in Paris. *The Ballad of Reading Gaol* was published in 1898 under his prison designation rather than his name. Constance died in April 1898. Wilde was received into the Catholic Church on his deathbed and died of meningitis at the Hôtel d'Alsace in Paris on 30 November 1900. He was buried at Bagneux, and in 1909 his remains were moved to Père Lachaise Cemetery.

## Writing

Wilde's writing across every form turns on the claims of beauty against those of conventional morality. His style is polished and epigrammatic, built on paradox and the inverted commonplace, and his characters often speak in finished aphorisms. The preface to *The Picture of Dorian Gray* states his creed in a string of such sentences: "There is no such thing as a moral or an immoral book. Books are well written, or badly written. That is all." It ends: "All art is quite useless." His fairy tales, by contrast, are written in a cadenced, richly ornamented prose close to the language of scripture and romance, and many of them are stories of love, sacrifice and redemption; he said that *A House of Pomegranates* was "intended neither for the British child nor the British public."

In his own time his reputation was divided. His poems drew limited notice, and *Punch* joked that "the poet is Wilde, but his poetry's tame." *The Picture of Dorian Gray* met with hostile reviews when it appeared in *Lippincott's*: the *Daily Chronicle* warned that it contained an element "which will taint every young mind that comes in contact with it," the *Scots Observer* asked why Wilde must "go grubbing in muck-heaps," and the bookseller W. H. Smith withdrew the issue from its railway bookstalls. Wilde defended the book publicly against the charge of immorality. Reviewers enjoyed the humour of "The Canterville Ghost" but faulted its shifts of tone and its caricatured Americans. His society comedies, on the other hand, were popular successes, and passages from *Dorian Gray* were read out against him at his trials in 1895.

## In this library

- [The Canterville Ghost](canterville-ghost) — A comic ghost story in which the American minister Hiram B. Otis buys a haunted English house and his family refuses to be frightened by its ghost, Sir Simon de Canterville; only the daughter, Virginia, takes pity on him and helps him find rest. It first appeared in *The Court and Society Review* in 1887.
- [The Picture of Dorian Gray](dorian-gray) — Wilde's only novel, in which a young man's portrait ages and records his sins while he himself stays young and handsome. The text here is the enlarged 1891 book version, with the Preface.
- [The Fisherman and His Soul](fisherman-and-his-soul) — A fairy tale from *A House of Pomegranates* (1891): a young fisherman cuts away his soul to win a mermaid, refuses the wisdom and riches it brings back to tempt him, and in the end is drowned beside her body.
