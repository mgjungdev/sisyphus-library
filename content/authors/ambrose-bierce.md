Ambrose Bierce (1842–1914?) was an American journalist, short-story writer, poet and satirist, and a veteran of the Civil War. He is best known for his grim war stories, above all "An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge," for his tales of horror, and for the satirical definitions of *The Devil's Dictionary*.

## Life

Ambrose Gwinnett Bierce was born on 24 June 1842 in a log cabin at Horse Cave Creek in Meigs County, Ohio, the tenth of thirteen children of Marcus Aurelius Bierce and Laura Sherwood Bierce, all of whom were given names beginning with the letter A. His parents were poor but fond of books, and they passed on to him a love of reading and writing. He grew up in Kosciusko County, Indiana, went to high school in Warsaw, and at fifteen left home to work as a printer's devil on a small abolitionist newspaper, the *Northern Indianan*. He briefly attended the Kentucky Military Institute before it closed after a fire in 1860.

When the Civil War began he enlisted in the 9th Indiana Infantry of the Union army. He fought in western Virginia in 1861, where he was praised in the newspapers for rescuing a badly wounded comrade under fire, and at the Battle of Shiloh in April 1862, an experience he later described in "What I Saw of Shiloh." In 1863 he was commissioned a first lieutenant and served on the staff of General William Babcock Hazen as a topographical engineer, mapping likely battlefields. In June 1864 he suffered a serious head wound at the Battle of Kennesaw Mountain, and in January 1865 he resigned his commission. In 1866 he joined Hazen on an expedition to inspect army posts across the Great Plains, which ended in San Francisco.

Bierce stayed in San Francisco and became a well-known contributor to, and editor of, newspapers and magazines such as *The Argonaut*, the *Overland Monthly* and *The Wasp*, where he wrote a column called "Prattle." On 25 December 1871 he married Mary Ellen "Mollie" Day; they had two sons and a daughter. From 1872 to 1875 the couple lived in England, where he wrote for the magazine *Fun* and published his first books under the pen name "Dod Grile." After returning to San Francisco he became one of the first regular columnists on William Randolph Hearst's *San Francisco Examiner*, and he remained connected with Hearst's papers until 1909. In 1896 Hearst sent him to Washington to fight a bill that would have excused the railroad companies from repaying huge government loans, and his attacks helped to defeat it.

His best-known stories were written in a rapid burst between 1888 and 1891 and collected in *Tales of Soldiers and Civilians* (1891), later reissued as *In the Midst of Life*, and *Can Such Things Be?* (1893). His private life was marked by loss. He separated from his wife in 1888, and both of his sons died young, Day by suicide in 1889 and Leigh of pneumonia in 1901. In 1899 he moved to Washington, D.C., and between 1909 and 1912 he edited his *Collected Works* in twelve volumes.

In October 1913, aged seventy-one, Bierce left Washington on a tour of his old Civil War battlefields and then crossed into Mexico, which was in the middle of a revolution. He is said to have travelled with Pancho Villa's army as an observer as far as the city of Chihuahua, and his last known letter, dated 26 December 1913, ended with the words "As to me, I leave here tomorrow for an unknown destination." He disappeared in Mexico and was never seen again, and the date of his death is unknown.

## Writing

During his lifetime Bierce was better known as a journalist than as a writer of fiction, and he was feared for his biting satire and criticism. His stories stress the inscrutability of the universe and the absurdity of death. He wrote of what he had seen in the war with grim realism in stories such as "An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge," "A Horseman in the Sky" and "Chickamauga," and his cycle of war stories has been called "the greatest anti-war document in American literature." He also helped to pioneer the psychological horror story, and H. P. Lovecraft called his fiction "grim and savage." *The Devil's Dictionary*, first published as a book in 1906 under the title *The Cynic's Word Book*, offers satirical definitions of English words that mock cant and political double-talk.

Critics have faulted him for "trick endings" and for a satire that showed little compassion for its targets, and Stephen Crane was among the few contemporaries who valued his experimental stories. William Dean Howells said, "Mr. Bierce is among our three greatest writers," to which Bierce replied, "I am sure Mr. Howells is the other two." H. L. Mencken called him "the one genuine wit that These States have ever seen," and Kurt Vonnegut considered "An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge" the "greatest American short story." His war stories influenced Crane, Ernest Hemingway and others.

## In this library

- [An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge](owl-creek-bridge) — Peyton Farquhar, an Alabama planter and Confederate sympathiser caught in a Union trap, is hanged from a railroad bridge, and the story follows his desperate escape down the creek and his long journey home to his wife. It was first published in the *San Francisco Examiner* on 13 July 1890 and collected in *Tales of Soldiers and Civilians* (1891).
