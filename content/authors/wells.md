H. G. Wells (1866–1946) was an English novelist, short-story writer, journalist and popular historian who wrote more than forty novels and dozens of short stories. He is best known for the "scientific romances" of the 1890s, among them *The Time Machine*, *The Invisible Man* and *The War of the Worlds*, which established many of the central themes of science fiction.

## Life

Herbert George Wells was born on 21 September 1866 at Atlas House, 162 High Street, Bromley, in Kent, the fourth and last child of Joseph Wells, a former gardener who kept a struggling china and sporting-goods shop and played professional cricket for Kent, and Sarah Neal, a former domestic servant. In 1874 a broken leg kept him in bed, and the books his father brought him from the local library turned him into a devoted reader. He attended Thomas Morley's Commercial Academy until 1880. In 1877 his father fractured his thigh, which ended his cricket career, and the family could no longer support itself; his mother went back into service as a lady's maid at Uppark, a country house in West Sussex, where the boy later read widely in the house library.

The family placed their sons as apprentices. After a brief spell as a pupil-teacher in Somerset and a short apprenticeship to a chemist in Midhurst, Wells spent 1880 to 1883 as a draper's apprentice at Hyde's Drapery Emporium in Southsea, working thirteen-hour days and sleeping in a dormitory. He later drew on these unhappy years for *Kipps* and *The History of Mr Polly*. In 1883 he persuaded his parents to release him and became a pupil-teacher at Midhurst Grammar School. The next year he won a scholarship to the Normal School of Science in South Kensington, London, where he studied biology under Thomas Henry Huxley. He stayed until 1887, joined the school's debating society, took an interest in the socialism of the Fabian Society and of William Morris, and helped found *The Science Schools Journal*, where he published "The Chronic Argonauts," the first germ of *The Time Machine*.

The following years were spent teaching, including a post at Henley House School in London, where A. A. Milne was among his pupils, and he took a Bachelor of Science degree from the University of London. His first book was a *Text-Book of Biology* (1893). He began writing short humorous articles for papers such as the *Pall Mall Gazette*. In 1891 he married his cousin Isabel Mary Wells; they separated in 1894, when he fell in love with a student, Amy Catherine Robbins, later known as Jane. The two moved to Woking, Surrey, in May 1895 and married that October.

*The Time Machine* appeared in 1895, and the months in Woking were among the most productive of his life. *The Island of Doctor Moreau* (1896), *The Invisible Man* (1897) and *The War of the Worlds* (1898) followed in quick succession, along with many short stories. In 1896 the couple moved to Worcester Park, and in 1901, for the sake of his health, to Spade House, the house he built at Sandgate near Folkestone. They had two sons, George Philip (1901) and Frank Richard (1903). Wells also had a daughter, Anna-Jane, with the writer Amber Reeves in 1909, and a son, Anthony West, with the novelist Rebecca West in 1914.

From 1901, with *Anticipations*, Wells turned increasingly to prophecy and social argument, and he joined the Fabian Society. His novels of ordinary English life, *Kipps* (1905), *Tono-Bungay* (1909), *Ann Veronica* (1909) and *The History of Mr Polly* (1910), were followed after the First World War by *The Outline of History* (1920), a best seller that made him rich. He visited Russia in 1914, 1920 and 1934, meeting Lenin on the second visit and interviewing Stalin on the third, and he stood unsuccessfully for Parliament as a Labour candidate in 1922 and 1923. Jane died in October 1927. As president of PEN International he oversaw the expulsion of the German PEN club in 1934, after the Nazis had burned his books in Berlin the year before. He published *Experiment in Autobiography* in 1934. Wells died on 13 August 1946, aged 79, at his home at 13 Hanover Terrace, overlooking Regent's Park in London.

## Writing

Wells wrote in nearly every form: scientific romances, comic and social novels, short stories, utopias, journalism, history, popular science and autobiography. His early fiction came from his training in biology. Evolution, degeneration and the place of humanity in a long natural history run through the books of the 1890s. The Eloi and Morlocks of *The Time Machine* project the class divisions of his own England into the far future, *The Island of Doctor Moreau* turns on vivisection and the boundary between animal and human, and *The War of the Worlds* imagines Britain suffering the kind of conquest it had imposed on others. His method was to grant one impossible premise and surround it with ordinary detail. In a 1934 introduction to his collected romances he wrote: "As soon as the magic trick has been done the whole business of the fantasy writer is to keep everything else human and real."

Contemporaries noticed the method at once. In 1898 Joseph Conrad hailed him as "O Realist of the Fantastic!" Reviewers praised *The War of the Worlds* as "the very best work he has yet produced," and a writer in *Harper's Weekly* admired how he turned "his most horrible of fancies into the language of the simplest, least startling denomination." *The Island of Doctor Moreau* met outrage instead: *The Times* called it "loathsome and repulsive," and the zoologist Peter Chalmers Mitchell, reviewing it for the *Saturday Review*, called Wells a scientific heretic, though the controversy made it his best-selling novel to that date. Israel Zangwill complained in 1895 that *The Time Machine* concerned itself with "our present discontents." Wells's later work was more openly didactic, and *The Outline of History* drew a mixed response from professional historians while selling widely. He was nominated for the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1921, 1932, 1935 and 1946.

## In this library

- [The Time Machine](time-machine) — A Victorian inventor tells his dinner guests of his journey to the year 802,701, where humanity has split into the delicate Eloi and the subterranean Morlocks. It grew out of "The Chronic Argonauts" and was Wells's first novel.
- [The Island of Doctor Moreau](island-of-doctor-moreau) — The shipwrecked Edward Prendick is left on a Pacific island where Doctor Moreau shapes animals into human-like creatures by vivisection. Wells called it "an exercise in youthful blasphemy."
- [The Invisible Man](invisible-man) — Griffin, a scientist who has made himself invisible and cannot reverse the process, arrives muffled in bandages at an inn in the Sussex village of Iping. It was serialised in *Pearson's Weekly* before appearing as a book in 1897.
- [The Star](the-star) — A wandering body strikes Neptune and becomes a burning new star that bears down on the Earth, and the story follows the catastrophe across the whole world. It first appeared in *The Graphic* in 1897.
- [The War of the Worlds](war-of-the-worlds) — An unnamed narrator in Surrey, and his brother in London, witness the Martian invasion of southern England. It was serialised in *Pearson's Magazine* in 1897 and published as a book by William Heinemann in 1898.
- [The Magic Shop](magic-shop) — A father takes his small son Gip into a magic shop in Regent Street whose tricks begin to look like real magic. It was published in *The Strand Magazine* in 1903.
- [The Country of the Blind](country-of-the-blind) — The mountaineer Nuñez falls into an isolated Andean valley whose people have been blind for generations, and finds that sight gives him no power over them. It first appeared in *The Strand Magazine* in April 1904.
- [The Door in the Wall](door-in-the-wall) — The successful politician Lionel Wallace is haunted by a door in a white wall that once led him, as a small child, into an enchanted garden, and that he keeps passing by. It was first published in the *Daily Chronicle* in 1906.
