Agatha Christie (1890–1976) was an English writer of detective fiction, the author of 66 detective novels, 14 short-story collections and the long-running play *The Mousetrap*. She is best known for her detectives Hercule Poirot and Miss Marple, and she is counted the best-selling novelist of all time.

## Life

Agatha Mary Clarissa Miller was born on 15 September 1890 at Ashfield, a villa in Torquay, Devon, the youngest of three children of Frederick Alvah Miller, an American-born gentleman of means, and his wife Clara. Her brother and sister were much older, and she spent much of her childhood alone with her pets and imaginary companions. Her mother wanted her educated at home, so her parents and sister taught her reading, writing and arithmetic, and she was reading by the age of four. She read widely, from Edith Nesbit and Lewis Carroll to Anthony Hope, Walter Scott, Charles Dickens and Alexandre Dumas. Her father died in November 1901, when she was eleven, and the family's money was by then much reduced. In 1905 she was sent to Paris to study singing and the piano, but she gave up the hope of performing professionally.

In the winter of 1907–1908 she and her mother spent three months in Cairo. At eighteen, recovering from an illness, she wrote her first short story, and other stories followed, most of them on spiritualist and supernatural themes; magazines rejected them all. Her first novel, *Snow Upon the Desert*, set in Cairo, was declined by six publishers. The novelist Eden Phillpotts, a family friend, encouraged her and introduced her to his literary agent. In October 1912 she met Archibald Christie, an officer of the Royal Artillery who soon joined the Royal Flying Corps, and they married on Christmas Eve 1914 while he was on leave from France. During the First World War she worked at the Red Cross hospital in Torquay, first as a nurse and later as a dispenser, and gained the knowledge of poisons that runs through her fiction.

She wrote her first detective novel, *The Mysterious Affair at Styles*, in 1916. Its Belgian detective, Hercule Poirot, was suggested by the Belgian refugees living in Torquay. Hodder & Stoughton and Methuen rejected the manuscript, and John Lane of The Bodley Head accepted it on condition that she change the way the solution was revealed. Her daughter Rosalind was born in August 1919. *The Secret Adversary* (1922) introduced Tommy and Tuppence, and from 1923 Poirot stories appeared in *The Sketch*. In 1922 the Christies joined a ten-month promotional tour of the British Empire, and in Hawaii they learned to surf standing up.

Her mother died in April 1926, and in August Archie asked for a divorce. On 3 December 1926 Christie disappeared from their home at Sunningdale, setting off a nationwide search; on 14 December she was found at a hotel in Harrogate, registered under the surname of her husband's lover. She never explained the episode, and her autobiography does not mention it. The divorce became final in October 1928. That year she travelled by the Orient Express to Istanbul and on to Baghdad, and on a return visit in 1930 she met the archaeologist Max Mallowan, whom she married in Edinburgh in September 1930. She went with him on his excavations in the Middle East, which supplied the settings of several novels.

In the 1930s and after she wrote many of her most famous books, among them *Murder on the Orient Express* (1934) and *And Then There Were None*. During the Second World War she lived in London and worked in the pharmacy of University College Hospital. *The Mousetrap* opened in the West End in 1952 and became the world's longest-running play. She was made a Commander of the Order of the British Empire in 1956 and a Dame in 1971, and was co-president of the Detection Club from 1958. Her last novel was *Postern of Fate* (1973). She died at her home, Winterbrook House, near Wallingford, on 12 January 1976, and was buried in the churchyard at Cholsey.

## Writing

Christie wrote during what is called the Golden Age of detective fiction, and her novels largely set its pattern: a closed circle of suspects in a country house, a village, a train, a ship or an island, a plot full of red herrings, and a final scene in which the detective gathers the survivors and names the culprit. Poison is used in more than half her novels, and her clues are often ordinary objects. The crime writer P. D. James observed that she tended to make the least likely character guilty. Poirot appeared in 33 novels and more than 50 stories, and Miss Marple, an elderly spinster who reads crime through the analogies of village life, in 12 novels and 20 stories. Under the name Mary Westmacott she also published six non-detective novels.

She has been called the "Queen of Crime." In 1955 she became the first Grand Master of the Mystery Writers of America, and in 2013 members of the Crime Writers' Association chose *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd*, whose narrator turns out to be the murderer, as the best crime novel ever written. Raymond Chandler and Julian Symons criticised the artificiality of her books, and Edmund Wilson found her prose banal and her characters superficial. Her novels have sold more than two billion copies, and she is the most translated individual author.

- [The Mysterious Affair at Styles](mysterious-affair-at-styles) — Captain Hastings, on sick leave from the Western Front, is staying at the Essex manor of Styles Court when its wealthy owner, Emily Inglethorp, is poisoned with strychnine, and he calls in his friend Hercule Poirot, a Belgian refugee in the village.
