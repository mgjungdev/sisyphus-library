Aesop is the name of a Greek storyteller said to have lived in the sixth century BCE, to whom the ancient world ascribed hundreds of short fables. Whether he really existed is uncertain, no writings of his survive, and the fables known as Aesop's were gathered and retold over many centuries by many hands. Tales such as "The Hare and the Tortoise" and "The Ants and the Grasshopper" have made his name one of the best known from antiquity.

## History

The earliest witness is the historian Herodotus, who mentions "Aesop the fable writer" in passing. He reports that Aesop was a slave of Iadmon of Samos and was put to death by the people of Delphi; when a pestilence followed, the Delphians offered compensation, which was claimed by Iadmon's grandson. Aristotle says that Aesop once defended a Samian politician in court, so he must by then have been free. Later writers added colour: Plutarch makes him an envoy of King Croesus of Lydia to Delphi and seats him at a dinner of the Seven Sages beside Solon, and he was said to have told the Athenians the fable of the frogs who asked for a king. His birthplace was claimed for Thrace, Phrygia, Samos, Sardis and other places. The classical scholar Ben Edwin Perry judged the links with Croesus and the Seven Sages to be literary fiction, and some scholars have doubted that Aesop ever lived.

Most popular stories about him come from an anonymous Greek work, *The Life of Aesop*, also called *The Aesop Romance*, written down in the early centuries of the common era and altered freely by later copyists. In it Aesop is a Phrygian slave on Samos, extremely ugly and at first unable to speak, until the goddess Isis gives him speech and a gift for storytelling. He outwits his master, the philosopher Xanthus, wins his freedom, serves as an envoy to Croesus, and is finally condemned at Delphi for his insulting fables and forced to leap to his death. A version of this *Life* was prefixed to the collection of fables made by the Byzantine monk Maximus Planudes.

Aesop probably did not write his fables down. Aristophanes has a character who learned Aesop's "absurdities" from talk at banquets, and Plato tells how Socrates, in prison, turned some of the fables he knew into verse. The first known collection was made by Demetrius of Phalerum in the fourth century BCE, in ten books for the use of orators; it is lost. Phaedrus, a freedman of the emperor Augustus, put the fables into Latin verse, Babrius put them into Greek verse, and Avianus later turned 42 of them into Latin elegiacs. In the Middle Ages, prose versions of Phaedrus, above all a collection under the name of Romulus, became the main source of Latin fable books, and in the twelfth century Marie de France adapted fables into French verse.

Fables were among the first printed books. Heinrich Steinhöwel's *Esopus*, published about 1476 with a life of Aesop and many woodcuts, was soon translated into Italian, French and English, and on 26 March 1484 William Caxton published the first English version of Aesop's fables. About 1480 the Greek collection of Planudes was printed at Milan. In the seventeenth century Jean de La Fontaine retold many of the fables in French verse. English translators included Sir Roger L'Estrange, who used the slang of his day, and Samuel Croxall, whose *Fables of Aesop and Others* (1722) added a long "application" to each fable and was reprinted into the nineteenth century. In 1867 George Fyler Townsend published his translation of three hundred fables. In the twentieth century Perry edited Babrius and Phaedrus and in 1952 compiled a numbered index of the fables by source, now known as the Perry Index.

## Tales

An Aesopic fable is a short, plainly told fiction, often only a few sentences long, in which animals, and sometimes plants or people, speak and act in ways that reveal human nature. Ancient writers on rhetoric said that fables should be brief and unaffected, useful for life and true to nature. A fable often began with a word on the occasion for telling it, and the moral was usually stated at the end. The philosopher Apollonius of Tyana is reported to have said that Aesop "made use of humble incidents to teach great truths," and that by telling stories everyone knew to be untrue he told the truth.

The morals can contradict one another, which is one reason modern scholars think Aesop cannot have been the author of all the fables credited to him: any fable without another known source tended to be given his name. Some have parallels in the literature of ancient Mesopotamia, and about a dozen are shared with the Indian Jataka tales and the *Panchatantra*. Some fables explain how things came to be, some are jokes, and some were invented to illustrate proverbs, while the titles of others, such as "The Goose that Laid the Golden Eggs" and "The Town Mouse and the Country Mouse," have themselves become proverbial.

The fables were first addressed to adults, on religious, social and political themes, but from the Renaissance onward they were used above all in teaching children. Townsend's translation helped establish the habit of ending each story with a short, pointed moral, such as "Slow but steady wins the race."
