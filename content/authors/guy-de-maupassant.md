Guy de Maupassant (1850–1893) was a French writer, celebrated as a master of the short story and associated with the naturalist school. A protégé of Gustave Flaubert, he wrote some three hundred stories, among them "Boule de Suif" and "The Necklace," as well as six novels, including *Bel-Ami* and *Pierre et Jean*.

## Life

Henri-René-Albert-Guy de Maupassant was born on 5 August 1850 at the Château de Miromesnil, near Dieppe in Normandy, the elder son of Gustave de Maupassant and Laure Le Poittevin. When he was eleven, his mother, an independent-minded and well-read woman with a love of Shakespeare, obtained a legal separation from her violent husband and kept custody of Guy and his younger brother, Hervé. Guy grew up with her at Étretat on the Normandy coast, where he came to love fishing and the outdoors. At thirteen he was sent with his brother to a boarding school whose ecclesiastical atmosphere he disliked, and from his early education he kept a marked hostility to religion. In 1867, at his mother's urging, he met Flaubert at Croisset. He then attended the lycée in Rouen, where he wrote poetry and took part in theatricals, and in October 1868 he helped to save the poet Algernon Swinburne from drowning off Étretat.

The Franco-Prussian War broke out soon after he finished school in 1870, and he volunteered for the army. Afterwards he moved to Paris and worked as a clerk, first in the Ministry of the Navy and later in the Ministry of Public Instruction; his chief recreation was boating on the Seine on Sundays and holidays. Flaubert acted as his literary guardian, guiding his first steps in journalism and literature, and at Flaubert's home he met Émile Zola, Ivan Turgenev and other writers of the realist and naturalist schools.

His breakthrough came in 1880, when "Boule de Suif," a story of the war of 1870, appeared in *Les Soirées de Médan*, a collection of stories by Zola and five younger writers, and immediately launched his career. Flaubert called it "a masterpiece that will endure." The same year he published a volume of poems, *Des vers*. He became a regular contributor to newspapers including *Le Figaro*, *Gil Blas*, *Le Gaulois* and *L'Écho de Paris*, sometimes under pseudonyms such as "Maufrigneuse," and for a decade he worked methodically, producing two or more volumes a year. His first collection of stories, *La Maison Tellier* (1881), reached its twelfth edition within two years, and his first novel, *Une vie* (1883), sold 25,000 copies in less than a year. *Bel-Ami* (1885) went through thirty-seven printings in four months, and *Pierre et Jean* followed in 1888.

His success made him wealthy. He disliked society and liked solitude, travelled in Algeria, Italy, England, Brittany, Sicily and the Auvergne, bringing back a book from each journey, and sailed his own yacht, named *Bel-Ami* after his novel. Alexandre Dumas fils was fond of him, and he admired the historian Hippolyte Taine. In 1887 he was among the writers and artists who signed a public protest against the building of the Eiffel Tower. He declined appointment to the Legion of Honour and election to the Académie française.

In his later years Maupassant suffered from the syphilis he had contracted in youth, which brought on a craving for solitude, fear of death and fears of persecution. In January 1892 he tried to kill himself by cutting his throat, and he was committed to the private clinic of Dr Esprit Blanche at Passy, in Paris, where he died on 6 July 1893. He was buried in the Montparnasse Cemetery.

## Writing

Maupassant is regarded as one of the fathers of the modern short story. From Flaubert he learned a concise, measured style and a detached attitude toward his subjects; the 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* wrote that "his style is exceedingly simple and exceedingly strong" and called him "the most perfect master of the short story." His stories are marked by economy and by neat, seemingly effortless endings. Many are set during the Franco-Prussian War and show civilians caught up in events beyond their control. Others, such as "Le Horla," describe apparently supernatural events that often reflect the troubled minds of their narrators; Maupassant was interested in the new science of psychiatry and attended Jean-Martin Charcot's lectures in 1885–1886.

His view of human nature is often disillusioned and harsh, and he wrote equally well in a realist and in a fantastic mode; works such as "L'Héritage" and *Bel-Ami* set out to recreate the France of the Third Republic. His skill in plotting made him a model for later story writers, among them Somerset Maugham and O. Henry; Maugham reworked the idea of "The Necklace" in "Mr Know-All" and "A String of Beads," and Henry James reversed its twist in "Paste." Leo Tolstoy devoted an essay to his work, and his stories have been widely adapted for film.
