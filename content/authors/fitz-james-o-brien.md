Fitz-James O'Brien (1828–1862) was an Irish-born American writer of short stories, poems and journalism, and a pioneer of fantasy and science fiction. He is best known for "The Diamond Lens," "What Was It? A Mystery" and "The Wondersmith," tales that brought the uncanny into everyday modern settings.

## Life

Fitz-James O'Brien was born in County Cork, Ireland, the son of James O'Brien, an attorney of some influence; the exact date of his birth is debated. After his father's death his mother remarried, and the family moved to Limerick, where he spent most of his teenage years. He grew up in comfort, hunting, fishing, riding, boating and watching birds, and read widely from an early age, especially the English Romantic poets. Edgar Allan Poe was the strongest influence on his early writing. His first poems, many of them about the landscape of southwestern Ireland, appeared in *The Nation*, a weekly paper of the Irish nationalist movement.

In 1849 he inherited a large sum from his father and his maternal grandfather and moved to London, where he went to parties and theaters and spent the money within two years. He then turned to writing for pay, contributing to *The Family Friend* and other London magazines, and during the Great Exhibition of 1851 he edited *The Parlour Magazine* from the Crystal Palace, translating French works and writing original pieces. In 1852, amid rumors of an affair with a married woman, he left suddenly for the United States, arriving with little money but with letters of recommendation.

In New York he found work on *The Lantern*, a comic paper launched by his Irish-American friend John Brougham, and he became a leading figure among the Bohemians who gathered at Pfaff's Beer Hall, a circle led by Henry Clapp Jr. and Ada Clare. His story "The Bohemian" appeared in *Harper's New Monthly Magazine* in 1855. Over the following years he published a steady stream of poems, stories and essays in *Harper's*, *Putnam's Monthly*, *Harper's Weekly* and other magazines, though much of this work appeared anonymously, as was usual at the time.

The year 1858 marked a turn toward weird and horror fiction. "The Diamond Lens" appeared in the *Atlantic Monthly* in January 1858 and remains his most celebrated story. It was followed in 1859 by "What Was It? A Mystery," in *Harper's*, about an invisible creature, and "The Wondersmith," in the *Atlantic*, with its army of miniature automata.

When the Civil War began in April 1861, O'Brien enlisted in the 7th New York Militia Regiment, which marched to defend Washington and returned to New York in June. Seeking active service, he then joined the staff of General Lander in Virginia. He was wounded in a fight with Confederate cavalry near Bloomery Gap, and he died of complications from the infected wound on 6 April 1862 at Cumberland, Maryland. His friend William Winter gathered his work into *The Poems and Stories of Fitz-James O'Brien* (1881), with recollections by those who had known him.

## Writing

O'Brien learned much from Poe, but he set his tales of terror in commonplace contemporary surroundings, mixing the supernatural with the methods of the new Realism. His fantastic stories often turn on a scientific idea or invention, and on the obsession of the person who pursues it. In "The Diamond Lens" a scientist driven by a thirst for knowledge and fame commits murder for the sake of a perfect instrument, and the marvel he finds becomes the cause of his ruin. The story, the *Encyclopedia of Science Fiction* says, "remains a masterpiece because he subordinated his brilliant invention to a profound exploration of the diseased psychology" of its narrator.

"What Was It?" describes a struggle with an invisible being whose nature is never explained, although a plaster cast taken while the creature lies chloroformed shows it to be small, hideous and humanlike, and "The Wondersmith" is often described as the first story about robots. His work helped establish a mode of science fiction characterized by surface realism, and it influenced both later science fiction and the development of the short story as a form. His tales were collected in 1881 and again in 1893, "The Diamond Lens" was reprinted in *Amazing Stories* and *Weird Tales* in the 1920s, and new editions of his speculative fiction have continued to appear.
