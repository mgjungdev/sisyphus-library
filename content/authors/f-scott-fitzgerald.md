F. Scott Fitzgerald (1896–1940) was an American novelist and short-story writer whose fiction portrays the glamour and excess of the Jazz Age, a term he popularized. His first novel, *This Side of Paradise* (1920), made him famous almost overnight, while *The Great Gatsby* (1925), a commercial disappointment in his lifetime, came after his death to be counted among the central works of American literature.

## Life

Francis Scott Key Fitzgerald was born on September 24, 1896, in Saint Paul, Minnesota, into a middle-class Catholic family and named after Francis Scott Key, a distant cousin who wrote "The Star-Spangled Banner." His mother, Mary McQuillan, was the daughter of an Irish immigrant who had grown rich as a wholesale grocer; his father, Edward Fitzgerald, had moved to Minnesota from Maryland and opened a wicker-furniture business. When it failed, the family moved to Buffalo, New York, where Edward became a salesman for Procter & Gamble, and the boy spent most of his first decade there. After his father was dismissed in 1908 the family returned to Saint Paul. Fitzgerald attended St. Paul Academy, where at thirteen he saw his first story printed in the school newspaper, and in 1911 he was sent to the Newman School, a Catholic preparatory school in Hackensack, New Jersey, where Father Sigourney Fay encouraged him to become a writer.

In 1913 he entered Princeton University. His friends there included Edmund Wilson and John Peale Bishop, and he wrote for the Triangle Club and the *Princeton Tiger*. During a Christmas visit home in his sophomore year he fell in love with Ginevra King, a sixteen-year-old Chicago debutante whose family discouraged the match; she became a model for Isabelle in *This Side of Paradise* and Daisy Buchanan in *The Great Gatsby*. In 1917 he left Princeton for the army and was commissioned a second lieutenant. While in training he hurriedly wrote a novel, *The Romantic Egotist*, which Scribner's rejected, although the editor Maxwell Perkins encouraged him to revise it. In June 1918 he was posted to Camp Sheridan near Montgomery, Alabama, where he met Zelda Sayre, the seventeen-year-old granddaughter of a Confederate senator. The war ended before he was sent overseas. After his discharge in February 1919 he wrote advertising copy in New York while his stories were rejected, and in June Zelda broke off their engagement. He went back to Saint Paul and rewrote his novel in his parents' house, and that autumn Scribner's accepted it.

*This Side of Paradise* appeared on March 26, 1920, and became a sensation. Magazines now bought his stories. He and Zelda married on April 3, 1920, at St. Patrick's Cathedral in New York, and they quickly became celebrities. Their only child, Frances Scott "Scottie" Fitzgerald, was born in Saint Paul on October 26, 1921. *The Beautiful and Damned* was published in March 1922, followed that year by the collection *Tales of the Jazz Age*. In October 1922 the family moved to Great Neck, Long Island; his play *The Vegetable* failed in November 1923, and he wrote magazine stories to pay his debts. In May 1924 they went to Europe, and on the French Riviera and in Rome he finished *The Great Gatsby*. In Paris he befriended Ernest Hemingway, whom he met in May 1925.

The Fitzgeralds returned to America in December 1926, spent two months in Hollywood early in 1927, and then rented a house near Wilmington, Delaware. They went back to Europe in 1929, and in 1930 Zelda was diagnosed with schizophrenia and treated at a clinic in Switzerland; from 1932 she was in hospitals in the United States. *Tender Is the Night*, drawing on these years, appeared in April 1934 to mixed reviews and slow sales. In the Depression his work fell out of favor, his royalties dwindled, and years of alcoholism damaged his health.

In 1937 he took a contract as a screenwriter with Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer and moved to Hollywood, where his only screen credit was for *Three Comrades* (1938). He lived with the columnist Sheilah Graham, wrote the Pat Hobby stories for *Esquire*, and worked on a novel about a film producer, *The Last Tycoon*. He had stopped drinking more than a year before he died of a heart attack in Hollywood on December 21, 1940, at the age of 44. He was buried in Rockville, Maryland, and in 1975 his and Zelda's remains were moved to the family plot in the town's Catholic cemetery, Saint Mary's. Edmund Wilson edited the unfinished *The Last Tycoon* for publication in 1941.

## Writing

Fitzgerald published four novels, four story collections and 164 short stories. His subjects are youth, ambition, and love entangled with money and social position, and he drew closely on his own life: Princeton, Ginevra King, Zelda and the parties of Long Island all passed into his fiction. "The whole idea of Gatsby," he explained, "is the unfairness of a poor young man not being able to marry a girl with money. This theme comes up again and again because I lived it." To pay for the life he and Zelda led he wrote many stories for well-paying magazines such as *The Saturday Evening Post*, *Collier's* and *Esquire*. For *Gatsby* he set out to move beyond the realism of his first two novels, consciously imitating Joseph Conrad and Willa Cather. Edmund Wilson described his manner as "romantic, but also cynical; he is bitter as well as ecstatic; astringent as well as lyrical."

*This Side of Paradise* was greeted with enthusiasm. Burton Rascoe of the *Chicago Tribune* predicted that its author would "be much heard of hereafter," and H. L. Mencken praised it in *The Smart Set*, though many at Princeton resented it. *The Great Gatsby* received generally favorable reviews but sold poorly, and in the Depression his work was dismissed as elitist and materialistic. At his death *The Nation* called his "a fair-weather talent which was not adequate to the stormy age." Interest revived with Wilson's edition of *The Last Tycoon*, which included *Gatsby*, and with the free Armed Services Editions of *Gatsby* given to American soldiers in the Second World War. By 1960 the novel was selling 100,000 copies a year, and it has since been widely regarded as a contender for the title of the "Great American Novel."

## In this library

- [This Side of Paradise](this-side-of-paradise) — Amory Blaine, a young Midwesterner convinced of a great destiny, passes through preparatory school and Princeton and a series of unfulfilling romances. It was published by Charles Scribner's Sons on March 26, 1920, and its first printing sold out in three days.
- [The Diamond as Big as the Ritz](diamond-as-big-as-the-ritz) — The schoolboy John T. Unger spends the summer at the Montana home of his classmate Percy Washington, whose family owns a mountain made of a single diamond and kills to keep it secret. It first appeared in *The Smart Set* in June 1922 and was collected in *Tales of the Jazz Age* the same year.
- [The Great Gatsby](the-great-gatsby) — On Long Island in 1922 the narrator Nick Carraway comes to know his neighbor Jay Gatsby, a mysterious millionaire determined to win back his former lover, Daisy Buchanan. It was published by Charles Scribner's Sons on April 10, 1925.
