Jack London (1876–1916) was an American novelist, short story writer, journalist and socialist from San Francisco and Oakland, California. He was one of the first American authors to become an international celebrity and to earn a fortune from writing for the new popular magazines. He is best known for *The Call of the Wild*, *White Fang* and the stories he set in the Klondike during the gold rush of the 1890s.

## Life

John Griffith London was born on January 12, 1876, in San Francisco. His mother, Flora Wellman, had come west from Massillon, Ohio, and worked in the city as a music teacher and spiritualist. After his birth the baby was nursed by Virginia Prentiss, a neighbor and former slave, who remained a mother figure to him throughout his life. Late in 1876 Flora married John London, a partially disabled Civil War veteran, and the family moved around the Bay Area before settling in Oakland, where the boy finished grade school. He was largely self-educated. In 1886 he found a sympathetic librarian at the Oakland Public Library, Ina Coolbrith, who encouraged his reading.

From 1889 he worked twelve to eighteen hours a day in a cannery. Seeking a way out, he borrowed money from Virginia Prentiss, bought the sloop *Razzle-Dazzle* and became an oyster pirate on San Francisco Bay, and later he joined the California Fish Patrol. In 1893 he signed on to the sealing schooner *Sophie Sutherland*, bound for the coast of Japan. Back home during the depression that followed the Panic of 1893, he worked in a jute mill and a power plant, then joined Coxey's Army and took to the road as a tramp. In 1894 he served thirty days for vagrancy in the Erie County Penitentiary in Buffalo, New York, an experience he later described in *The Road*. Returning to Oakland, he attended Oakland High School, studied at Heinold's First and Last Chance Saloon on the waterfront, and joined the Socialist Labor Party in April 1896. That year he was admitted to the University of California at Berkeley, but lack of money forced him to leave in 1897.

On July 12, 1897, he sailed north with his brother-in-law to join the Klondike Gold Rush. He found no fortune and developed scurvy, losing four front teeth, but the winter in the Yukon gave him the material for his first successful stories. Back in California in 1898 he set out to sell his writing, a struggle he later retold in *Martin Eden*. *The Overland Monthly* offered him five dollars for "To the Man on Trail," and he nearly gave up until *The Black Cat* paid forty dollars for "A Thousand Deaths," the "first money I ever received for a story." His first collection, *The Son of the Wolf*, appeared on April 7, 1900, the day he married Elizabeth "Bessie" Maddern. They had two daughters, Joan and Becky, born in 1901 and 1902, and divorced in 1904. In 1901 he joined the new Socialist Party of America, and he ran unsuccessfully as its candidate for mayor of Oakland in 1901 and 1905.

In early 1903 he sold *The Call of the Wild* to *The Saturday Evening Post* for $750 and the book rights to Macmillan, and its success made his name. In 1904 he covered the Russo-Japanese War for the *San Francisco Examiner*; he was arrested several times by the Japanese authorities and left the front that June. In 1905 he married Charmian Kittredge and bought a ranch at Glen Ellen in Sonoma County, which he called the dearest thing in the world to him after his wife. Writing became the means of paying for it. He lectured across the country on socialism in 1906 and, with Charmian, sailed the yacht *Snark* to Hawaii and the South Pacific. His great stone house on the ranch, Wolf House, burned down shortly before the couple was to move in.

In later years he spent long periods in Hawaii and drank heavily, and in 1914 he went to Mexico as a war correspondent. He resigned from the Socialist Party in 1916. Suffering from kidney failure, he died at his ranch on November 22, 1916; his death certificate gives the cause as uremia. His ashes were buried on the property, which is now Jack London State Historic Park.

## Writing

London wrote quickly and in great quantity: some nineteen collections of short stories and more than twenty novels, along with essays, memoirs, plays and reportage. Much of his fiction draws directly on his own life at sea, on the road and in the North. Like Frank Norris and Theodore Dreiser, he was influenced by the naturalism of Émile Zola, and his stories set men and animals against a hostile environment in which survival depends on strength, instinct and knowledge. He was drawn also to Darwin, Herbert Spencer and Nietzsche, and his socialism, which he traced in "How I Became a Socialist" to his years among the poor, shapes *The People of the Abyss* and the dystopian *The Iron Heel*. George Orwell later found in his "exaltation of the primitive" what "one might fairly call a Fascist strain."

His work sold widely from the start. H. L. Mencken wrote that "no other popular writer of his time did any better writing than you will find in *The Call of the Wild*." Ambrose Bierce called Wolf Larsen of *The Sea-Wolf* a "tremendous creation" but thought the love story "awful." In 1907 President Theodore Roosevelt named London among the "nature fakers," citing a dog fight in *White Fang* as "the very sublimity of absurdity." Because he bought plots, including some from the young Sinclair Lewis, and worked from newspaper clippings, he faced accusations of plagiarism; he once wrote that "expression, you see—with me—is far easier than invention." His spare prose in passages of action has been seen as a forerunner of Hemingway's style.

## In this library

- [The Call of the Wild](call-of-the-wild) — Buck, a large dog stolen from a ranch in California's Santa Clara Valley, is sold as a sled dog in the Klondike and gradually returns to the wild. Serialized in *The Saturday Evening Post* in 1903 and published as a book by Macmillan the same year.
- [The Sea-Wolf](sea-wolf) — The literary critic Humphrey Van Weyden, rescued after a ferry collision, is forced to serve aboard a sealing schooner under the brutal and self-taught captain Wolf Larsen. Published in 1904, when its first printing of forty thousand copies sold out before publication.
- [White Fang](white-fang) — A wolf-dog born in the wild of the Yukon grows up in a native camp, is sold to a cruel dog-fighter, and is at last tamed by the mining expert Weedon Scott, who takes him home to California. Serialized in *Outing* magazine from May to October 1906.
- [To Build a Fire](to-build-a-fire) — A newcomer to the Yukon ignores an old-timer's warning and sets out alone with a husky on a trail at seventy-five degrees below zero, where everything comes to depend on keeping a fire alight. London's second version of the story, published in *The Century Magazine* in 1908, replaced a milder one printed in *The Youth's Companion* in 1902.
