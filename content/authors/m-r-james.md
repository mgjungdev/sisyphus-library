M. R. James (1862–1936) was an English medieval scholar and writer of ghost stories who served as provost of King's College, Cambridge, and then of Eton College. His tales, first read aloud to friends at Christmas and collected in *Ghost Stories of an Antiquary* and its successors, are widely considered among the finest ghost stories in English.

## Life

Montague Rhodes James was born on 1 August 1862 in a clergy house at Goodnestone, near Dover, in Kent. His father, Herbert James, was an Evangelical Anglican clergyman, and his mother, Mary Emily Horton, was the daughter of a naval officer. He was the youngest of four children. From 1865 until 1909 the family home was the rectory at Great Livermere in Suffolk, and Suffolk later provided the settings of several of his stories, among them "Oh, Whistle, and I'll Come to You, My Lad," set on the coast near Felixstowe, and "A Warning to the Curious," set at Aldeburgh.

In September 1873 James went as a boarder to Temple Grove School in East Sheen, and from 1876 to 1882 he was at Eton College, where he claimed to have translated the apocryphal Book of Baruch from Ethiopic. In 1882 he went up to King's College, Cambridge, which was to be his home for more than thirty years. He won many academic prizes and became a fellow in 1887, and he served the college as dean, tutor and lecturer in divinity. At Cambridge he acted with success in a staging of Aristophanes' *The Birds*, with music by Hubert Parry, and the same gift was later evident when he read his ghost stories aloud.

James's scholarship was immense. Between 1895 and 1914 he compiled descriptive catalogues of the manuscripts in the libraries of the Cambridge colleges. His discovery of a manuscript fragment led in 1902 to excavations at the ruined abbey of Bury St Edmunds, where the graves of several twelfth-century abbots were found. He translated the New Testament apocrypha, wrote on the illustrated Apocalypse manuscripts and on the roof bosses of Norwich Cathedral, and later wrote popular books such as *Suffolk and Norfolk* (1930). He was assistant director of the Fitzwilliam Museum in Cambridge from 1886 and its director from 1893 to 1908, and secured important paintings and manuscripts for it.

James began the custom of reading a new ghost story to friends at King's, often on Christmas Eve, at meetings of the Chit-Chat Club and similar gatherings. His friend James McBryde drew four illustrations for the stories but died suddenly in June 1904; James published *Ghost Stories of an Antiquary* later that year with only McBryde's drawings, as a tribute to him. *More Ghost Stories of an Antiquary* followed in 1911, *A Thin Ghost and Others* in 1919, and *A Warning to the Curious* in 1925. In 1931 the stories were gathered in *The Collected Ghost Stories of M. R. James*.

James was elected provost of King's College in 1905 and served as vice-chancellor of the university from 1913 to 1915. In 1918 he returned to Eton as its provost, the only man to have been provost of both foundations, and he held the post until his death. He was admitted to the Order of Merit in 1930. James died on 12 June 1936, aged 73, and was buried in the town cemetery at Eton.

## Writing

James set out to put the reader into the position of saying, "If I'm not very careful, something of this kind may happen to me!" His typical story has a quiet, well-observed setting, such as an English village, a seaside town, an old abbey or a university, and a reserved, rather naive scholar as its hero, who finds an old book or object that draws the attention of something malevolent from the past. He described the method himself: the characters should be introduced "in a placid way," going about their ordinary business, and into this calm "let the ominous thing put out its head, unobtrusively at first, and then more insistently, until it holds the stage." He insisted that the ghost be "malevolent or odious," and he preferred reticence and suggestion to explicit horror, leaving much to the reader's imagination.

His precise antiquarian detail and dry humour ground the supernatural in a believable world. James edited and introduced the stories of Sheridan Le Fanu, whom he admired. H. P. Lovecraft praised his tales as the peak of the form, and many later writers imitated his manner. Adaptations, above all for BBC television, have kept his name associated with the English ghost story and with folk horror.
