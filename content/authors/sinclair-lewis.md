Sinclair Lewis (1885–1951) was an American novelist and short-story writer and, in 1930, the first American to receive the Nobel Prize in Literature. In *Main Street*, *Babbitt*, *Arrowsmith* and *Elmer Gantry* he satirized the conformity, commercialism and complacency of small-town and middle-class America.

## Life

Harry Sinclair Lewis was born on 7 February 1885 in Sauk Centre, Minnesota, the youngest of three sons of Edwin J. Lewis, a physician of Welsh descent, and Emma Kermott Lewis. His mother died in 1891, when he was six, and the next year his father married Isabel Warner, who became an important presence in his life. In 1902 he left Sauk Centre for Oberlin Academy in Ohio, and in 1903 he entered Yale University, where he became an editor of the *Yale Literary Magazine*. He interrupted his studies to work at Upton Sinclair's Helicon Home Colony and to travel to Panama, and graduated from Yale in 1908.

For several years he travelled about the United States and worked in journalism and in New York publishing houses. His first novel, *Hike and the Aeroplane* (1912), appeared under the pseudonym Tom Graham. *Our Mr. Wrenn* (1914) began his serious career and was followed by *The Trail of the Hawk* (1915) and *The Job* (1917). He also earned a good income from short stories sold to the *Saturday Evening Post* and other magazines. On 15 April 1914 he married Grace Livingston Hegger, whom he had met in New York; their son Wells was born in 1917.

*Main Street* (1920), set in the prairie town of Gopher Prairie, a portrait of Sauk Centre, was one of the most sensational events in American publishing history, selling some 180,000 copies in its first six months; its heroine was loosely based on his stepmother. *Babbitt* followed in 1922 and *Arrowsmith* in 1925. *Arrowsmith* was awarded the Pulitzer Prize, which Lewis declined. *Mantrap* (1926), *Elmer Gantry* (1927), *The Man Who Knew Coolidge* (1928) and *Dodsworth* (1929) completed the most productive decade of his life.

His first marriage ended in divorce, and in 1928 he married the foreign correspondent Dorothy Thompson; their son Michael was born in 1930. In that year the Swedish Academy awarded Lewis the Nobel Prize in Literature "for his vigorous and graphic art of description." In his Nobel lecture, "The American Fear of Literature," delivered on 12 December 1930, he criticized the American literary establishment.

Lewis wrote eleven more novels after the prize, but none is generally ranked with his work of the 1920s. The best known is *It Can't Happen Here* (1935), which imagines a fascist dictatorship coming to power in the United States. His second marriage also ended in divorce. From 1939 he had a long relationship with Rosemary Marcella Powers. In 1944 his elder son, Wells, was killed in France during the Second World War. That year Lewis moved to Duluth, Minnesota, where he worked on *Kingsblood Royal* (1947), a novel about race in America. He struggled for many years with alcoholism. He died in Rome on 10 January 1951, at the age of sixty-five; his body was cremated and his ashes buried in Greenwood Cemetery in Sauk Centre.

## Writing

Lewis's novels joined realism and satire, setting recognizable American types against a closely observed background of small towns, growing cities and the business culture of the years between the wars. *Main Street* satirized small-town life, and *Babbitt* portrayed the life, business culture and social anatomy of an American city with a deft balance between realism and satire. Besides the novels he wrote a great many short stories for popular magazines such as the *Saturday Evening Post* and *The Century*. He became known for his critical views of American capitalism and materialism and for his strong characterizations of modern working women. H. L. Mencken praised him as a "novelist with an authentic call to the trade."

The Swedish Academy particularly admired *Babbitt*, whose hero it saw as the ideal of the American middle-class popular hero, and the name of that hero passed into the dictionary as a word for a businessman who conforms unthinkingly to middle-class standards. *Babbitt* was filmed by Warner Bros. in 1924 and again in 1934. After the 1930s his reputation declined beside those of Hemingway and Fitzgerald, but interest in his work revived after 2016, particularly in *It Can't Happen Here* and its picture of an American dictatorship.
