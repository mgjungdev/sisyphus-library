Daniel Defoe (1661?–1731) was an English novelist, journalist, pamphleteer, merchant and political agent, and one of the earliest writers of the English novel. He is famous above all for *Robinson Crusoe*, and also for *Moll Flanders*, *A Journal of the Plague Year* and *Roxana*.

## Life

Daniel Foe was born in the parish of St Giles, Cripplegate, in London, around 1660; the exact date is unknown. His father, James Foe, was a prosperous tradesman, and the family were Presbyterian dissenters, outside the Church of England. As a boy he lived through the Great Plague of 1665 and the Great Fire of London the next year. At about fourteen he was sent to the dissenting academy kept by Charles Morton at Newington Green, near London, which prepared students for the ministry, but he chose trade instead. In later life he added the aristocratic-sounding "De" to his name.

Defoe became a merchant, dealing in hosiery, woollen goods and wine and travelling on business in Europe. In 1685 he joined the Duke of Monmouth's rebellion against James II and escaped punishment. After the Revolution of 1688 he became a close ally of William III. In 1692 he failed in business with debts of about £17,000, and he was rarely free from debt afterwards. He later managed a brick and tile works at Tilbury in Essex.

He soon made his name as a writer. *An Essay upon Projects* proposed schemes for banks, insurance, asylums and schools for women, and his satirical poem *The True-Born Englishman* (1701), defending King William against xenophobic attacks, was his greatest success in verse. After Queen Anne's accession, his pamphlet *The Shortest-Way with the Dissenters* (1702), which ironically urged the most extreme measures against dissenters, was at first approved in earnest by some high Tories. Defoe was convicted of seditious libel, fined and imprisoned, and in July 1703 he stood three days in the pillory, where the crowd took his side; he answered with *A Hymn to the Pillory*. His imprisonment ruined the Tilbury works.

The minister Robert Harley secured Defoe's release and employed him as a writer and secret agent. In 1704 Defoe began *A Review of the Affairs of France*, a periodical he wrote almost single-handedly until 1713, and he published *The Storm* (1704), a collection of eyewitness accounts of the great storm of November 1703. In 1706 he was sent to Edinburgh to report on Scottish opinion and work quietly for the union of England and Scotland, and in 1709 he published a history of the Union. After the Tories fell in 1714 he went on writing and gathering intelligence for the Whig government.

Defoe was in his late fifties when he published *Robinson Crusoe* in April 1719. It was immediately popular, and he followed it with a second part the same year and a volume of *Serious Reflections* in 1720. In the next few years came *Captain Singleton* (1720), *Moll Flanders*, *A Journal of the Plague Year* and *Colonel Jack* (all 1722) and *Roxana* (1724), together with *A Tour thro' the Whole Island of Great Britain* (1724–1727), *The Complete English Tradesman* (1726), books on the supernatural and many pamphlets. He died in April 1731 in Ropemaker's Alley, Moorfields, not far from where he was born, and was buried in Bunhill Fields, where a monument to him was put up in 1870.

## Writing

Defoe was one of the most productive writers in English; more than five hundred titles have been attributed to him, most of them published anonymously or under some of his many pen names, so that the full list of his works is still disputed. The 1911 *Britannica* admired his "lively and lucid style" even in his early *Essay upon Projects*. He was known for the didactic aim of his writing, and he presented his novels as the true memoirs of their narrators. The same article praised the "peculiar verisimilitude" of his invented voyages.

His novels are usually moral in purpose, and *Robinson Crusoe* draws on the form of the spiritual autobiography, tracing its narrator's struggles with faith and his final conversion. *Moll Flanders* and *Roxana* were published anonymously and were not printed under Defoe's name until the 1770s. *Moll Flanders*, whose heroine is a thief and bigamist who keeps the reader's sympathy and finally repents, challenged the period's ideas of womanhood, while *Roxana* is unusual among his books in that its heroine has no true conversion. His narrators are often outsiders, a castaway, a thief, a pirate, a courtesan, who must make their way by their own wits and labour. Samuel Johnson placed *Robinson Crusoe* among the only three books that readers wish longer, and it has been called the first English novel, though the label is disputed. Its many imitations gave a name to a whole genre, the Robinsonade, and Swift's *Gulliver's Travels* has been read partly as a reply to it.

## In this library

- [Robinson Crusoe](robinson-crusoe) — A young man from York runs away to sea against his father's wishes, and after captivity in Morocco and years as a planter in Brazil he is shipwrecked alone on an island off the coast of South America, where he builds a life for himself over twenty-eight years and rescues a captive whom he names Friday. It was published in London by William Taylor on 25 April 1719 as *The Life and Strange Surprizing Adventures of Robinson Crusoe*, presented as the work of Crusoe himself, and went through four editions within months.
