Upton Sinclair (1878–1968) was an American novelist, muckraking journalist and socialist political campaigner who wrote nearly a hundred books. He is best known for *The Jungle*, his novel of immigrant workers in the Chicago meatpacking plants, whose exposure of unsanitary conditions helped bring about the Pure Food and Drug Act and the Meat Inspection Act of 1906.

## Life

Upton Beall Sinclair Jr. was born on 20 September 1878 in Baltimore, Maryland, the son of Upton Beall Sinclair Sr., a liquor salesman whose alcoholism shadowed his son's childhood, and Priscilla Harden Sinclair, a strict Episcopalian. His father's Southern family had been ruined by the Civil War, while his mother's parents were prosperous, and the boy's stays with them showed him how both the rich and the poor lived. The family moved often. He learned to love reading at five but did not start school until he was ten. In 1888 the family moved to Queens, New York City, and on 15 September 1892, five days before his fourteenth birthday, he entered the City College of New York, paying his way by writing jokes, dime novels and stories for boys' weeklies and pulp magazines.

He graduated in June 1897 and went on to Columbia University, where he studied law and languages but left without a degree, still supporting himself with boys' adventure stories, at times writing up to 8,000 words a day with the help of stenographers. On 18 October 1900 he married Meta Fuller, and their son David was born in 1901. His first serious novels, *King Midas* (1901), *Prince Hagen* (1902), *The Journal of Arthur Stirling* (1903) and the Civil War novel *Manassas* (1904), were well reviewed but sold poorly.

In 1904 Sinclair went undercover in the meatpacking district of Chicago's Union Stock Yards to gather material for the socialist newspaper *Appeal to Reason*, which serialised *The Jungle* in 1905. Published as a book in 1906, it became a bestseller and made him famous. In the spring of 1905 he called for the formation of the Intercollegiate Socialist Society. With his earnings from the novel he founded the Helicon Home Colony, a utopian community in Englewood, New Jersey, which burned down within a year, and in 1906 he ran for Congress in New Jersey as a Socialist.

In 1910 the Sinclairs moved to the single-tax village of Arden, Delaware. Meta left him in 1911 for the poet Harry Kemp, and an Amsterdam court annulled the marriage in 1912. In 1913 he married Mary Craig Kimbrough, of a prominent family in Greenwood, Mississippi, who had come to one of his lectures on *The Jungle*. Trips to the Colorado coalfields in 1913–1914 led to the novel *King Coal* (1917), and in 1914 he helped organise demonstrations in New York against Rockefeller at the Standard Oil offices. He broke with the Socialist Party in 1917 to support American entry into the First World War, but later returned to it.

In 1916 the Sinclairs moved to southern California. There he founded the Southern California chapter of the American Civil Liberties Union, and in 1923, speaking at a rally during the San Pedro maritime strike, he was arrested for reading the Bill of Rights. He ran for Congress, the Senate and the governorship of California on the Socialist ticket. *The Brass Check* (1919), his attack on American journalism, was followed by novels on the oil and auto industries, *Oil!* (1927) and *The Flivver King* (1937). With his wife he produced Sergei Eisenstein's film *¡Que viva Mexico!* in 1930–32.

In 1934 Sinclair won the Democratic nomination for governor of California with his End Poverty in California (EPIC) campaign. Opposed by newspapers, business and the Hollywood studios, he received 879,000 votes and lost to Governor Frank Merriam, an experience he described in *I, Candidate for Governor: And How I Got Licked* (1935). Between 1940 and 1953 he wrote eleven novels about Lanny Budd, the son of an American arms manufacturer, and the third of them, *Dragon's Teeth* (1942), about the Nazi takeover of Germany, won the Pulitzer Prize for the Novel in 1943.

Mary Craig Sinclair died in 1961, and later that year he married Mary Elizabeth Willis, who died in 1967. Sinclair died in a nursing home in Bound Brook, New Jersey, on 25 November 1968, and was buried beside her in Rock Creek Cemetery in Washington, D.C.

## Writing

Sinclair devoted his career, in fiction and non-fiction alike, to documenting and criticising the social and economic conditions of industrial America and the effects of poverty on the working class. His novels look at industry from the side of the worker and of the owner: *King Coal* confronts Rockefeller's role in the Ludlow Massacre, and *The Flivver King* follows the rise of Henry Ford and his slide into antisemitism. *The Brass Check*, which he called "the most important and most dangerous book I have ever written," argued that "American Journalism is a class institution, serving the rich and spurning the poor."

*The Jungle* was written to win readers to socialism, and it ends with its hero converted to the socialist cause. Jack London called it "the Uncle Tom's Cabin of wage slavery." But the public fastened on its passages about diseased and adulterated meat, and Sinclair wrote in *Cosmopolitan* in October 1906, "I aimed at the public's heart, and by accident I hit it in the stomach." His popularity was greatest in the first half of the twentieth century; the Lanny Budd novels were bestsellers and were translated in many countries. *Time* magazine called him "a man with every gift except humor and silence."
