Elizabeth Gaskell (1810–1865) was an English novelist, short-story writer and biographer whose novels give detailed pictures of Victorian society, including the lives of the very poor. She is best known for *Mary Barton*, *Cranford*, *North and South* and *Wives and Daughters*, and for *The Life of Charlotte Brontë*, which did much to establish the lasting fame of the Brontë sisters.

## Life

Elizabeth Cleghorn Stevenson was born on 29 September 1810 in Lindsey Row, Chelsea, in London. Her father, William Stevenson, came from Berwick-upon-Tweed and had been a Unitarian minister before becoming Keeper of the Records to the Treasury. Her mother, Elizabeth Holland, came from a Cheshire family and died when the child was a baby, and Elizabeth was taken to Knutsford in Cheshire to be brought up by her mother's sister, Hannah Lumb. The little country town, where she spent much of her childhood, later became the model for Cranford. As a girl she attended a school kept by the Byerley sisters, which settled at Avonbank near Stratford-upon-Avon. Her only surviving brother, John, a sailor in the merchant service, disappeared on a voyage about 1827, and her father died in 1829. As a young woman she visited relatives in London, spent time in Newcastle upon Tyne in the family of the Unitarian minister William Turner, and stayed in Edinburgh.

On 30 August 1832, at Knutsford, she married William Gaskell, minister of the Cross Street Unitarian Chapel in Manchester, and from then on her life belonged to that city. Their first daughter was stillborn, and four daughters, Marianne, Meta, Florence and Julia, grew up. Husband and wife wrote poems together on the lives of the poor, one of which appeared in *Blackwood's Magazine* in January 1837, and her account of Clopton Hall was printed in William Howitt's *Visits to Remarkable Places* in 1840. Her first short stories appeared in *Howitt's Journal* in 1847 under the pseudonym "Cotton Mather Mills."

The Gaskells' only son, William, died of scarlet fever in infancy while the family was in North Wales, and it was to turn her mind from grief that her husband encouraged her to write a long work of fiction. The result, *Mary Barton: A Tale of Manchester Life*, was published anonymously by Chapman & Hall in October 1848, the publishers paying £100 for the copyright. It was a great success and won her the praise and friendship of Thomas Carlyle, Walter Savage Landor and Charles Dickens, though some Manchester manufacturers complained that she had maligned them. Dickens invited her to write for his new weekly *Household Words*, whose first number, in March 1850, began her story "Lizzie Leigh." That year the family moved to a house at 84 Plymouth Grove, where they received many writers and reformers.

In August 1850 she met Charlotte Brontë, who became a close friend and stayed with her in Manchester. The sketches that became *Cranford* appeared in *Household Words* from December 1851 to May 1853; *Ruth*, a novel about a seduced girl's redemption, came out in 1853; and *North and South* was serialised in 1854 and 1855. After Charlotte Brontë's death in March 1855 her father asked Gaskell to write her life. *The Life of Charlotte Brontë* (1857) was widely admired, but some statements in it brought threats of libel, and a retraction was published and the third edition revised.

During the cotton famine of 1862 and 1863 she worked for hours each day organising sewing rooms for the relief of the Manchester poor. She visited Whitby to gather material for *Sylvia's Lovers* (1863), a story of the press-gang, and the idyll *Cousin Phillis* was serialised in *The Cornhill Magazine*, which also began her last novel, *Wives and Daughters*, in August 1864. With the earnings from her writing she bought a house at Holybourne, near Alton in Hampshire, as a surprise for her husband. She died there suddenly of heart disease on 12 November 1865, with *Wives and Daughters* almost finished, and was buried in the graveyard of the Unitarian chapel at Knutsford.

## Writing

Gaskell's early work belongs to the "industrial" or social novel. *Mary Barton* brought the slums of Manchester alive for readers who had never seen them, and *North and South* weighs the claims of masters and workers with greater balance. Her Unitarian faith gave her fiction a spirit of tolerance and sympathy across lines of class and creed. She wrote vividly of women's lives, used local dialect words even in the mouths of middle-class characters, and also produced ghost stories in the Gothic vein. Beside the social novels stand the gentle humor of *Cranford*, which Lord Houghton called "the purest piece of humoristic description that has been added to British literature since Charles Lamb," and the quiet domestic realism of *Wives and Daughters*.

George Sand said that she had written novels which excited "the deepest interest in men of the world, and yet which every girl will be the better for reading." After her death *Cranford* remained popular and was accepted as a classic, but her other novels were long neglected, and early twentieth-century critics dismissed her as a minor writer with "feminine" sensibilities. From the 1950s critics such as Kathleen Tillotson, Arnold Kettle and Raymond Williams re-examined her treatment of social and industrial problems, and her standing in Victorian literary studies has grown steadily since. *Cranford*, *North and South* and *Wives and Daughters* have all been adapted for television by the BBC.

## In this library

- [Cranford](cranford) — In a small country town ruled by a society of genteel widows and spinsters practising "elegant economy," the young narrator Mary Smith follows the fortunes of the gentle Miss Matty Jenkyns through love remembered, a bank failure and the return of a long-lost brother. It appeared in instalments in Dickens's *Household Words* from December 1851 to May 1853 and was published as a book by Chapman & Hall in June 1853.
- [North and South](north-and-south) — Margaret Hale, a clergyman's daughter from the rural south of England, moves with her parents to the northern manufacturing town of Milton and clashes with the mill owner John Thornton amid strikes and poverty. It was serialised in twenty weekly parts in *Household Words* from September 1854 to January 1855 and published, revised and enlarged, by Chapman & Hall in two volumes in 1855.
