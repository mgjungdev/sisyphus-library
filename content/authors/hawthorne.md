Nathaniel Hawthorne (1804–1864) was an American novelist and short story writer from Salem, Massachusetts. His tales and romances, most of them set in Puritan or post-Puritan New England, turn on guilt, secret sin, and the long reach of the past. *The Scarlet Letter* (1850) made him one of the best-known writers in the United States.

## Life

Hawthorne was born in Salem, Massachusetts, on July 4, 1804, the son of Nathaniel Hathorne, a sea captain, and Elizabeth Clarke Manning. His father died of fever in Surinam in 1808. Among his ancestors was John Hathorne, a magistrate who examined accused witches in Salem in 1692; Hawthorne added the "w" to the family name as a young man, possibly to distance himself from that inheritance. In 1816 the family moved to Raymond, Maine, and in 1819 he was sent back to Salem for school.

In 1821 he entered Bowdoin College, where his friends included Henry Wadsworth Longfellow and Franklin Pierce, and he graduated in 1825. He returned to Salem and spent the following years in relative seclusion, reading and writing. His first novel, *Fanshawe*, appeared anonymously in 1828 at his own expense. Through the 1830s he published tales without his name in magazines and gift books such as *The Token*. In 1837 his friend Horatio Bridge put up the money to collect them, and *Twice-Told Tales* came out that year; an expanded edition followed in 1842.

Writing did not support him. In 1839 he took a post as weigher and gauger at the Boston Custom House, and in 1841 he joined the Brook Farm community, which he left before the year was out. On July 9, 1842, he married Sophia Peabody in Boston, and the couple settled at the Old Manse in Concord, where they lived for three years. *Mosses from an Old Manse*, collecting many of his tales, was published in 1846. That year he became surveyor of the Salem Custom House, a political appointment he lost after the election of 1848.

Out of office, he wrote *The Scarlet Letter*, published in March 1850 with an introductory sketch, "The Custom-House," that offended many in Salem. The first printing of 2,500 copies sold out within ten days. The family then moved to Lenox in the Berkshires, where on August 5, 1850, Hawthorne met Herman Melville at a picnic; Melville later dedicated *Moby-Dick* to him. At Lenox Hawthorne wrote *The House of the Seven Gables* (1851), and *The Blithedale Romance*, drawing on his Brook Farm months, followed in 1852.

In 1852 the Hawthornes returned to Concord and bought a house they named The Wayside. Hawthorne wrote a campaign biography of his college friend Franklin Pierce, and after Pierce's election as president he was appointed United States consul in Liverpool in 1853. After the consulship the family lived in France and Italy, and they returned home in 1860, the year *The Marble Faun* was published, his first new book in seven years. *Our Old Home*, a book of English sketches, appeared in 1863 with a dedication to Pierce. During the Civil War he met Abraham Lincoln and wrote about the visit. While on a tour of the White Mountains, he died in his sleep at Plymouth, New Hampshire, in May 1864. He was buried in Sleepy Hollow Cemetery in Concord.

## Writing

Hawthorne's fiction belongs to the darker side of American Romanticism. His subjects are hidden guilt, pride, the isolation of the individual, and the moral inheritance of Puritan New England, treated through symbol and allegory: a veil, a birthmark, a poisonous garden, an embroidered letter. He called his longer works romances rather than novels, claiming for them a freedom from "the possible or probable course of ordinary experience," and he often leaves the reader uncertain whether an event is supernatural, dreamed, or explained.

His tales were first noticed in the late 1830s. Longfellow reviewed *Twice-Told Tales* warmly in the *North American Review* in 1837. Edgar Allan Poe, reviewing the 1842 edition in *Graham's Magazine*, praised its originality and wrote that "the style of Hawthorne is purity itself," while objecting to his reliance on allegory. In 1850 Melville's essay "Hawthorne and His Mosses," published in the *Literary World*, praised his "great power of blackness" and traced it to a "Calvinistic sense of Innate Depravity and Original Sin."

*The Scarlet Letter* was a best-seller and opened the most profitable period of his career. Literary critics admired it, though Edwin Percy Whipple objected to its "morbid intensity," and religious reviewers attacked its subject; Orestes Brownson held that Hawthorne misunderstood confession and remorse. *The House of the Seven Gables* went through four printings in its first year. Longfellow called it "a weird, wild book" and Whipple thought it Hawthorne's greatest work, while Catharine Maria Sedgwick wrote, "The book is an affliction."

## In this library

- [Young Goodman Brown](young-goodman-brown) — A young Puritan of Salem Village leaves his wife Faith for a night in the forest, where he seems to find his neighbors gathered at a witches' meeting. First published anonymously in the *New-England Magazine* in 1835 and collected in *Mosses from an Old Manse* (1846).
- [The Minister's Black Veil](ministers-black-veil) — The Reverend Mr. Hooper puts on a black veil one Sunday and wears it, unexplained, until his death and burial. First printed in *The Token* in 1836 and collected in *Twice-Told Tales* (1837).
- [The Birth-Mark](birth-mark) — The scientist Aylmer cannot bear the small hand-shaped mark on his wife Georgiana's cheek and sets out to remove it. First published in *The Pioneer* in 1843.
- [Rappaccini's Daughter](rappaccinis-daughter) — In Padua the student Giovanni Guasconti falls in love with Beatrice, raised by her father among poisonous plants until she has become poisonous herself. First published in the *Democratic Review* in 1844.
- [The Great Stone Face](great-stone-face) — Ernest grows up in a mountain valley waiting for the man foretold to resemble a great face formed in the rock, while a rich merchant, a general, a statesman, and a poet are each hailed in turn. Published in 1850 and collected in *The Snow-Image, and Other Twice-Told Tales*.
- [The Scarlet Letter](scarlet-letter) — In Puritan Boston of the 1640s, Hester Prynne is made to wear a scarlet "A" for adultery, while her partner, the minister Arthur Dimmesdale, keeps his secret and her husband, Roger Chillingworth, hunts it out.
- [The House of the Seven Gables](house-of-the-seven-gables) — The Pyncheon family lives in a Salem house built on land taken from Matthew Maule, executed for witchcraft, whose curse is said to follow them. Written at Lenox and published in 1851.
