Jerome K. Jerome (1859–1927) was an English humorist, novelist, playwright and magazine editor. He is remembered above all for *Three Men in a Boat (To Say Nothing of the Dog)*, the comic account of a boating holiday on the Thames, which has never been out of print, and also wrote the essays *Idle Thoughts of an Idle Fellow* and the stage success *The Passing of the Third Floor Back*.

## Life

Jerome was born in Walsall on 2 May 1859, the fourth and last child of Jerome Clapp, who later took the name Jerome Clapp Jerome, and his wife Marguerite, née Jones. He was registered at birth as Jerome Clapp Jerome; the exiled Hungarian general György Klapka was staying with the family at the time, and Jerome later took Klapka as a middle name. His father, a nonconformist lay preacher, owned a coal mine in Staffordshire, and when it failed the family moved to Stourbridge and then to east London, where he set up, without much success, as an ironmonger. At nine the boy won a place at the Philological School in Lisson Grove, Marylebone, his only formal education, which he thought foolish and useless. His father died in 1871, and Jerome left school to support the family as a clerk for the London and North Western Railway.

Inspired by his sister Blandina's love of the theatre, he joined a struggling repertory company in 1877 and spent three years on the road as an actor. He then tried journalism, but most of what he wrote was rejected, and he worked in turn as a schoolmaster, a packer and a solicitor's clerk. Success came with *On the Stage – and Off* (1885), a comic memoir of his acting days published in weekly instalments in *The Play*, and the next year a one-act comedy of his was staged at the Globe Theatre in London. *Idle Thoughts of an Idle Fellow*, essays first printed in the magazine *Home Chimes*, sold exceptionally well, helped by its publisher Andrew Tuer, who bound it in pale lemon cloth and printed a new "edition" number on the cover with every thousand copies sold.

On 21 June 1888 Jerome married Georgina Marris at St Luke's Church, Chelsea, nine days after her divorce from an abusive first husband; she brought a young daughter, Elsie, and in 1897 they had a daughter, Rowena, who became an actress. Encouraged by his wife, he gave up his clerk's post to write full time. He proposed a trip up the Thames in a hired skiff with two friends, the bank clerk George Wingrave and the printer Carl Hentschel, and took a notebook in case it furnished a book. The result, *Three Men in a Boat* (1889), was scorned by reviewers and bought in enormous numbers by the public.

In 1892 Jerome became co-editor, with Robert Barr, of the new illustrated monthly *The Idler*, which published Mark Twain, Robert Louis Stevenson, Rudyard Kipling and Arthur Conan Doyle, and in 1893 he founded the weekly *To-Day*. In 1897 he lost a libel action brought by the inventor Sampson Fox over an article in *To-Day*; the damages were a farthing, but his legal costs of £9,000 forced him to sell his interest in both magazines. He went on writing for the stage throughout the 1890s, though most of his plays were never published.

*Three Men on the Bummel* (1900) took George, Harris and J. on a cycling tour of Germany. The semi-autobiographical novel *Paul Kelver* (1902) won the critical praise he had long been denied, and he regarded it as his best work. *The Passing of the Third Floor Back*, a modern morality play first produced at the St James's Theatre with Johnston Forbes-Robertson in the lead, was his greatest theatrical success. Jerome made lecture tours of the United States in 1908 and 1914, and in the First World War, too old for the British army, he served as an ambulance driver with the French army. His autobiography, *My Life and Times*, appeared in 1926. In 1927, in poor health, he set out on a long motoring tour of England, was taken ill, and died in hospital in Northampton on 14 June 1927. His ashes were buried in the churchyard of St Mary's Church, Ewelme, in Oxfordshire.

## Writing

Jerome's humour was colloquial and of its time, mixing light-hearted satire, sharp dialogue and slapstick, and it was this everyday voice that critics condemned. Reviewers called *Three Men in a Boat* vulgar, *Punch* dubbed its author "'Arry J. 'Arry," and Jerome later wrote that for twenty years he was "the best abused author in England." The public loved the book for the same reasons. It was first planned as a serious guide to the river, but the comedy took over, and the book is remembered for set pieces such as the plaster-of-Paris trout and the Irish stew.

The book sold 202,000 copies in Britain before its second edition in 1909, and Jerome was told that pirated American editions had sold another million. It was translated into many languages, and in Russia it became a standard school text; *Three Men on the Bummel* was long used as a school book in Germany. His later work won more praise from critics: several reviewers ranked *Paul Kelver* with the novels of Dickens, and Max Beerbohm, who called *The Passing of the Third Floor Back* "vilely stupid," admitted that he had seldom seen greater enthusiasm in a theatre. Jerome himself noted that, whatever cleverer or funnier books he wrote, the public persisted in remembering him as the author of *Three Men in a Boat*.
