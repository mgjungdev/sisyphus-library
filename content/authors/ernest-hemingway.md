Ernest Hemingway (1899–1961) was an American novelist, short-story writer and journalist whose economical, understated prose influenced generations of later writers. He is best known for the novels *The Sun Also Rises*, *A Farewell to Arms* and *For Whom the Bell Tolls* and the novella *The Old Man and the Sea*, and he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1954.

## Life

Ernest Miller Hemingway was born on July 21, 1899, in Oak Park, Illinois, a suburb of Chicago, the second of six children of Clarence Edmonds Hemingway, a physician, and Grace Hall Hemingway, a musician. His mother taught him the cello, and his father taught him to hunt, fish and camp during the family's summers at Walloon Lake in northern Michigan, the beginning of a lifelong love of the outdoors. At Oak Park and River Forest High School he boxed, played football and edited the school newspaper and yearbook. After graduating he spent six months as a cub reporter on *The Kansas City Star*, whose style guide ("Use short sentences... Use vigorous English") became a foundation of his prose.

Rejected by the army because of poor eyesight, he joined the American Red Cross as an ambulance driver and reached the Italian front in June 1918. On July 8, at Fossalta di Piave, he was badly wounded by mortar fire while bringing supplies to the front line; despite his wounds he helped Italian soldiers to safety and was decorated by Italy. While recovering in Milan he fell in love with Agnes von Kurowsky, a Red Cross nurse, who later broke off their relationship. Back home he wrote for the *Toronto Star*, and in Chicago he met the novelist Sherwood Anderson. On September 3, 1921, he married Hadley Richardson, and on Anderson's advice the couple moved to Paris, where Hemingway worked as a foreign correspondent for the *Toronto Star*.

In Paris he came to know Gertrude Stein, who became his mentor, Ezra Pound and James Joyce, and he covered the Greco-Turkish War. In December 1922 Hadley lost a suitcase holding almost all his manuscripts at the Gare de Lyon. His first book, *Three Stories and Ten Poems*, appeared in Paris in 1923, the year his son John was born in Toronto. Returning to Paris in 1924, he helped Ford Madox Ford edit *The Transatlantic Review*, and his story collection *In Our Time* won praise for its crisp, declarative style. In 1925 he went with a group of expatriate friends to the festival of San Fermín in Pamplona, Spain, and began *The Sun Also Rises* on his birthday a few days after it ended. Scribner's published the novel in October 1926. He and Hadley were divorced in January 1927, and in May he married Pauline Pfeiffer, who had come to Paris to work for *Vogue*.

In 1928 he and Pauline moved to Key West, Florida. That December his father killed himself. *A Farewell to Arms*, drawn on his war experience, appeared in 1929 and established him as a major American writer. In the 1930s he wrote about bullfighting in *Death in the Afternoon*, went on safari in East Africa, and published *Green Hills of Africa* and *To Have and Have Not*. In 1937 he went to Spain to report on the Civil War, and his novel of that war, *For Whom the Bell Tolls* (1940), was a great success. He settled at Finca Vigía near Havana, and in 1940 married the journalist Martha Gellhorn. In the Second World War he was a correspondent in Europe, watched the Normandy landings from a landing craft and was present at the liberation of Paris. In 1946 he married Mary Welsh.

After the poorly received *Across the River and into the Trees* (1950), *The Old Man and the Sea* (1952) won the Pulitzer Prize. In January 1954 he survived two plane crashes in Africa that left him in pain and ill health, and later that year he was awarded the Nobel Prize. He left Cuba for the last time in July 1960 and settled in Ketchum, Idaho. Suffering from depression and paranoia, he was treated at the Mayo Clinic with electroconvulsive therapy. On July 2, 1961, he shot himself at his home in Ketchum. His memoir of Paris, *A Moveable Feast*, and several unfinished novels were published after his death.

## Writing

Like Mark Twain and Stephen Crane before him, Hemingway was a journalist before he was a novelist. His prose is spare and plain: most of his sentences are simple, without subordinate clauses, and he often links clauses with "and" instead of commas. He called his method the "iceberg theory," in which the facts float above the surface while the deeper meaning lies out of sight; as he wrote, "If you leave out important things or events that you know about, the story is strengthened." His aim was not to eliminate emotion but to convey it through action, dialogue and images rather than stating it outright.

His recurring themes are love, war, travel, expatriation, wilderness and loss, and he often wrote about bullfighting, hunting and fishing. *The Sun Also Rises* made famous Gertrude Stein's phrase "Lost Generation" for the young people damaged by the First World War, though Hemingway insisted that his characters were "battered" but not lost. In 1926 *The New York Times* praised its "lean, hard, athletic narrative prose that puts more literary English to shame." The Swedish Academy awarded him the Nobel Prize "for his mastery of the art of narrative, most recently demonstrated in The Old Man and the Sea, and for the influence that he has exerted on contemporary style." Many later writers imitated his manner, and Saul Bellow mocked his followers' suppression of feeling as "Do you have emotions? Strangle them."
