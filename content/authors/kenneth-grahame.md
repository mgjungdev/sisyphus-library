Kenneth Grahame (1859–1932) was a Scottish writer and essayist who spent his working life at the Bank of England. He is best remembered for *The Wind in the Willows*, a classic of children's literature, and for his books about childhood, *The Golden Age* and *Dream Days*.

## Life

Kenneth Grahame was born on 8 March 1859 at 32 Castle Street, Edinburgh, the son of James Cunningham Grahame, an advocate, and Elizabeth Ingles. When he was a little over a year old his father became sheriff-substitute in Argyllshire, and the family moved to Inveraray on Loch Fyne. In 1864 his mother died of scarlet fever soon after the birth of his younger brother Roland; Kenneth caught the disease too and, though he recovered, remained prone to chest infections all his life. The four children were sent to their maternal grandmother at The Mount, a large house at Cookham Dean in Berkshire, where an uncle took them boating on the Thames. Their father, who had taken to drink, recalled them to Scotland briefly, then gave up his post, went to live in France, and had no further contact with them.

In 1868 Grahame became a boarder at St Edward's School, Oxford, where he won prizes, captained the rugby fifteen and became head boy. He hoped to go on to Oxford University, but his uncle John Grahame, who supported the children, refused to pay for it, and he went instead to work as a clerk in his uncle's firm of parliamentary agents. On 1 January 1879 he entered the Bank of England as a "gentleman clerk," having scored the highest marks of his intake in the entrance examination. He stayed at the Bank for nearly thirty years and rose to become its Secretary, one of its three highest officers, in 1898.

The Bank left him time to write. His first published piece appeared in the *St James's Gazette* in December 1888, and the poet W. E. Henley made him a regular contributor to the *National Observer*. At Henley's suggestion he sent a collection of essays and sketches to John Lane at The Bodley Head, which published it as *Pagan Papers*, and he became a contributor to Lane's periodical *The Yellow Book*. *The Golden Age* (1895), stories of a family of children ruled by grown-ups whom they call the Olympians, made him famous, and its sequel, *Dream Days*, followed in 1898.

On 22 July 1899 Grahame married Elspeth (Elsie) Thomson at Fowey in Cornwall; his cousin, the novelist Anthony Hope, was best man. Their only child, Alastair, was born in 1900, blind in one eye. Grahame told him bedtime stories about a mole, a beaver and a water rat, and in letters of 1907 added stories about a toad. In 1903 a man entered the Bank and fired three shots at Grahame, all of which missed, a shock from which he never fully recovered. He retired from the Bank in 1908, aged forty-nine, officially on grounds of ill health. *The Wind in the Willows* was published four months later.

In 1910 the family moved to a farmhouse at Blewbury, near Oxford. Alastair went up to Christ Church, Oxford, in 1918, and on 7 May 1920 his body was found on the railway line near Oxford; the inquest returned a verdict of accidental death. The Grahames spent several years travelling in Italy and then settled at Church Cottage, Pangbourne, on the Thames, where Kenneth Grahame died of a cerebral haemorrhage on 6 July 1932. He was buried at Pangbourne, and his body was later moved to Holywell Cemetery in Oxford to lie beside his son's. He left the royalties from his works to the Bodleian Library, which also holds his archive.

## Writing

Grahame wrote only a few books, and most of his early work took the form of short essays and sketches for periodicals. *The Golden Age* and *Dream Days* look back on childhood from the children's side, with the adults shown as remote and out of touch with the real griefs and pleasures of the young. *The Golden Age* established Grahame as an authority on childhood, and Algernon Swinburne called it "well-nigh too praiseworthy for praise." *Dream Days* was also well received, although it has often been judged a thinner sequel except for "The Reluctant Dragon," which has become his best-known short story. His biographer Peter Green read its two heroes as two sides of the author: St George the respectable establishment figure, the dragon his anarchic and artistic self.

*The Wind in the Willows*, built from the stories told to his son, follows Mole, Rat, Toad and Badger in their life along the river. The book was turned down by Grahame's usual publisher and most reviews were unfavourable, though Richard Middleton in *Vanity Fair* called it "the best book ever written for children and one of the best written for adults." It sold well and went on selling; Theodore Roosevelt wrote to Grahame that he had read it and reread it, and E. H. Shepard's illustrations of 1931 became the best known.
