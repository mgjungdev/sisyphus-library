Sherwood Anderson (1876–1941) was an American short-story writer and novelist who left a career in business to write about the hidden lives of small-town and Midwestern people. He is best known for *Winesburg, Ohio*, a cycle of linked stories, and his plain, unsentimental style influenced a younger generation of American writers, among them William Faulkner and Ernest Hemingway.

## Life

Sherwood Anderson was born on 13 September 1876 in Camden, Ohio, the third of seven children of Irwin McLain Anderson, a former Union soldier and harness-maker, and Emma Smith. The family left Camden before he was a year old and, after several moves, settled in Clyde, Ohio. He attended school only irregularly and left at fourteen, taking whatever work he could find as a newsboy, errand boy and bicycle assembler, which earned him the nickname "Jobby." His mother died of tuberculosis in 1895.

Anderson went to Chicago, where he worked in a warehouse and attended the Lewis Institute. He enlisted in the army during the Spanish-American War and reached Cuba early in 1899, after the fighting had ended. He then studied at Wittenberg Academy in Springfield, Ohio, from which he graduated in 1900, and returned to Chicago as an advertising copywriter; his first professional article, "The Farmer Wears Clothes," appeared in *Agricultural Advertising* in 1902. In 1904 he married Cornelia Lane, with whom he had two sons and a daughter. He became president of the United Factories Company in Cleveland in 1906, and in 1907 he set up his own business in Elyria, Ohio, selling a roof paint under the name "Roof-Fix."

On 28 November 1912 Anderson walked out of his Elyria office after dictating a distracted business letter. On 1 December he was found in a Cleveland drugstore, confused and unable to remember who he was, and was taken to hospital. He later told the story as a deliberate break with business and materialism, but biographers have described the episode as a fugue state. Early in 1913 he returned to Chicago and to advertising work, and turned seriously to writing.

His first novels, *Windy McPherson's Son* (1916) and *Marching Men* (1917), were followed by a book of poems, *Mid-American Chants* (1918), and in 1919 by *Winesburg, Ohio*, which made his reputation. In 1916 he divorced Cornelia and married Tennessee Mitchell. *Poor White* (1920), the story collections *The Triumph of the Egg* (1921) and *Horses and Men* (1923), and the novel *Many Marriages* (1923) followed. In 1921 he met Gertrude Stein in Paris. In 1924 he married Elizabeth Prall and moved to New Orleans, where he lived in the Pontalba Apartments and befriended the young William Faulkner. *Dark Laughter* (1925) was the only best-seller of his lifetime, and *Tar: A Midwest Childhood* (1926) drew on his own boyhood.

Anderson then settled in the mountains of southwestern Virginia, where he built a house, Ripshin, near Troutdale. In 1927 he bought the two weekly newspapers of nearby Marion, the Republican *Smyth County News* and the *Marion Democrat*, and filled them with editorials, columns and sketches, some collected in *Hello Towns!* (1929). He grew interested in the social and economic conditions of the region and of industrial workers, writing *Beyond Desire* (1932) and *Puzzled America*. After his third marriage ended in divorce, he married Eleanor Copenhaver in 1933. In 1941 he was a founding member of The Free Company, a radio project. He died on 8 March 1941 in Colón, Panama, of peritonitis caused by a toothpick he had swallowed accidentally, and was buried in Round Hill Cemetery in Marion under the epitaph "Life, Not Death, Is the Great Adventure."

## Writing

Rather than plot and action, Anderson emphasized mood and inner life, using "a simple, precise, unsentimental style to reveal the frustration, loneliness, and longing in the lives of his characters." He was among the first American writers to bring the ideas of Freudian psychology into fiction. *Winesburg, Ohio* drew on memories of Clyde, though Anderson said he took the hint for almost every character from his fellow lodgers in a Chicago rooming house. The book is a sequence of separate stories held together by one town and by the figure of George Willard, and it was dedicated to the memory of his mother, whose observation of life had awakened his own hunger to see beneath its surface. It sold slowly at first, about 3,000 copies by 1921. His later journalism in Marion included pieces written in the voice of an invented character, Buck Fever.

On its publication Hart Crane wrote that "America should read this book on her knees," and H. L. Mencken praised its "remarkable writing," while other reviewers objected to its "sordid" subject matter. Anderson's directness and his attention to ordinary, inarticulate people made him an important influence on younger writers, among them William Faulkner and Ernest Hemingway. In 1998 the Modern Library ranked *Winesburg, Ohio* twenty-fourth among the hundred best English-language novels of the twentieth century.

## In this library

- [Winesburg, Ohio](winesburg-ohio) — In the small Ohio town of Winesburg, the young reporter George Willard grows up among lonely and thwarted townspeople, the "grotesques" whose stories are told one by one, until he leaves the town for the city.
