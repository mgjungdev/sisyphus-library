Emily Brontë (1818–1848) was an English novelist and poet, the sister of Charlotte and Anne Brontë. She is best known for her only novel, *Wuthering Heights*, published under the pen name Ellis Bell, which puzzled and shocked many of its first reviewers but is now considered a classic of English literature. Her poems, first printed in the sisters' joint volume of 1846, are also widely admired.

## Life

Emily Jane Brontë was born on 30 July 1818 at Thornton, near Bradford in Yorkshire, the fifth of six children of Patrick Brontë, a curate from a poor Irish family, and Maria Branwell, who came from Penzance in Cornwall. In 1820, soon after the birth of the youngest child, Anne, the family moved to Haworth, a village on the moors, where Patrick became perpetual curate. Maria Brontë died on 15 September 1821, when Emily was three, and her sister Elizabeth Branwell stayed on to bring up the children.

In 1824 Emily, then six, was sent with her three older sisters to the Clergy Daughters' School at Cowan Bridge, where food was poor and disease frequent. After the two eldest, Maria and Elizabeth, fell ill and died in 1825, Charlotte and Emily were brought home and thereafter educated at the parsonage, where their father let them read freely from his library and from newspapers and magazines. The children wrote stories about imaginary kingdoms, first Glass Town and Angria; when Emily was about thirteen, she and Anne, who were very close, began a world of their own called Gondal, which absorbed them into adulthood. Emily learned to play the piano well, drew and painted, and loved the moors and animals, especially her dog, Keeper.

At seventeen she went as a pupil to Roe Head School, where Charlotte was teaching, but she was so homesick that she left after a few months, and Anne took her place. In September 1838 she became a teacher at Law Hill School near Halifax, where the long working days damaged her health, and she returned home in April 1839. There she helped the family servant with the housework, taught herself German and continued to write poems.

In 1842 Emily went with Charlotte to the Pensionnat Heger in Brussels to improve their languages, so that they might one day open a school. She disliked the city and the other pupils mocked her old-fashioned dress, but Constantin Heger thought her intellect even greater than Charlotte's, saying that "she should have been a man – a great navigator." She also taught music there. When their aunt died in the autumn of 1842 the sisters came home, and Emily stayed at Haworth to run the household while Charlotte went back to Brussels. In 1844 the sisters' attempt to open a school at the parsonage failed for want of pupils.

In February 1844 Emily began copying her poems into two notebooks, one of them labelled "Gondal Poems." In the autumn of 1845 Charlotte discovered them and insisted they be published; Emily, angered at the invasion of her privacy, gave way when Anne revealed poems of her own. *Poems by Currer, Ellis, and Acton Bell* was published at the sisters' own expense by Aylott & Jones in 1846, with twenty-one poems by Emily. Only two copies were sold, though one reviewer singled out Ellis Bell's verse for its power. The sisters then offered three novels to publishers, and Thomas Cautley Newby accepted Emily's *Wuthering Heights* and Anne's *Agnes Grey*, which appeared together in December 1847, two months after Charlotte's *Jane Eyre*.

Emily kept her pseudonym to protect her privacy, and few of her letters survive. Her brother Branwell died on 24 September 1848, and at his funeral she caught a severe cold that turned to an inflammation of the lungs, and her health failed rapidly. She refused medical help, saying she would have "no poisoning doctor" near her, until it was too late. She died at the parsonage on 19 December 1848, aged thirty, and was buried in the family vault in the church at Haworth. In 1850 Charlotte edited a second edition of *Wuthering Heights*, adding a biographical notice that revealed her sister's identity.

## Writing

*Wuthering Heights* is a Gothic story of love, possession and revenge, influenced by Romanticism and by the Gondal writings, and told through layered narrators, the tenant Lockwood and the housekeeper Nelly Dean. The story moves across two generations and between two houses, the old farmhouse of Wuthering Heights and the more genteel Thrushcross Grange, and ends with the reconciliation of the younger Cathy and Hareton Earnshaw. Its unusual structure, violence and passion bewildered the first reviewers, many of whom assumed the author was a man; one called the book "wild, confused, disjointed, and improbable" and its characters "savages ruder than those who lived before the days of Homer." It was controversial for its depiction of cruelty and its challenges to Victorian morality, religion and class.

After her death Charlotte presented Emily as a solitary "nursling of the moors" who "did not know what she had done," an image that long shaped her reputation. Later readers reversed the verdict on her work. The 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* described the novel as "a monument of intensity owing nothing to tradition," and ranked her poems "Old Stoic" and "Last Lines" among the finest poetry any woman had written in English. *Wuthering Heights* has been called the "twentieth century's favourite nineteenth-century novel," and it has been adapted many times for film, stage, radio, television and music; poems such as "No coward soul is mine" remain widely read.
