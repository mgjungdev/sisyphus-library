Baroness Orczy (1865–1947), born Emma Orczy in Hungary, was a British novelist and playwright who wrote historical romances and detective stories. She is best known for *The Scarlet Pimpernel*, whose hero, an apparently foolish English baronet who secretly rescues French aristocrats from the guillotine, helped to establish the figure of the hero with a secret identity.

## Life

Emma Magdalena Rozália Mária Jozefa Borbála Orczy was born on 23 September 1865 at Tarnaörs, in Hungary, the daughter of Baron Félix Orczy, a composer, and Countess Emma Wass. In 1868 her parents left their country estate for Budapest, fearing a peasant revolt, and the family afterwards lived in Budapest, Brussels and Paris, where she studied music without success. In 1880, when she was fourteen, the family moved to London and lodged with a fellow Hungarian in Great Portland Street. She studied at the West London School of Art and then at Heatherley's School of Fine Art, and some of her paintings were later exhibited at the Royal Academy.

At art school she met Montagu Barstow, an illustrator and the son of an English clergyman, and they were married at St Marylebone parish church on 7 November 1894. She later described their marriage as "for close on half a century, one of perfect happiness and understanding." They had little money, and she worked with her husband as a translator and illustrator. Their only child, John Montague Orczy-Barstow, was born on 25 February 1899. She began to write soon afterwards, but her first novel, *The Emperor's Candlesticks* (1899), failed. She found readers with detective stories in *The Royal Magazine*, where the armchair detective known as the Old Man in the Corner first appeared in 1901, and her next novel, *In Mary's Reign* (1901), did better.

She conceived Sir Percy Blakeney, the Scarlet Pimpernel, while standing on a platform of the London Underground. She wrote the novel in five weeks, but most London publishers turned it down. Meanwhile she and her husband wrote a play on the same story, which the actors Fred Terry and Julia Neilson accepted. It opened at the Theatre Royal in Nottingham on 15 October 1903 without success, but with a rewritten last act it opened at the New Theatre in London on 5 January 1905. Critics thought it old-fashioned, but it became one of the most popular shows staged in Britain and was eventually given more than 2,000 times. The novel, published after the play, was an immediate success.

The Pimpernel made Orczy wealthy. She and her husband lived in Kent and in London, and later bought the Villa Bijou in Monte Carlo. Over the next thirty-five years she wrote more than a dozen sequels, beginning with *I Will Repay* (1906), the most popular of them, and ending with *Mam'zelle Guillotine* (1940). She also published the detective stories of *Lady Molly of Scotland Yard* (1910) and many other historical romances, and she was a founding member of the Detection Club in 1930. None of her later plays matched the success of *The Scarlet Pimpernel*.

Orczy believed strongly in the aristocracy and supported British imperialism. During the First World War she founded the Women of England's Active Service League, which asked women to pledge "to persuade every man I know to offer his service to his country"; some 20,000 women joined. She spent the Second World War in Monte Carlo, where her husband died in 1942, and there, alone and unable to travel, she wrote her memoir, *Links in the Chain of Life* (1947). She was able to return to England only after the war. She died at Henley-on-Thames, Oxfordshire, on 12 November 1947.

## Writing

Orczy's novels are mannered, fast-moving melodramas, most often historical, and the critic Mary Cadogan called them "highly wrought and intensely atmospheric." The Scarlet Pimpernel books are set mainly during the Reign of Terror. Their hero hides behind the public face of a slow-witted, foppish playboy, works with a band of loyal followers, the League of the Scarlet Pimpernel, and leaves behind a small red flower as his calling card. His disguises, his signature weapon, his skill in outwitting his enemies and his calling card became standard conventions of later superheroes. Zorro, the Shadow, Superman and Batman followed within a few decades, and Stan Lee called the Pimpernel "the first character who could be called a superhero."

Her detective fiction was also influential. The Old Man in the Corner, who solves crimes by pure reasoning while sitting in a London tea shop and tying knots in a piece of string, was one of the first armchair detectives created in the wake of Sherlock Holmes, and Lady Molly of Scotland Yard was an early example of a woman detective as the central character. The play of *The Scarlet Pimpernel* was performed in France, Italy, Germany and Spain, and the novel was translated into sixteen languages and has been adapted many times for film, television, radio and the musical stage.

## In this library
