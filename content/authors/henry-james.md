Henry James (1843–1916) was an American-born novelist, short story writer and critic who spent most of his adult life in Europe and became a British subject the year before his death. His fiction turns on the meeting of Americans and Europeans and on the inner lives of his characters, traced through a point of view that grew more intricate as his career went on. *Daisy Miller* made his name in 1878, and *The Turn of the Screw* remains his most widely adapted work.

## Life

James was born on April 15, 1843, at 21 Washington Place in Manhattan, New York City, the son of Henry James Sr., a lecturer and philosopher with an inherited income, and Mary Walsh. His elder brother was the philosopher and psychologist William James; there were two younger brothers, Garth Wilkinson and Robertson, and a sister, the diarist Alice James. Before he was a year old the family went to Europe, returning to New York in 1845. Between 1855 and 1860 they moved between London, Paris, Geneva, Boulogne-sur-Mer, Bonn and Newport, Rhode Island, following the father's interests. Henry was taught mostly by tutors, felt at home in France, and became fluent in French.

Back in Newport in 1860, he became friends with Thomas Sergeant Perry and the painter John La Farge, who introduced him to French literature and to Balzac, whom James later called his "greatest master." In the autumn of 1861 he was injured while fighting a fire, and the injury left him unfit for service in the Civil War, in which his two younger brothers fought. He spent a short time at Harvard Law School but found he cared more for literature. After the family moved to Boston in 1864 he came to know William Dean Howells and Charles Eliot Norton, and James T. Fields and Annie Adams Fields became his first professional mentors. He wrote reviews, sketches and stories for *The Nation* and the *Atlantic Monthly*, which serialized his first novel, *Watch and Ward*, in 1871.

On a fourteen-month journey through Europe in 1869–70 he met John Ruskin, Charles Dickens, Matthew Arnold, William Morris and George Eliot, and in Rome he wrote to his brother: "At last—for the first time—I live!" *Transatlantic Sketches*, *A Passionate Pilgrim* and *Roderick Hudson* appeared in 1874 and 1875. In the autumn of 1875 he moved to Paris, where he met Flaubert, Zola, Daudet, Maupassant and Turgenev, and a year later he settled in London. *The American* (1877), *The Europeans* (1878) and a study of Hawthorne (1879) followed, and *Daisy Miller* (1878) made him famous on both sides of the Atlantic. *The Portrait of a Lady* appeared in 1881. His mother died in January 1882 and his father at the end of that year.

In 1886 he published *The Bostonians* and *The Princess Casamassima*, both shaped by his reading of the French realists. Their poor sales and reviews, he told Howells, had "reduced the desire, and demand, for my productions to zero." He spent a long stay in Italy in 1887 and published *The Aspern Papers* and *The Reverberator* in 1888. From 1890 he tried hard to succeed in the London theatre, but the opening night of his drama *Guy Domville*, on January 5, 1895, ended with hissing from the gallery as he took his bow, and he soon gave up the stage. These years also brought the deaths of his sister Alice in 1892 and of Robert Louis Stevenson and Constance Fenimore Woolson in 1894.

In 1897 he took a lease on Lamb House in Rye, Sussex, where he lived until 1914, and there he wrote *The Turn of the Screw* (1898). His last great novels, *The Wings of the Dove* (1902), *The Ambassadors* (1903) and *The Golden Bowl* (1904), followed. He revisited the United States in 1904, recording the journey in *The American Scene* (1907), and he revised his fiction and wrote new prefaces for the twenty-four-volume New York Edition. William James died in 1910, with Henry near him. James wrote the memoirs *A Small Boy and Others* and *Notes of a Son and Brother*, did war work after 1914, and became a British citizen in 1915. He never married. He received the Order of Merit in 1916 and died on February 28 of that year in Chelsea, London. He was cremated at Golders Green, and his ashes were buried in the family plot in Cambridge Cemetery, Massachusetts.

## Writing

James's central subject was the encounter between the New World and the Old: Americans, often young women, confronting a European society that is cultivated, alluring and sometimes corrupt. He narrowed his attention more and more to consciousness, presenting events as they are perceived by one character, and the 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* observed that he "early fell in love with the 'point of view.'" Philip Guedalla joked that his prose passed through three phases, "James I, James II, and The Old Pretender." The early style is direct; the late style, dictated to a typist from the late 1890s, is known for long sentences, double negatives and deferred verbs. In "The Art of Fiction" (1884) he argued for the novelist's freedom of subject and method, and he wrote criticism, travel books, plays, and more than ten thousand of his letters survive.

His contemporaries divided over him. Howells wrote that the new realism found "its chief exemplar in Mr. James." Oscar Wilde said he wrote "fiction as if it were a painful duty," Edith Wharton found some late passages all but incomprehensible, and H. G. Wells compared him to a hippopotamus trying to pick up a pea. He was nominated for the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1911, 1912 and 1916. After his death some American critics, Van Wyck Brooks among them, resented his expatriation, but F. R. Leavis placed him in *The Great Tradition* (1948) as a novelist of "established pre-eminence." His focus on the minds of his characters anticipated much of twentieth-century fiction. Jorge Luis Borges wrote: "I know of no stranger work than that of Henry James."

## In this library

- [Daisy Miller](daisy-miller) — In Vevey and then Rome, the expatriate Frederick Winterbourne is drawn to the frank young American Daisy Miller, whose freedom with an Italian companion scandalizes the American colony. First published in *The Cornhill Magazine* in June and July 1878, and as a book in 1879.
- [Washington Square](washington-square) — In New York in the 1840s and 1850s, the shy heiress Catherine Sloper is courted by the charming Morris Townsend, whom her father, Dr. Austin Sloper, believes is after her money. Published in 1880, from a story told to James by the actress Fanny Kemble.
- [The Aspern Papers](aspern-papers) — An unnamed narrator takes rooms in a Venetian palazzo to get at the letters of the dead poet Jeffrey Aspern kept by his aged former lover Juliana Bordereau and her niece. Serialized in three parts in the *Atlantic Monthly* from March to May 1888 and published as a book the same year.
- [The Turn of the Screw](turn-of-the-screw) — A governess in charge of two orphaned children, Miles and Flora, at the country house of Bly becomes convinced that the ghosts of two former servants are haunting them. Serialized in *Collier's Weekly* from January 27 to April 16, 1898, and collected that October in *The Two Magics*.
