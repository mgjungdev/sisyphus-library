Hans Christian Andersen (1805–1875) was a Danish writer of plays, travel books, novels and poems. He is best remembered for his literary fairy tales, 156 stories in all, which have been translated into more than 125 languages. Among the most famous are "The Emperor's New Clothes," "The Little Mermaid," "The Ugly Duckling" and "The Snow Queen."

## Life

Andersen was born in Odense, on the Danish island of Funen, on 2 April 1805. His father, also named Hans, was a shoemaker who read the *Arabian Nights* to his son; his mother, Anne Marie Andersdatter, was an illiterate washerwoman, and the family lived and slept in one small room. After his father died in 1816 the boy built himself a toy theatre, sewed clothes for his puppets and read every play he could borrow, among them those of Holberg and Shakespeare. He had little schooling and worked as an apprentice to a weaver and then to a tailor.

In September 1819, at fourteen, he set out for Copenhagen, hoping to become a singer and actor. He was nearly reduced to starvation before the musicians Christoph Weyse and Siboni and the poet Frederik Hoëgh Guldberg took him up, and he found a place at the Royal Theatre until his voice changed. Jonas Collin, a director of the Royal Theatre, became his lifelong friend and patron, and with support from King Frederick VI sent him to the grammar school at Slagelse and later to a school at Elsinore. Andersen remained there until 1827 and called those years the darkest and bitterest of his life. Before leaving for school he had published his first book, *The Ghost at Palnatoke's Grave* (1822).

His first real success came in 1829 with the fantastic tale *A Journey on Foot from Holmen's Canal to the East Point of Amager*, followed by a play and a volume of poems. A small travel grant from the king in 1833 sent him on the first of many European journeys; he wrote "Agnete and the Merman" in the Swiss Jura and reached Rome in October 1834. His first novel, *The Improvisatore*, a fictionalised autobiography drawn from his Italian travels, appeared in 1835 and was immediately successful. The same year the publisher C. A. Reitzel issued the first booklet of *Fairy Tales Told for Children*. A third booklet, containing "The Little Mermaid" and "The Emperor's New Clothes," followed on 7 April 1837, the year of his novel *Only a Fiddler*. New series of tales began in 1838 and 1845, and by the mid-1840s he was celebrated throughout Europe, though Denmark itself still showed some resistance to his pretensions.

Andersen never married. His unrequited loves included Riborg Voigt, whose letter was found in a pouch on his chest when he died, Louise Collin, daughter of his patron, and the Swedish singer Jenny Lind, whom he met in 1840. His letters to Edvard Collin also express a passionate attachment. From 1845 to 1864 he lived at Nyhavn 67 in Copenhagen. In June 1847 he made his first visit to England, where he met Charles Dickens at a party given by the Countess of Blessington; when he left, Dickens saw him off from Ramsgate pier. A second visit in 1857, when Andersen stretched a short stay at Dickens's home at Gads Hill Place into five weeks, ended the friendship, and Dickens stopped answering his letters.

He also wrote travel books, among them *A Poet's Bazaar* (1842) and *In Spain* (1863). In 1868 Horace Scudder, editor of the American *Riverside Magazine for Young People*, offered him $500 for twelve new stories, and ten of the tales the magazine printed appeared there before they were published in Denmark. His fairy tales continued to appear in instalments until Christmas 1872. In 1872 he fell out of bed and was badly hurt, and he never fully recovered; signs of liver cancer followed. He died on 4 August 1875 at Rolighed, the country house near Copenhagen of his friends Moritz Melchior and his wife, and was buried in the Assistens Kirkegård in Copenhagen. By then he was internationally revered, and the Danish government paid him an annual stipend as a "national treasure."

## Writing

Andersen called his tales *Eventyr og Historier*, "fairy tales and stories." The first three tales of 1835, among them "The Tinderbox" and "The Princess and the Pea," retold folktales he had heard as a child, while "Little Ida's Flowers" was his own invention. He drew on literary sources as well: "The Little Mermaid" answered Friedrich de la Motte Fouqué's *Undine*, and "The Emperor's New Clothes" adapted a medieval Spanish story, its ending revised on the eve of publication so that a child calls out that the Emperor is wearing no clothes. Danish reviewers of the first booklets in 1836 were not enthusiastic: they disliked the chatty, informal style and the apparent immorality, since children's books were expected to educate rather than amuse. "The Little Mermaid" established his international reputation, and the tales' fame then grew steadily across Europe. Writing for the 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, Edmund Gosse observed that Andersen "still desired to excel as a novelist and a dramatist, which he could not do, and he still disdained the enchanting Fairy Tales, in the composition of which his unique genius lay."

The ending of "The Little Mermaid," in which the mermaid becomes a daughter of the air who may earn a soul through good deeds, has long been debated. Andersen himself wrote in 1837 that, unlike Fouqué, "I have permitted my mermaid to follow a more natural, more divine path," while P. L. Travers objected to the passage telling children that their behaviour adds to or shortens her trial: "Andersen, this is blackmail." His travel books combine description with reflections on authorship, immortality and fiction. The tales have been adapted into ballets, operas, plays and animated and live-action films.

## In this library

- [The Little Mermaid](little-mermaid) — A young mermaid princess falls in love with a human prince and trades her voice to a sea witch for legs, hoping to win his love and with it an immortal soul. Written in 1836, it was first published by C. A. Reitzel in Copenhagen on 7 April 1837 in the third booklet of *Fairy Tales Told for Children*.
- [The Snow Queen](snow-queen) — Splinters of a troll's distorting mirror lodge in the heart and eye of the boy Kai, who is carried off by the Snow Queen, and his friend Gerda sets out alone to find him. Told in seven parts, it was first published on 21 December 1844 in *New Fairy Tales. First Volume. Second Collection*.
- [The Marsh King's Daughter](marsh-kings-daughter) — Storks nesting on a Viking's castle in Denmark tell the story of an Egyptian princess dragged down into the marsh by the Marsh King and of her daughter Helga, raised by the Viking's wife, who is beautiful and cruel by day and a gentle frog by night until a captive Christian priest changes her. It was first published on 15 May 1858 in *New Fairy Tales and Stories*.
