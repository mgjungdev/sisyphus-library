Anne Brontë (1820–1849) was an English novelist and poet, the youngest of the Brontë sisters, who published under the pen name Acton Bell. She is known for *Agnes Grey* (1847), drawn from her years as a governess, and *The Tenant of Wildfell Hall* (1848), often called one of the first feminist novels.

## Life

Anne Brontë was born on 17 January 1820 at the parsonage in Market Street, Thornton, on the outskirts of Bradford, the last of the six children of Patrick Brontë, an Irish clergyman, and Maria Branwell, the daughter of a merchant of Penzance in Cornwall. In April 1820 the family moved to the parsonage at Haworth, where her father had been appointed perpetual curate. Her mother died on 15 September 1821, and her mother's sister, Elizabeth Branwell, stayed on to bring up the children; Anne shared her aunt's room and was said to be her favourite. In 1825 her two eldest sisters, Maria and Elizabeth, died after falling ill at the Clergy Daughters' School at Cowan Bridge, and the surviving children were taught at home by their aunt and father.

The children read widely in their father's library and wrote stories, poems and plays about imaginary kingdoms. Around 1831 Anne and Emily, who were especially close, broke away from the Angrian world of Charlotte and Branwell to create their own, Gondal, which occupied them into adulthood. In October 1835 Anne took Emily's place as a pupil at Roe Head School, where Charlotte was teaching. She stayed two years, but in December 1837 she fell seriously ill, apparently in part from a religious crisis, and was brought home.

In April 1839, aged nineteen, she became governess to the children of the Ingham family at Blake Hall, near Mirfield. The children were unruly, she was not allowed to punish them, and after nine months she was dismissed. Her time there is believed to have been the main source of *Agnes Grey*. From 1840 to 1845 she was governess to the children of the Reverend Edmund Robinson and his wife Lydia at Thorp Green Hall, near York. She was homesick and at first unhappy there, but she made a success of the post, and her pupils became lifelong friends. With the Robinsons she spent summers at Scarborough, a place that became dear to her. In 1843 she found a post at Thorp Green for her brother Branwell as tutor to the Robinsons' son; he was later sent home in disgrace after becoming infatuated with Mrs Robinson. Anne resigned in June 1845.

That year Charlotte discovered Emily's poems, and Anne revealed that she too had been writing verse. With money left by their aunt, who had died in 1842, the sisters paid for *Poems by Currer, Ellis, and Acton Bell*, published in May 1846 with 21 poems by Anne. It received favourable reviews but sold only two copies in its first year. Anne's first novel, *Agnes Grey*, was accepted with Emily's *Wuthering Heights* by the publisher Thomas Cautley Newby, who required the authors to pay fifty pounds towards costs and brought the two out together in three volumes in 1847, after the success of Charlotte's *Jane Eyre*. Newby published *The Tenant of Wildfell Hall* in three volumes in June 1848. In July 1848 Anne and Charlotte went to London to show Charlotte's publisher, George Smith, that the "Bell brothers" were not one person.

Branwell died on 24 September 1848 and Emily on 19 December. Anne fell ill with influenza over Christmas, and in January 1849 a Leeds physician found advanced consumption. In May, hoping that sea air might help, she travelled to Scarborough with Charlotte and their friend Ellen Nussey. She died there on 28 May 1849, aged 29, and was buried in St Mary's churchyard below the castle walls.

## Writing

Anne Brontë's novels are realistic and draw on what she had seen. Charlotte thought "quiet description and simple pathos" her forte, and the critics Ada Harrison and Derek Stanford later called her the "first realist woman writer" in Britain. *Agnes Grey* records the humiliations of a governess in households of the gentry, and *The Tenant of Wildfell Hall* deals with drunkenness, the abuse of a wife and a woman's legal powerlessness in marriage. Some features of Arthur Huntingdon resemble her brother Branwell. In the preface to the second edition of *The Tenant* she defended her frankness: "When we have to do with vice and vicious characters, I maintain it is better to depict them as they really are than as they would wish to appear." She also rebuked reviewers who speculated on the author's sex, holding that "if a book is a good one, it is so whatever the sex of the author may be."

*The Tenant* sold well but shocked many reviewers. Charles Kingsley in *Fraser's Magazine* found it "a powerful and an interesting book" but blamed its "coarseness," and *The Spectator* saw in its writer "a morbid love for the coarse, not to say the brutal." After Anne's death Charlotte judged that the choice of subject had been "a mistake" and prevented the book's republication, and later critics paid her little attention; the 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* held that her two novels survived "principally through the exceeding vitality of the Brontë tradition." George Moore was an exception, calling *Agnes Grey* "the most perfect prose narrative in English letters." Since the mid-twentieth century biographers and critics have brought her a far larger reputation, and Stevie Davies called *The Tenant* "a feminist manifesto of revolutionary power and intelligence."

## In this library

- [Agnes Grey](agnes-grey) — Agnes, the daughter of a poor clergyman, goes out as a governess, first to the cruel and spoiled children of the Bloomfields and then to the selfish daughters of the Murrays, and finds consolation in the curate Edward Weston. It was published by Thomas Cautley Newby in 1847 as the third volume of a set whose first two held Emily Brontë's *Wuthering Heights*.
- [The Tenant of Wildfell Hall](tenant-of-wildfell-hall) — The farmer Gilbert Markham tells in letters to a friend how a mysterious young widow, Helen Graham, came with her small son to live at the old mansion of Wildfell Hall, and her diary reveals that she has fled a dissolute and abusive husband. It was published by Newby in three volumes in June 1848.
