G. K. Chesterton (1874–1936) was an English writer of essays, novels, short stories, poetry, criticism and Christian apologetics, one of the dominant figures of the London literary scene in the early twentieth century. He created the priest-detective Father Brown and wrote the novel *The Man Who Was Thursday*, and his wit and fondness for paradox earned him the name "prince of paradox."

## Life

Gilbert Keith Chesterton was born on 29 May 1874 at Campden Hill in Kensington, London, the son of Edward Chesterton, an estate agent, and Marie Louise Grosjean, who was of Swiss-French origin. He was baptised into the Church of England, though the family were irregularly practising Unitarians. With his younger brother Cecil he experimented with Ouija boards, and he later wrote that he had been a pagan at twelve and a complete agnostic by sixteen. He was educated at St Paul's School, where his friends included Edmund Clerihew Bentley, inventor of the clerihew, and then studied at the Slade School of Art, meaning to become an illustrator, while also taking literature classes at University College London. He completed no degree.

In September 1895 he went to work for the London publisher George Redway, and in October 1896 moved to T. Fisher Unwin, where he stayed until 1902, writing meanwhile as a freelance art and literary critic. His first books were collections of poems and essays, among them *The Wild Knight and Other Poems* (1900) and *The Defendant* (1901). In 1896 he met Frances Blogg, the eldest of seven children of a family of Huguenot origin, and they married at St Mary Abbots, Kensington, on 28 June 1901. Frances managed his appointments, accounts and dealings with publishers, and Chesterton credited her with leading him back to Anglicanism. They had no children.

In 1902 *The Daily News* gave him a weekly column, and from 1905 he wrote a weekly column for *The Illustrated London News* for the next thirty years. A stream of books followed, including the novel *The Napoleon of Notting Hill* (1904), *Heretics* (1905), *Charles Dickens: A Critical Study* (1906), *The Man Who Was Thursday* and *Orthodoxy* (both 1908). In 1909 the Chestertons moved to Beaconsfield, Buckinghamshire, where they lived for the rest of their lives. Father Brown first appeared in 1910, and the first collection, *The Innocence of Father Brown*, came out in 1911, the year of his long poem *The Ballad of the White Horse*. A large man, six feet four inches tall, usually wearing a cape and a crumpled hat, Chesterton enjoyed public debate with George Bernard Shaw, H. G. Wells, Bertrand Russell and Clarence Darrow. He was close to the poet and essayist Hilaire Belloc, and Shaw coined the name "Chesterbelloc" for the pair.

In the autumn of 1914 he fell seriously ill and was bedridden for months. When Cecil, who had edited *The New Witness*, enlisted in the army, Gilbert took over the paper; Cecil died in a military hospital in France in December 1918. In 1922 Chesterton was received into the Catholic Church, a conversion in which the Bradford priest John O'Connor, the loose model for Father Brown, had a part; Frances followed in 1926. In 1925 the paper was renamed *G. K.'s Weekly*, and it took much of his energy for the rest of his life. His later books included *Saint Francis of Assisi* (1923), *The Everlasting Man* (1925) and *Saint Thomas Aquinas* (1933). He became the first president of the Detection Club, serving from 1930 until his death, gave popular radio talks for the BBC from 1931, and was nominated for the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1935. Chesterton died of congestive heart failure at his home in Beaconsfield on 14 June 1936. Ronald Knox preached at his Requiem Mass in Westminster Cathedral, and he was buried in Beaconsfield.

## Writing

Chesterton wrote around 80 books, several hundred poems, some 200 short stories and about 4,000 essays, most of them newspaper columns. His manner was paradox: *Time* magazine observed that "whenever possible, Chesterton made his points with popular sayings, proverbs, allegories—first carefully turning them inside out." Christian themes run through his work long before his conversion, and he argued for tradition, against both capitalism and socialism, and for the "distributism" he shared with Belloc. *The Man Who Was Thursday* is the best known of his novels, but Father Brown, who appears only in short stories, is his most famous creation: a little Catholic priest who solves crimes by intuition and an understanding of human nature, and who seeks the criminal's repentance as much as his capture.

T. S. Eliot judged that *The Man Who Was Thursday* turned "the Stevensonian fantasy to more serious purpose," and called his *Dickens* "the best essay on that author that has ever been written." Kingsley Amis called *Thursday* "the most thrilling book I have ever read." *The Everlasting Man* contributed to C. S. Lewis's conversion, and Étienne Gilson thought his *Saint Thomas Aquinas* the best book ever written on its subject. Jorge Luis Borges, who said that "Chesterton knew how to make the most of a detective story," compared his work with Edgar Allan Poe's, and Neil Gaiman has named him as an influence.

## In this library

- [The Man Who Was Thursday](man-who-was-thursday) — Gabriel Syme, recruited at Scotland Yard to a secret anti-anarchist police corps, gets himself elected "Thursday" on the seven-man council of European anarchists, only to find that the others are not what they seem and that their president, Sunday, is stranger still. Subtitled *A Nightmare*, it was published in 1908 with a dedicatory poem to E. C. Bentley.
- [The Blue Cross](blue-cross) — Valentin, head of the Paris police, follows a trail of spilled soup, switched salt and smashed windows across London in pursuit of the master thief Flambeau, who is after a priceless cross carried by a little priest, Father Brown. The first Father Brown story, it appeared as "Valentin Follows a Curious Trail" in the *Saturday Evening Post* on 23 July 1910 and as "The Blue Cross" in *The Story-Teller* in September 1910.
- [The Hammer of God](hammer-of-god) — In the village of Bohun Beacon the dissolute Colonel Norman Bohun is found with his skull crushed by a small hammer outside the church where his brother, the devout Reverend Wilfred Bohun, is curate, and suspicion falls on the village blacksmith. It appeared as "The Bolt from the Blue" in the *Saturday Evening Post* on 5 November 1910 and was collected in *The Innocence of Father Brown* (1911).
