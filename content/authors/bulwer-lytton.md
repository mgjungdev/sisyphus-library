Edward Bulwer-Lytton (1803–1873) was an English novelist, poet, playwright and politician, one of the most popular and prolific writers of his day. He is remembered for novels such as *Pelham* and *The Last Days of Pompeii*, for the play *Money*, and for phrases like "the pen is mightier than the sword" and "It was a dark and stormy night."

## Life

Edward George Earle Lytton Bulwer was born in London on 25 May 1803, the youngest son of General William Earle Bulwer of Heydon Hall, Norfolk, and Elizabeth Barbara Lytton, daughter of Richard Warburton Lytton of Knebworth, Hertfordshire. His father died when he was four, and his mother settled in London. A delicate and unhappy boy at boarding schools, he found an admiring teacher in a Mr Wallington at Ealing, who encouraged him to publish, at fifteen, a volume called *Ishmael and other Poems*. In 1822 he entered Trinity College, Cambridge, and soon moved to Trinity Hall; in 1825 he won the Chancellor's medal for English verse with a poem on "Sculpture."

In August 1827, against his mother's wishes, he married Rosina Doyle Wheeler, an Irish beauty. His mother withdrew his allowance, and he set to work in earnest as a writer. His first novel, *Falkland* (1827), was only a moderate success, but *Pelham* (1828), with its intimate study of the dandyism of the age, made him famous. *The Disowned*, *Devereux*, *Paul Clifford* and *Eugene Aram* followed, and he reached the height of his popularity with *The Last Days of Pompeii* (1834) and *Rienzi* (1835). He edited the *New Monthly* in 1831 and 1832 and also wrote for the stage: *The Lady of Lyons* (1838), *Richelieu* (1839) and *Money* (1840) were all successful. His marriage meanwhile broke down, and the couple were legally separated in 1836. Rosina caricatured him in a novel, *Cheveley* (1839), and attacked him in public for years afterwards.

Bulwer entered Parliament in 1831 as member for St Ives, and from 1832 sat for Lincoln for nine years. A follower of Bentham, he spoke for the Reform Bill and worked for the reduction of the newspaper stamp duties. Lord Melbourne offered him a lordship of the Admiralty, which he declined so as to keep writing. He was made a baronet in 1838. After leaving Parliament in 1841 he spent much time in travel on the Continent, and in 1843 he published *The Last of the Barons*. When his mother died in 1843 he succeeded to the Knebworth estate and, under the terms of her will, added Lytton to his surname. Exhausted by overwork, he tried the water cure at Malvern and wrote about it in *Confessions of a Water Patient*.

In *The New Timon* (1846) he lampooned Tennyson, who answered in kind. *The Caxtons* (1849), published at first anonymously, began a new and more domestic line of fiction. In 1852 he returned to Parliament as a Conservative member for Hertfordshire, having differed with Lord John Russell over the corn laws. In June 1858, while he was standing for Hertfordshire, Rosina denounced him at the hustings; he had her placed under restraint as insane, and she was released a few weeks later. That year he entered Lord Derby's government as Secretary of State for the Colonies, and in that office he helped to organise the new colony of British Columbia, where the town of Lytton is named after him. In 1866 he was raised to the peerage as Baron Lytton of Knebworth.

His interest in the occult showed in *Zanoni* (1842), in the ghost story "The Haunted and the Haunters" (1859) and in *A Strange Story* (1862), and his late novel *The Coming Race* (1871) was published without his name. He had long suffered from a disease of the ear, and for his last years he lived at Torquay, where he died on 18 January 1873. Against his own wishes he was buried in Westminster Abbey.

## Writing

Bulwer-Lytton wrote in almost every form: society novels, crime novels, historical romances, domestic fiction, occult tales, an early work of science fiction, verse satire, an epic poem and comedies for the stage. The *Dictionary of National Biography* called him one of the authors "upon whose merits the critics have never agreed with the public," for he won immense popularity in the face of hostile reviews. The 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* found his language "artificial and over-decorated" but granted that he built each story "upon a deliberate and careful framework" and was diligent in historical research.

His influence spread in many directions. *Rienzi* became the source of an opera by Richard Wagner, and *The Last Days of Pompeii* and *The Lady of Lyons* were also turned into operas. *The Coming Race*, about a powerful race living beneath the earth, contributed to the growth of science fiction and lent the word "vril" to the meat extract Bovril. His occult fiction was taken up by Helena Blavatsky and other theosophists, and *A Strange Story* influenced Bram Stoker's *Dracula*. He persuaded Charles Dickens to change the ending of *Great Expectations*. The opening of *Paul Clifford* (1830), "It was a dark and stormy night," became so famous as an example of florid writing that a contest for the worst opening sentence of an imaginary novel was named after him.
