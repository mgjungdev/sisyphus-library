Bram Stoker (1847–1912) was an Irish novelist, theatre critic and theatre manager, for many years the business manager of Henry Irving's Lyceum Theatre in London. He is known above all as the author of the Gothic horror novel *Dracula* and the creator of Count Dracula, the most influential figure in vampire fiction.

## Life

Abraham Stoker was born on 8 November 1847 at 15 Marino Crescent, Clontarf, in Dublin, the third of seven children of Abraham Stoker, a senior civil servant, and Charlotte Thornley, who had been raised in County Sligo. The family belonged to the Church of Ireland. He was bedridden with an unknown illness until he started school at the age of seven, and then recovered completely. "The leisure of long illness," he later wrote, "gave opportunity for many thoughts which were fruitful according to their kind in later years." He was educated privately and attended Trinity College Dublin from 1864 to 1870, where he became University Athlete and played rugby for Dublin University. He was auditor of the College Historical Society and president of the University Philosophical Society, to which he gave his first paper, on "Sensationalism in Fiction and Society."

Stoker entered the civil service at Dublin Castle, where he worked for ten years, and wrote a handbook, *The Duties of Clerks of Petty Sessions in Ireland* (1879). In his spare time he became the theatre critic of the *Dublin Evening Mail*, which was co-owned by the Gothic writer Sheridan Le Fanu. His first story, "The Crystal Cup," appeared in 1872. In December 1876 he wrote a favourable review of Henry Irving's Hamlet in Dublin; Irving invited him to dinner, and the two became friends.

In 1878 Stoker married Florence Balcombe, a celebrated beauty who had once been courted by Oscar Wilde, and moved to London to become the acting manager and then the business manager of Irving's Lyceum Theatre, a post he held for twenty-seven years. Their only child, Irving Noel Thornley Stoker, was born on 31 December 1879. Working for the most famous actor of his day brought Stoker into London society, where he met Whistler and Arthur Conan Doyle; the novelist Hall Caine became one of his closest friends. On Irving's tours he travelled widely, especially in the United States, where he was twice invited to the White House and met his literary idol Walt Whitman, to whom he had written in 1872.

Stoker wrote his books in the spare time left by his theatre work, beginning with the novel *The Snake's Pass* (1890). He visited Whitby in 1890 and did research there and at the London Library, and from 1892 to 1910 he spent long holidays at Cruden Bay on the Aberdeenshire coast, where he wrote much of *Dracula*. The novel was published in May 1897. It was well reviewed but earned him little money, and his reputation as a writer was made only after his death. Among his later books were the novels *The Mystery of the Sea* (1902), *The Jewel of Seven Stars* (1903), *The Lady of the Shroud* (1909) and *The Lair of the White Worm* (1911). After Irving's death he published *Personal Reminiscences of Henry Irving*, which sold well.

In politics Stoker was a Liberal and an admirer of Gladstone, whom he knew, and he supported Home Rule for Ireland achieved by peaceful means. After a number of strokes he died at 26 St George's Square, London, on 20 April 1912. His ashes are kept at Golders Green Crematorium. In 1914 his widow, Florence, published a collection of his stories, *Dracula's Guest and Other Weird Stories*.

## Writing

During his lifetime Stoker was better known as Irving's manager than as an author, and he supplemented his income with romances and sensation novels. Hall Caine wrote in his obituary that, apart from his book on Irving, Stoker had written only "to sell." *Dracula* changed that judgment after his death. It is an epistolary novel, assembled from diaries, letters, telegrams, a ship's log and newspaper cuttings, a method that lent the story realism and gave voice to the many people who combine to defeat the Count. Stoker drew on folklore for some features of his vampire, such as the aversion to garlic and the stake, but invented others, including the need to be invited into a house and the absence of a reflection. He took the name Dracula from a history of Wallachia and Moldavia, which explained that it meant "devil."

Contemporary reviewers compared the novel with Wilkie Collins's *The Woman in White* and with the Gothic romances of Ann Radcliffe, and one called Stoker "the Edgar Allan Poe of the nineties." Arthur Conan Doyle wrote to congratulate him "for having written so fine a book." Since the 1970s *Dracula* has received wide academic attention, and Count Dracula has appeared in more than 700 adaptations, shaping the popular image of the vampire ever since.
