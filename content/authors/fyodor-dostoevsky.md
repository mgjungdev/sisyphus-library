Fyodor Dostoevsky (1821–1881) was a Russian novelist, short-story writer, essayist and journalist. His fiction explores the human condition in the troubled political, social and spiritual atmosphere of nineteenth-century Russia, and his best-known novels include *Crime and Punishment*, *The Idiot*, *Demons* and *The Brothers Karamazov*. His novella *Notes from Underground* (1864) is regarded as one of the first works of existentialist literature.

## Life

Fyodor Mikhailovich Dostoevsky was born in Moscow in 1821, the second child of Mikhail Dostoevsky, a doctor at the Mariinsky Hospital for the Poor, and Maria Nechayeva. He grew up in the family's lodgings in the hospital grounds, on the edge of the city. His nanny told him sagas, fairy tales and legends, his mother taught him to read from the Bible, and his parents read him Karamzin, Pushkin, Schiller, Walter Scott and Homer. His mother died of tuberculosis in 1837, and in January 1838 he entered the Nikolayev Military Engineering Institute in Saint Petersburg, a school he disliked. His father died in 1839.

He took a post as a lieutenant engineer in 1843 and published a translation of Balzac's *Eugénie Grandet* that year, but soon left the service for literature. His first novel, *Poor Folk*, was carried by the writer Dmitry Grigorovich to the poet Nikolay Nekrasov, who showed it to the critic Vissarion Belinsky; Belinsky called it Russia's first "social novel." It appeared in January 1846 and was a success, but *The Double* and the stories that followed were coolly received. Dostoevsky joined the Petrashevsky Circle, a group that discussed social reform and banned books, and on 23 April 1849 he was arrested with its other members and held in the Peter and Paul Fortress. In December 1849 the prisoners were taken to Semyonov Place to be shot; at the last moment a message from the tsar commuted the sentence. Dostoevsky later gave the experience to Prince Myshkin in *The Idiot*.

He served four years of hard labour at a prison camp in Omsk, shackled and allowed to read only the New Testament, and then served as a soldier at Semipalatinsk. There he met Maria Isaeva, a widow, whom he married in Kuznetsk; the marriage was unhappy. In 1859 he was allowed to return to European Russia, settling in Saint Petersburg, where *Vremya* ("Time"), a new magazine funded by his brother Mikhail's cigarette factory, published his work. His account of prison life, *The House of the Dead*, was the first published novel about Russian prisons. On trips to Western Europe in 1862 and 1863 he visited London's Crystal Palace, which he saw as a monument to soulless materialism, met Polina Suslova in Paris, and lost nearly all his money gambling at Wiesbaden and Baden-Baden. After the deaths of his wife and of Mikhail he supported his stepson and his brother's family, and the failure of their second magazine, *Epoch*, left him deep in debt.

*Crime and Punishment* began to appear in *The Russian Messenger* in January 1866. That autumn, bound by a contract with the publisher Fyodor Stellovsky, he dictated *The Gambler* in 26 days to a young stenographer, Anna Grigoryevna Snitkina. They married on 15 February 1867 and soon left Russia to escape creditors, living for more than four years in Germany, Switzerland and Italy, where he continued to gamble and wrote *The Idiot* (1869). Their first daughter, Sofya, born in Geneva in 1868, died at three months; a second daughter, Lyubov, was born in Dresden. News of a political murder by a revolutionary group in 1869 started him on *Demons*.

The family returned to Saint Petersburg in July 1871. *Demons* was issued by their own publishing company, whose finances Anna managed. In 1873 Dostoevsky began *A Writer's Diary* in Vladimir Meshchersky's magazine *The Citizen*; issued later as a book, it sold more than twice as many copies as his earlier works. *The Adolescent* followed in 1875. Their son Alexey, born in 1875, later died after a severe epileptic seizure. On 8 June 1880 Dostoevsky spoke at the unveiling of the Pushkin memorial in Moscow; the speech was met with thunderous applause, and even his old rival Turgenev embraced him. *The Brothers Karamazov* was his last novel. He died in Saint Petersburg on 9 February 1881, after a series of pulmonary hemorrhages, and was buried in the Tikhvin Cemetery of the Alexander Nevsky Convent. His funeral was, in the words of the 1911 *Britannica*, "one of the most remarkable demonstrations of public feeling ever witnessed in the Russian capital."

## Writing

Dostoevsky wrote novels, novellas, short stories, essays and journalism, and his subjects include poverty, suicide, crime and guilt, faith and doubt, and the relation between fathers and sons. His early fiction looked at society through the methods of realism and naturalism; after prison, religious themes drawn from Russian Orthodoxy became central to his work. The critic Mikhail Bakhtin described them as "polyphonic," setting many independent voices and points of view against one another without a final authorial word.

Reception was divided from the start. Belinsky praised *Poor Folk* and attacked *The Double*. Nikolay Strakhov recalled that "only *Crime and Punishment* was read in 1866," while the radical critic Grigory Eliseev called the novel a slander on the student body. Turgenev objected to "excessive psychologizing," and later Vladimir Nabokov judged him "not a great writer, but rather a mediocre one." Others ranked him among the greatest of novelists. Friedrich Nietzsche called him "the only psychologist from whom I had something to learn," Sigmund Freud called *The Brothers Karamazov* "the most magnificent novel ever written," and Virginia Woolf wrote that "out of Shakespeare there is no more exciting reading." Constance Garnett's translations, made between 1912 and 1920, made his novels widely read in English. His influence reached Kafka, Camus, Sartre and the existentialists, and his books have been translated into more than 170 languages.

## In this library

- [Notes from Underground](notes-from-underground) — A bitter, isolated retired official in Saint Petersburg, the "Underground Man," attacks rational egoism and the utopian Crystal Palace, then recalls a humiliating dinner with old schoolfellows and his cruelty to the young prostitute Liza. It first appeared in the journal *Epoch* in 1864.
- [The Gambler](the-gambler) — Alexei Ivanovich, tutor to the family of a ruined Russian general at a German spa, is in love with the general's stepdaughter Polina and is drawn into roulette with everyone around him. Dostoevsky dictated it in 1866 to meet a deadline set by his publisher, Fyodor Stellovsky.
- [Crime and Punishment](crime-and-punishment) — Rodion Raskolnikov, an impoverished former student in Saint Petersburg, murders an old pawnbroker to prove a theory about "extraordinary" men and is then consumed by guilt and fear. It was serialised in twelve monthly installments in *The Russian Messenger* during 1866 and published in one volume in 1867.
