George Eliot (1819–1880), the pen name of Mary Ann Evans, was an English novelist, poet, journalist and translator, one of the leading writers of the Victorian era. Her seven novels, among them *Adam Bede*, *The Mill on the Floss*, *Silas Marner* and *Middlemarch*, are known for their realism, psychological insight and close portrayal of provincial English life.

## Life

Mary Ann Evans was born on 22 November 1819 on the Arbury Hall estate near Nuneaton, Warwickshire, the third child of Robert Evans, who managed the estate, and his second wife, Christiana Pearson. Early in 1820 the family moved to Griff House, between Nuneaton and Bedworth. She was a voracious reader, and her father paid for an education unusual for a girl: she boarded at schools in Attleborough, Nuneaton and Coventry, where the evangelical teacher Maria Lewis was an early influence. After she turned sixteen she had little formal schooling, but the library of Arbury Hall allowed her to go on teaching herself, and she learned German and Italian.

Her mother died in 1836, and Mary Ann kept house for her father. When her brother Isaac married and took over the family home, she and her father moved in 1841 to Foleshill, near Coventry. There she became close friends with the ribbon manufacturer Charles Bray and his wife Cara, whose house was a meeting place for freethinkers, and under their influence she gave up her religious faith. Her first major work was a translation of David Strauss's *Life of Jesus* (1846), which caused a stir in England as the original had in Germany. After her father's death in 1849 she travelled to Switzerland with the Brays and stayed on alone in Geneva.

In 1850 she moved to London, calling herself Marian Evans, and lodged with the publisher John Chapman, who had bought the *Westminster Review*. As its assistant editor from 1851 she did most of the work of producing the journal and wrote many of its essays and reviews. In 1854 she published a translation of Ludwig Feuerbach's *The Essence of Christianity*. In 1851 she had met the critic George Henry Lewes, who was married to Agnes Jervis in an open marriage, and in July 1854 they travelled to Germany together. They lived together openly from then until his death, which caused considerable disapproval, and she called him her husband.

Lewes encouraged her to write fiction. "The Sad Fortunes of the Reverend Amos Barton" appeared in *Blackwood's Magazine* in 1857 under the name George Eliot; she later explained that George was Lewes's first name and Eliot "a good mouth-filling, easily pronounced word." *Scenes of Clerical Life* followed as a book in 1858, and *Adam Bede* (1859) was an immediate success, which set off such curiosity about the author that a pretender, Joseph Liggins, claimed it; she then let it be known who she was. *The Mill on the Floss* (1860), *Silas Marner* (1861), *Romola* (1862–1863), *Felix Holt, the Radical* (1866), *Middlemarch* (1871–1872) and *Daniel Deronda* (1876) followed, along with the verse drama *The Spanish Gypsy* (1868). Society gradually accepted the couple, and Queen Victoria herself read her novels.

Lewes died on 30 November 1878. In May 1880 she married John Walter Cross, a friend and financial adviser twenty years younger than herself, and took the name Mary Ann Cross; her brother Isaac, who had broken off contact with her over her life with Lewes, sent congratulations. She died at her new home in Cheyne Walk, Chelsea, on 22 December 1880. Because of her rejection of Christianity and her relationship with Lewes she was not buried in Westminster Abbey but in Highgate Cemetery, beside Lewes. A memorial stone was placed in Poets' Corner in 1980.

## Writing

Before she wrote fiction, Eliot had a long apprenticeship as a translator and critic. In her essay "Silly Novels by Lady Novelists" (1856) she mocked the trivial plots of much contemporary fiction by women, and in other reviews she praised realism, the principle she followed in her own novels. She took a male pen name partly so that her fiction would be judged apart from her reputation as an editor and critic, and partly to keep her private life out of view.

Much of her material came from her own experience of the Midlands. Like Wordsworth, she believed that there was value and beauty in the ordinary details of country life, and her early books, *Adam Bede*, *The Mill on the Floss* and *Silas Marner*, deal with outsiders and the pressures of small communities. The 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* saw *Silas Marner* as "a reversion to her early manner—the manner of *Scenes of Clerical Life*." Her translations of Strauss and Feuerbach left a mark on her fiction, which treats religious feeling with respect while reading it in human terms. Later novels widened her range: *Romola* is set in fifteenth-century Florence, and *Middlemarch*, set in a provincial town on the eve of the Reform Act of 1832, is admired for its psychological depth. Virginia Woolf called it "one of the few English novels written for grown-up people."
