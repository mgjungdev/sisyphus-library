Willa Cather (1873–1947) was an American novelist and short-story writer who grew up on the Nebraska prairie. She is best known for her novels of pioneer life on the Great Plains, among them *O Pioneers!* and *My Ántonia*, and she won the Pulitzer Prize in 1923 for *One of Ours*.

## Life

Wilella Sibert Cather was born on December 7, 1873, in the home of her maternal grandmother in the Back Creek Valley near Winchester, Virginia, the eldest child of Charles Fectigue Cather and Mary Virginia Boak, a former schoolteacher. The family soon moved to Willow Shade, a house near Winchester belonging to her paternal grandparents. In April 1883, when she was nine, they followed the grandparents to Webster County, Nebraska, settling on the high plain between the Little Blue and Republican rivers known as the Divide. After about eighteen months of farming her father moved the family into the town of Red Cloud, where he opened a real estate and insurance business. The girl was fascinated by the many immigrant families of the county and spent hours visiting the older immigrant women and listening to their stories. She also went on house calls with a local doctor and planned to become a physician.

After graduating from high school in Red Cloud in 1890 she went to Lincoln, where she studied first at the university's preparatory school and then at the University of Nebraska. In 1891 an essay of hers on Thomas Carlyle was printed in the *Nebraska State Journal* without her knowledge, and she later said that seeing her words in print had "a kind of hypnotic effect" on her. She gave up science, wrote columns and drama criticism for the *Journal*, edited the student paper *The Hesperian*, and graduated in 1895.

In 1896 she moved to Pittsburgh to edit a family magazine, the *Home Monthly*. From 1897 she worked on the telegraph desk of the *Pittsburgh Leader* and wrote play and book reviews. In 1899 she met Isabelle McClung, who became her closest friend and in whose family home she later lived. After a few months in Washington she returned to Pittsburgh in 1901 to teach Latin and English at Central High School, and later taught at Allegheny High School. Her first book, *April Twilights*, a collection of verse, appeared in 1903. Her stories caught the attention of the editor S. S. McClure, who printed "Paul's Case" and "The Sculptor's Funeral" in *McClure's Magazine* and arranged for the publication of her collection *The Troll Garden* in 1905.

In 1906 she moved to New York and joined the staff of *McClure's*, becoming its managing editor in 1908. She spent much of 1907 in Boston working on a series about Mary Baker Eddy that was published under the name of Georgine Milmine. In 1908 she met the writer Sarah Orne Jewett, who urged her to leave the magazine and write about the country and people she knew. Cather took leave in 1911, and her first novel, *Alexander's Bridge*, appeared in 1912. *O Pioneers!* followed in 1913, *The Song of the Lark*, inspired by the Swedish-born soprano Olive Fremstad, in 1915, and *My Ántonia*, built around her childhood friend Annie Pavelka, in 1918. In New York she lived with the editor Edith Lewis, her companion for the rest of her life, at 5 Bank Street in Greenwich Village and later on Park Avenue.

Dissatisfied with Houghton Mifflin, she moved to the young publisher Alfred A. Knopf. *One of Ours* (1922), based on the life of her cousin G. P. Cather, killed in France in 1918, won the Pulitzer Prize. *A Lost Lady* (1923), *The Professor's House* (1925), *My Mortal Enemy* (1926) and *Death Comes for the Archbishop* (1927), set in nineteenth-century New Mexico, followed. A visit to Quebec led to *Shadows on the Rock* (1931), which won the Prix Femina Américain. She spent many summers in a cottage on Grand Manan Island in New Brunswick. *Obscure Destinies* (1932), *Lucy Gayheart* (1935) and *Sapphira and the Slave Girl* (1940), set in the Virginia of her early childhood, followed; *Sapphira* was her last completed novel. In 1938 her brother Douglass and Isabelle McClung both died. She received the gold medal of the National Institute of Arts and Letters in 1944. Cather died of a cerebral hemorrhage at her home at 570 Park Avenue, Manhattan, on April 24, 1947, and was buried at Jaffrey, New Hampshire. At her instruction Lewis destroyed the manuscript of her unfinished novel set in Avignon, *Hard Punishments*.

## Writing

Cather's fiction rests on a sense of place. In her prairie novels the land itself gives the book its shape, and her characters, many of them European immigrants, struggle against it and find community through it. Nostalgia and exile run through her work, and she drew on the memories of her Nebraska childhood for seven of her books. She admired Henry James, and Jewett's advice to write about her "own country" turned her from *Alexander's Bridge*, which she later dismissed as imitative of Edith Wharton and James, to the material of *O Pioneers!*, which she dedicated to Jewett's memory. In *A Lost Lady* she told her story by suggestion rather than by minute detail, and the critic Susan J. Rosowski wrote that she was perhaps the first to grant immigrants a respectable position in American literature.

Reviewers greeted *O Pioneers!* as a new voice in American letters, and *My Ántonia* was praised on its appearance in 1918. H. L. Mencken wrote, "No romantic novel ever written in America, by man or woman, is one half so beautiful as *My Ántonia*," and Randolph Bourne in *The Dial* placed her within the modern literary movement. Sinclair Lewis praised her for making Nebraska known to the wider world. *One of Ours* was attacked for its picture of the war; Ernest Hemingway wrote in a 1923 letter, "Poor woman, she had to get her war experience somewhere." *Death Comes for the Archbishop* was hailed as "an American classic." In the 1930s left-leaning critics such as Granville Hicks and Edmund Wilson judged her romantic and nostalgic, out of touch with the problems of the present, though her books continued to sell widely.

## In this library

- [Paul's Case](pauls-case) — Paul, a Pittsburgh schoolboy who loves the theatre and concert hall and despises his drab home on Cordelia Street, steals money from his employer and spends a few days of luxury in New York. It was printed in *McClure's Magazine* in May 1905 and collected in *The Troll Garden* the same year.
- [O Pioneers!](o-pioneers) — Alexandra Bergson, daughter of Swedish immigrants, inherits the family farm near the Nebraska town of Hanover and makes it prosper while her neighbors give up, and her younger brother Emil falls in love with the married Marie Shabata. It was published by Houghton Mifflin in June 1913, and its title comes from Walt Whitman's poem "Pioneers! O Pioneers!"
- [My Ántonia](my-antonia) — Jim Burden, an orphan sent from Virginia to his grandparents in Nebraska, looks back on his friendship with Ántonia Shimerda, the daughter of a Bohemian immigrant family, from childhood on the prairie to her life as a farm wife and mother. It was published by Houghton Mifflin in 1918.
