Washington Irving (1783–1859) was an American short-story writer, essayist, biographer, historian and diplomat, one of the first American writers to win acclaim in Europe. He is best known for "Rip Van Winkle" and "The Legend of Sleepy Hollow," both from his collection *The Sketch Book of Geoffrey Crayon, Gent.*

## Life

Washington Irving was born in New York on 3 April 1783, the youngest of eight surviving children of William Irving, a merchant from the Orkney Islands, and Sarah Saunders of Falmouth in Cornwall. He was born in the same week that New Yorkers learned of the treaty ending the Revolutionary War, and his mother named him after George Washington. He was an indifferent student who preferred adventure stories and the theater. During a yellow fever outbreak in 1798 he was sent up the Hudson to Tarrytown, where he came to know the Dutch customs and ghost stories of the valley, and on later trips he passed through the Catskill Mountains.

In 1802 he began writing letters on the city's social and theatrical life for the *Morning Chronicle*, a newspaper edited by his brother Peter, under the pseudonym Jonathan Oldstyle. His brothers, who were merchants and often supported him, paid for a long tour of Europe from 1804 to 1806 for the sake of his health. Back in New York he studied law and was admitted to the bar, but showed little interest in practicing. With his brother William and his friend James Kirke Paulding he wrote *Salmagundi*, a satirical magazine begun in 1807 in which he gave New York the nickname "Gotham." In 1809 came *A History of New York*, a burlesque history credited to an imaginary Dutch scholar, Diedrich Knickerbocker, and launched with a hoax of newspaper notices about the missing historian. Its success made his name. He wrote it while grieving the death of his young fiancée, Matilda Hoffman, and he never married.

In 1815 Irving went to England to help save the family trading firm, and he stayed in Europe for seventeen years. The firm went bankrupt, and he turned to writing for a living. In 1817 he visited Walter Scott, who became a lifelong friend, and while staying with his sister in Birmingham he wrote "Rip Van Winkle" in a single night. *The Sketch Book*, published under the name Geoffrey Crayon, appeared in installments in 1819 and 1820 and was a great success on both sides of the Atlantic; through Scott he found a London publisher in John Murray. *Bracebridge Hall* (1822) was well received, but *Tales of a Traveller* (1824) was panned by critics.

In 1826 Irving went to Madrid to work in the Spanish archives, and his *History of the Life and Voyages of Christopher Columbus* (1828) was widely read in America and Europe, although it helped spread the myth that medieval Europeans believed the earth was flat. He lived for a time in the Alhambra palace in Granada, which inspired his *Tales of the Alhambra* (1832). From 1829 he served as secretary to the American legation in London.

He returned to New York in 1832 and travelled into the Indian Territory, which he described in *A Tour on the Prairies*. He bought a cottage on the Hudson near Tarrytown, which he named Sunnyside and spent years enlarging. He encouraged younger writers, among them Edgar Allan Poe, and in 1842 he welcomed Charles Dickens to Sunnyside. That year President John Tyler appointed him Minister to Spain, a post he held for four years in a time of political upheaval. Back at Sunnyside he prepared a revised edition of his works for the publisher George Palmer Putnam and wrote lives of Oliver Goldsmith and Muhammad. His five-volume life of George Washington appeared between 1855 and 1859. He died of a heart attack at Sunnyside on 28 November 1859, at the age of 76, and was buried in what is now Sleepy Hollow Cemetery.

## Writing

Irving is often called the first American man of letters and the first American to earn his living by his pen alone. He is credited with perfecting the American short story and with setting his tales firmly in America, even when he borrowed their plots from German or Dutch folklore, and with writing to entertain rather than to teach a moral. His prose is light, humorous and graceful; the 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* called his style "pellucid" and its execution "almost faultless." William Makepeace Thackeray named him the "ambassador whom the New World of Letters sent to the Old," and a critic writing in 1881 called him "the first of the American humorists, as he is almost the first of the American writers." Henry Wadsworth Longfellow praised him in 1859 as "the first to win for our country an honourable name and position in the History of Letters," and the editor George William Curtis remembered how generously he encouraged young writers.

Not every critic agreed. Some charged that he wrote for English rather than American tastes, Poe wrote in 1838 that "Irving is much over-rated," and the later critic Barrett Wendell complained, "The man had no message." Yet Irving left a lasting mark on American culture. He popularised "Gotham" as a name for New York, made "Knickerbocker" a byword for New Yorkers, and through the Christmas sketches in *The Sketch Book* and a flying St. Nicholas in *A History of New York* helped shape the way Americans celebrate Christmas.

## In this library

- [Rip Van Winkle](rip-van-winkle) — An idle, henpecked villager in the Catskill Mountains drinks with a strange company of old-fashioned Dutchmen playing ninepins and falls asleep for twenty years, waking to find that the American Revolution has come and gone. It appeared in 1819 in the first installment of *The Sketch Book of Geoffrey Crayon, Gent.*, published in New York.
- [The Legend of Sleepy Hollow](sleepy-hollow) — Ichabod Crane, a lanky and superstitious schoolmaster from Connecticut, courts the heiress Katrina Van Tassel against the rivalry of Brom Bones, and vanishes after a midnight chase by the Headless Horseman. The longest story in *The Sketch Book*, it appeared in 1820 in the sixth number of the New York edition and the second volume of the London edition.
