Anton Chekhov (1860–1904) was a Russian short-story writer, playwright and physician. He began with comic sketches written for money while he studied medicine and went on to write stories whose quiet, open endings shaped the modern short story. His four late plays, *The Seagull*, *Uncle Vanya*, *Three Sisters* and *The Cherry Orchard*, made him one of the founding figures of modern theatre.

## Life

Anton Pavlovich Chekhov was born on 29 January 1860 (17 January Old Style) in Taganrog, a port on the Sea of Azov in southern Russia, the third of six surviving children. His father, Pavel Yegorovich Chekhov, the son of a former serf, kept a grocery shop, directed the parish choir and beat his children; his mother, Yevgeniya, was a gifted storyteller. "Our talents we got from our father," Chekhov later said, "but our soul from our mother." He attended the Greek School and the gymnasium in Taganrog and sang in his father's choirs, which he remembered as a kind of penal servitude.

In 1876 his father went bankrupt and fled to Moscow to escape debtors' prison, taking the family with him. Anton stayed behind for three years to sell the family's possessions and finish school, supporting himself by tutoring and sending what money he could to Moscow. As a schoolboy he became devoted to the Taganrog theatre. In 1879 he joined his family and entered medical school in Moscow.

From then on he supported the whole household. To pay for it he wrote short humorous sketches of Russian life for weekly papers, many of them signed with pseudonyms such as "Antosha Chekhonte," and by 1882 he was writing for Nikolai Leykin's comic magazine *Oskolki* (*Fragments*). He qualified as a physician in 1884 and always regarded medicine as his main profession, treating the poor without charge; "Medicine is my lawful wife," he once said, "and literature is my mistress." In the same years he began coughing blood, the first signs of the tuberculosis he long refused to admit. Early in 1886 Alexey Suvorin, owner of the St. Petersburg paper *Novoye Vremya* (*New Times*), invited him to write for it at double Leykin's rate, and became perhaps his closest friend. That year the novelist Dmitry Grigorovich wrote to tell him that he had "real talent" and urged him to write less and more carefully.

Chekhov took the advice. "The Steppe," a long story of a boy's journey across the southern plains, appeared in the literary journal *Severny Vestnik*, and in 1888 his collection *At Dusk* won the Pushkin Prize. His first full-length play, *Ivanov*, was written in a fortnight and staged in November 1887. The death of his brother Nikolai from tuberculosis in 1889 left him restless, and in 1890 he travelled across Siberia by train, carriage and river steamer to the penal colony on Sakhalin Island, where he spent three months interviewing convicts and settlers for a census. His report, *The Island of Sakhalin*, was published in 1893 and 1894.

In the early 1890s he settled on a small estate at Melikhovo, where he farmed, planted trees and treated peasants who came to him from miles around, experience he drew on in stories such as "Peasants." There he began *The Seagull*. Its first performance, at the Alexandrinsky Theatre in St. Petersburg on 17 October 1896, was booed, and Chekhov swore off the theatre. Two years later Konstantin Stanislavski revived it at the new Moscow Art Theatre with great success, and the company went on to stage *Uncle Vanya*.

In March 1897 a severe lung haemorrhage forced him to change his way of life. After his father's death in 1898 he built a villa outside Yalta in the Crimea, where he lived with his mother and sister, received Leo Tolstoy and Maxim Gorky, and wrote "The Lady with the Dog." On 25 May 1901 he married Olga Knipper, an actress of the Art Theatre; she continued to act in Moscow while he lived mostly in Yalta, and their letters form a long record of his last years. He spent a year each on *Three Sisters* and *The Cherry Orchard*. In June 1904, gravely ill, he travelled with Olga to the German spa town of Badenweiler, where he died on 15 July 1904, aged 44. His body was taken back to Moscow, and he was buried beside his father in the Novodevichy Cemetery.

## Writing

Chekhov's early work was fast comic journalism, harsher in tone than his later fiction. From the late 1880s he wrote stories that drift with the thoughts of their characters, avoid conventional plots and leave their problems unresolved. He held that a writer's duty is not to solve problems but to state them correctly. His plays replace dramatic action with mood and with what lies beneath the dialogue; Stanislavski wrote that Chekhov "often expressed his thought not in speeches, but in pauses or between the lines." The principle known as "Chekhov's gun" comes from his advice that a rifle hanging on the wall in the first chapter "absolutely must go off" later.

Tolstoy admired his stories and sorted them into first and second quality; "Vanka" was in his first group. Radical thinkers read him as a witness to Russian poverty: Peter Kropotkin told doubters to "read only Chekhov's novels!" and Lenin is said to have felt, after reading "Ward No. 6," that he was "shut up in Ward 6" himself. British critics in Chekhov's lifetime were often hostile, but after his death Constance Garnett's translations won him English readers and the admiration of James Joyce, Virginia Woolf and Katherine Mansfield. Woolf, noting that his stories seem "inconclusive," wrote of endings that are "a note of interrogation or merely the information that they went on talking." Vladimir Nabokov called "The Lady with the Dog" "one of the greatest stories ever written," and Raymond Carver praised "the awesome frequency with which he produced masterpieces." Ernest Hemingway was more grudging: "Chekhov wrote about six good stories." George Bernard Shaw subtitled *Heartbreak House* "A Fantasia in the Russian Manner on English Themes."

## In this library

- [Vanka](vanka) — On Christmas Eve, nine-year-old Vanka, apprenticed to a Moscow shoemaker, secretly writes to his grandfather in the village begging to be taken home, and addresses the letter simply "To the village, to my grandfather." First published in the *Peterburgskaya Gazeta* on 25 December 1886, signed A. Chekhonte.
- [The Lottery Ticket](lottery-ticket) — Ivan Dmitritch, a contented man of modest income, finds that the series of his wife's lottery ticket has won, and as husband and wife dream of the seventy-five thousand roubles they begin to hate each other. First published in March 1887.
- [The Bet](the-bet) — A banker recalls the wager he made fifteen years earlier with a young lawyer, who agreed to spend fifteen years in solitary confinement for two million roubles. First published in *Novoye Vremya* on 1 January 1889 under the title "Fairytale," and revised and shortened for Chekhov's collected works.
- [The Lady with the Dog](lady-with-the-dog) — Dmitri Gurov, a married Moscow banker on holiday in Yalta, begins an affair with Anna Sergeyevna, a young married woman walking a Pomeranian, and finds back in Moscow that he cannot forget her. First published in the December 1899 issue of *Russkaya Mysl* (*Russian Thought*).
