Theodore Dreiser (1871–1945) was an American novelist and journalist of the naturalist school. His novels, above all *Sister Carrie* and *An American Tragedy*, follow characters driven by ambition and desire through the cities and businesses of a changing America, and present them without moral judgment.

## Life

Theodore Herman Albert Dreiser was born on 27 August 1871 in Terre Haute, Indiana, the twelfth of thirteen children of John Paul Dreiser, a German immigrant from Mayen in the Rhine Province of Prussia, and Sarah Maria Schanab, who came from a Mennonite farming community near Dayton, Ohio, and had been disowned by her family for becoming a Roman Catholic in order to marry. The children were raised as Catholics, and the family lived in severe poverty. One of his older brothers, Paul, changed the spelling of his name and became the popular songwriter Paul Dresser. Dreiser finished high school in Warsaw, Indiana, and attended Indiana University in Bloomington in 1889–1890 without taking a degree.

In 1892 he began work as a reporter and drama critic, and over the next years he wrote for newspapers in Chicago, St. Louis, Toledo, Pittsburgh and New York. His first fiction, "The Return of Genius," appeared in the *Chicago Daily Globe* under the name Carl Dreiser. By 1895 he was writing for magazines, interviewing public figures such as Andrew Carnegie, Marshall Field and Thomas Edison. In the same year business associates of his brother gave him the editorship of the magazine *Ev'ry Month*, where he published his first story, "Forgotten." While working in St. Louis he had met the schoolteacher Sara Osborne White; they became engaged in 1893 and married on 28 December 1898.

In 1899 the Dreisers stayed with the journalist Arthur Henry and his wife at the House of Four Pillars in Maumee, Ohio, and there, at Henry's urging, Dreiser began his first novel, *Sister Carrie*. Doubleday & McClure published it in 1900, but it sold poorly and was considered controversial. Dreiser returned to editing: he edited *Smith's Magazine* and the *Broadway Magazine*, and from 1907 he was editor-in-chief of the Butterick publications, among them the women's magazine *The Delineator*, a post that brought him financial independence. He and Sara separated in 1909, though they never divorced.

His second novel, *Jennie Gerhardt*, appeared in 1911. *The Financier* (1912) and *The Titan* (1914), based on the life of the Chicago streetcar magnate Charles Tyson Yerkes, began a trilogy whose last volume, *The Stoic*, was published only after his death. *The "Genius"* followed in 1915, and he also wrote plays, travel books and the autobiographical *A Book About Myself* (1922). Returning from his first European vacation, he had planned to sail on the *Titanic* but was persuaded to take a cheaper ship. His frank treatment of sexual matters repeatedly brought him into conflict with censors. In 1919 he met his cousin Helen Patges Richardson, who remained his companion for the rest of his life.

His first commercial success was *An American Tragedy* (1925), based on the 1906 murder of Grace Brown by Chester Gillette in upstate New York. A visit to the Soviet Union in 1927 produced *Dreiser Looks at Russia* (1928). In 1930 he was nominated for the Nobel Prize in Literature, which went instead to Sinclair Lewis. He campaigned for radicals he believed to be victims of injustice, and in November 1931 he led the National Committee for the Defense of Political Prisoners to the coalfields of Harlan County, Kentucky, to take testimony from miners about violence against them and their unions. He wrote *Tragic America* (1931) and *America Is Worth Saving* (1941) as critiques of capitalist America.

His first wife died in 1942, and on 13 June 1944 he married Helen Richardson. In August 1945 he joined the Communist Party USA. Dreiser died in Hollywood, California, on 28 December 1945. *The Bulwark* was published in 1946 and *The Stoic* in 1947.

## Writing

Dreiser is counted among the major American naturalists. His characters often reach their goals without any firm moral code, and their lives are shaped less by choice than by forces of environment, money and desire; his great theme was the tension among ambition, desire and social convention. *Sister Carrie* broke with the moralizing fiction of the Victorian era: its heroine has illicit affairs and is not punished for them, and Dreiser presented her without judging her.

His prose was often attacked. Arnold Bennett said in 1930 that "Dreiser simply does not know how to write, never did know, never wanted to know," and F. R. Leavis wrote that he "seems as though he learned English from a newspaper." The critic Michael Lydon answered that his patience and powers of observation gave an accurate picture of the city and of the ambitions of its people. H. L. Mencken, his strongest champion, wrote that American writing "before and after his time, differed almost as much as biology before and after Darwin." In his Nobel lecture of 1930 Sinclair Lewis said that *Sister Carrie* "came to housebound and airless America like a great free Western wind," and Sherwood Anderson wrote that because of Dreiser later writers would never have to face "the road through the wilderness of Puritan denial."
