W. Somerset Maugham (1874–1965) was an English playwright, novelist and short-story writer, one of the most popular and widely read authors of his time. He is known for the novels *Of Human Bondage*, *The Moon and Sixpence* and *Cakes and Ale*, for his stage comedies, and for short stories set in the colonial outposts of Asia and the South Seas.

## Life

William Somerset Maugham was born on 25 January 1874 in the British Embassy in Paris, where the ambassador had set up a maternity ward so that British children born in France would not be claimed as French citizens. He was the fourth son of Robert Ormond Maugham, a prosperous solicitor who handled the embassy's legal affairs, and Edith Mary Snell. His mother died of tuberculosis in January 1882, and his father about two and a half years later. The ten-year-old boy, whose first language was French, was sent to England to live with his uncle Henry MacDonald Maugham, vicar of Whitstable in Kent. He grew shy and developed a stammer that stayed with him all his life.

From 1885 to 1890 he attended The King's School, Canterbury, where he was teased as an outsider. A small legacy from his father let him go at sixteen to Heidelberg, where for a year and a half he studied literature, philosophy and German. Back in England in 1892, after a month in an accountant's office, he began to study medicine at St Thomas's Hospital in Lambeth. His work among the poor of south London gave him the material for his first novel, *Liza of Lambeth* (1897), a story of working-class adultery whose first printing sold out within three weeks. He qualified as a physician the month after it appeared but never practised, and turned to writing for good.

Success came first in the theatre. His comedy *Lady Frederick* opened at the Court Theatre in London in October 1907 and ran for 422 performances, and by the next year he had four plays running at once in the West End. When the First World War began he was too old to enlist and served in France as a volunteer ambulance driver for the British Red Cross. There he met Gerald Haxton, a young San Franciscan who became his lover and companion for the next thirty years. *Of Human Bondage*, the partly autobiographical novel he had finished before the war, appeared in 1915. In September of that year Syrie Wellcome, with whom he had been having an affair, gave birth to their daughter, Liza; they married in New Jersey in May 1917.

After his daughter's birth Maugham worked for a year in Geneva as an agent of the British Secret Service, experiences he later used in the Ashenden stories. In November 1916 he was sent to the South Seas, and in 1917 he went to Petrograd to encourage the Russian government of Alexander Kerensky to stay in the war, arriving too late to affect the outcome. Ill with tuberculosis, he spent three months in a sanatorium in Scotland. *The Moon and Sixpence* appeared in 1919.

Barred from Britain, Haxton travelled with him through the Far East, the Pacific, Malaya and south-east Asia in the early 1920s, and Maugham gathered material for his stories wherever they went. *The Painted Veil* (1925), the story collections *The Trembling of a Leaf* (1921) and *The Casuarina Tree* (1926), and plays such as *The Circle* (1921) and *The Constant Wife* (1926) followed. He and Syrie agreed to live apart, she in London and he at the Villa La Mauresque on Cap Ferrat in the south of France, and they divorced in 1929. *Cakes and Ale*, a satire on the literary world, appeared in 1930. In 1933, tired of the theatre, he wrote his thirty-second and last play, *Sheppey*.

When France fell in June 1940 Maugham escaped to England on a coal freighter from Nice, and he spent most of the war in the United States, where he wrote *The Razor's Edge* (1944). Haxton died in November 1944, and Alan Searle became his secretary and companion. After the war Maugham endowed the Somerset Maugham Award for young British writers, introduced film adaptations of his stories and an American television series, and in 1954 was made a Companion of Honour. His last years were marked by senility and by quarrels with his family. He died in the Anglo-American Hospital in Nice on the night of 15-16 December 1965, and his ashes were buried at The King's School, Canterbury.

## Writing

Between 1902 and 1933 Maugham had thirty-two plays staged, and between 1897 and 1962 he published nineteen novels and nine volumes of short stories, as well as travel books, memoirs and essays. His American publishers estimated that four and a half million copies of his books were bought in the United States during his lifetime. He named Guy de Maupassant as an early influence, and his mature style was plain and lucid, built on short, direct sentences; critics such as H. E. Bates objected to his frequent clichés. He admitted that he was not an inventive writer: he studied the people and places he saw and used them, sometimes with little disguise, in his fiction.

His popularity provoked highbrow critics, many of whom dismissed him as merely competent, but later opinion generally ranks *Of Human Bondage* as a masterpiece, and many readers find his best work in the short stories. Maugham himself agreed that *Of Human Bondage* was his best book, but said the one he liked best was *Cakes and Ale*. Unlike Shaw, he did not see drama as a means of moral instruction, and he wrote his plays to entertain. In *The Spectator* the critic J. D. Scott wrote of "The Maugham Effect": "This quality is one of force, of swiftness, of the dramatic leap." *The Moon and Sixpence* shows his habit of building fiction from life: its painter hero is drawn in part from Paul Gauguin, and its narrator pieces together the story of a man who is already dead when the novel opens.
